## cn

Q: A bakery imports 40 reais of flour and sells 100 reais of bread to households in the same year. What is its contribution to domestic GDP, and does the expenditure approach agree?
- It adds 60 reais, the value added; expenditure records 100 reais of consumption minus 40 reais of imports, giving the same figure.
- It adds 100 reais, the value of the final good; the flour was already taxed at the border, so deducting it again would understate output.
- It adds 60 reais, the value added; expenditure records the flour as inventory investment, written off only in the year the bread is sold.
- It adds 100 reais, the value of the final good; the flour counts as value added in the exporting country and needs no deduction here.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> Value added is 100 − 40 = 60 reais. The expenditure identity $Y = C + I + G + (X-M)$ reaches the same number: consumption of 100 minus imports of 40. The import subtraction is not a penalty on trade — it removes from domestic output what was produced abroad. Option C confuses the treatment of unsold inventories with that of an input consumed within the year.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 1, §1.1 (p. 15); regras [[01_mensuracao_agregados]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 1, Exercício 1.1 (p. 27); Lista 1, Q1

Q: A government pays unemployment insurance to households and buys new office computers in the same year. How does each item enter the national accounts?
- Both belong in G; they differ only in how they are financed, and neither adds to the measured capital stock.
- The benefits belong in G because they are tax-financed; the computers are intermediate consumption and stay out of GDP.
- The benefits are a transfer and stay out of G; the computers are government investment and enter GDP as final demand.
- The benefits are the transfer component of G; the computers are a purchase of an existing asset and stay out of GDP.
<!-- YW5zOjI= -->
> Transfers move purchasing power without buying output, so they are excluded from G — counting them would double-count the consumption they finance. Durable goods bought by the government are gross fixed capital formation: they are final demand and they add to the capital stock. The frequent error is treating "money the government spends" as G.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 1, §1.1 (p. 15); regras [[01_mensuracao_agregados]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 2, Exercício 2.4 (p. 41)

Q: In a country where foreigners own a large share of the domestic capital stock and where capital depreciates quickly, which ranking follows from the definitions alone?
- GNP exceeds GDP, and net domestic product exceeds both, since depreciation is a cost borne abroad.
- GDP exceeds both GNP and net domestic product, while the ranking between those two is left open.
- Net domestic product exceeds GNP, because depreciation is smaller than the income paid to foreign owners.
- GDP equals GNP by construction, and net domestic product is the only aggregate that falls below the others.
<!-- YW5zOjE= -->
> GNP = GDP + net factor income from abroad, which is negative here, and NDP = GDP − depreciation, which is also below GDP. Both subtract something different from the same base, so GDP is the largest, but nothing in the definitions says whether foreign factor payments exceed depreciation. Answering that would require the two magnitudes, which the question deliberately does not give.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 1, §1.1 (p. 15)
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 5, Exercício 5.7 (p. 96)

## idx

Q: Real GDP growth computed at initial-year prices usually exceeds the same growth computed at final-year prices. What generates the gap?
- Initial-year prices are lower on average, so any given bundle is valued at a smaller total and the measured percentage change is larger.
- Final-year prices already embed the inflation of the period, so using them deflates the series a second time and shrinks measured growth.
- Indices built on final-year prices drop imported goods from the basket, and it is imports whose quantities grow fastest over the period.
- Quantities grow fastest for goods whose relative prices are falling, and initial-year prices give exactly those goods the larger weight.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> This is the Laspeyres versus Paasche gap. Growth is a quantity index weighted by prices; the goods that expand most are typically the ones getting relatively cheaper, so the old price vector overweights them and the new one underweights them. Nothing about the price level as such is involved — only the relative weights.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 1, §1.2 (p. 22); regras [[01_mensuracao_agregados]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 1, Exercício 1.3 *Chained GDP* (p. 28); Lista 1, Q1(c)

Q: The price of imported oil doubles while domestic output and domestic prices are unchanged. What happens to the consumer price index and to the GDP deflator?
- The consumer index rises, since imported fuel sits in the fixed basket; the deflator barely moves, since it weighs domestic output.
- Both rise in the same proportion, since every price index is anchored to the same underlying basket of market transactions.
- The deflator rises and the consumer index does not, since current weights make the deflator react faster to any price change.
- Neither index moves, since both are built to exclude goods whose prices are determined outside the domestic economy.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> The CPI prices a fixed consumption basket that includes imports; the deflator is the ratio of nominal to real GDP and therefore covers only what the country produces. In the expenditure formulation imports enter with a negative sign, so a pure import price shock can even push the deflator down — what it cannot do is push it up. This is the cleanest case separating the two indices.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 1, §1.2 (p. 22); regras [[01_mensuracao_agregados]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 1, Exercício 1.5 (p. 29)

Q: Converting a poor country's GDP at market exchange rates instead of purchasing power parity has which effect?
- It overstates its real income, because market rates are held down by capital controls that make the local currency artificially cheap.
- It understates its real income, because non-traded goods are systematically cheaper there, and it inflates measured cross-country dispersion.
- It leaves real income unchanged on average, because deviations from parity wash out once several years of exchange rates are averaged.
- It understates its real income, because market rates ignore the tariffs that raise the domestic price of every traded good.
<!-- YW5zOjE= -->
> Market rates are set mainly by traded goods, while haircuts, housing and local services are far cheaper in poor countries — the Balassa-Samuelson effect. Converting at market rates therefore values a poor country's consumption at rich-country relative prices for goods it never trades, understating real income and exaggerating the ratio between richest and poorest.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 1, §1.2 (p. 22); regras [[01_mensuracao_agregados]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 1, Exercício 1.2 (p. 27); Lista 1, Q1(d)-(e)

Q: What does chain weighting fix in the measurement of real GDP, and what does it give up?
- It fixes the understatement of quality gains by revaluing goods each year, and gives up comparability with the consumer price index.
- It fixes the exclusion of imported goods by adding them at border prices, and gives up the identity between output and expenditure.
- It fixes the drift of the capital share by reweighting factor payments yearly, and gives up a constant base for factor prices.
- It fixes the dependence of measured growth on an arbitrary base year by using adjacent-year prices, and gives up additivity of components.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> Chaining averages the Laspeyres and Paasche perspectives period by period, so the answer no longer hinges on which year was declared the base. The price is that chained components no longer sum to the chained total, which is why published contributions to growth need a separate residual line.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 1, §1.2 (p. 22)
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 1, Exercício 1.3 *Chained GDP* (p. 28)

## bem

Q: The Human Development Index aggregates income, schooling and life expectancy with a geometric mean rather than an arithmetic one. What does that choice buy?
- It makes the index invariant to the units of each component, so no normalisation of the subindices is required beforehand.
- It gives income a smaller weight than the other two dimensions, correcting the tendency of rich countries to dominate the ranking.
- It penalises imbalance across dimensions: a country very weak in one of them cannot fully offset it with strength in another.
- It guarantees that the index rises whenever any one of the three components rises, which an arithmetic mean fails to deliver.
<!-- YW5zOjI= -->
> A geometric mean goes to zero if any component does, and in general it rewards balance: the same total spread across dimensions scores higher than a lopsided profile. The switch away from the arithmetic mean was made precisely so that a country could not buy its way up the ranking on income alone. Normalisation of the subindices is still performed separately.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 2, §2.1-2.2 (pp. 31-33); regras [[01_mensuracao_agregados]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 2, Exercício 2.2 (p. 41); Lista 1, Q2

Q: Two countries have Lorenz curves that cross. What can still be said about ranking their inequality?
- The Gini still gives a complete ranking, but it no longer follows from Lorenz dominance and depends on the weights the Gini uses.
- Neither the Gini nor any other scalar index can be computed, since crossing curves violate the conditions for it to be defined.
- Lorenz dominance still holds over the range where the curves do not cross, which is enough to rank the two distributions.
- The country whose curve starts lower is unambiguously more unequal, because the bottom of the distribution carries more weight.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> The Gini is a number and is always computable, so it always ranks. What crossing curves destroy is the unanimity: with dominance, every inequality-averse observer agrees; without it, the ranking reflects the particular weighting the Gini imposes, and another index can reverse it. Confusing "a ranking exists" with "the ranking is uncontroversial" is the trap.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 2, §2.1-2.2 (pp. 31-33)
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 2, Exercício 2.3 (p. 41)

Q: A reform moves work that households previously did for themselves into the paid market, with the amount of the activity unchanged. What follows?
- Measured GDP is unchanged, since the accounts already impute the value of unpaid household work at market wages.
- Measured GDP falls, since households now pay for a service they used to obtain at no cost, reducing their disposable income.
- Measured GDP rises and welfare rises by the same amount, since market provision is by construction the more efficient arrangement.
- Measured GDP rises even though the activity is the same, so the recorded gain overstates the improvement in welfare.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> Only market transactions are recorded, so formalising an existing activity creates measured output out of nothing real. There may be a genuine gain from specialisation, but the measured jump is an upper bound on it, not an estimate. The same logic explains why GDP falls when someone marries their housekeeper.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 2, §2.1-2.2 (pp. 31-33); regras [[01_mensuracao_agregados]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 2, Exercícios 2.7-2.10 (pp. 42-43)

## fatos

Q: Which Kaldor fact most directly disciplines the use of a Cobb-Douglas production function with a constant exponent on capital?
- The roughly constant growth rate of output per worker over long horizons in the developed economies.
- The roughly constant shares of capital and labour in national income over long horizons.
- The wide dispersion of growth rates across countries over any given several-decade window.
- The roughly constant capital-output ratio observed alongside a rising capital stock per worker.
<!-- YW5zOjE= -->
> With competitive factor markets, the capital share equals the elasticity of output with respect to capital. Constant shares while the capital-labour ratio changes a great deal is exactly the statement that this elasticity does not move — which is what Cobb-Douglas, and only unit elasticity of substitution, delivers. The other facts constrain the model but do not pin the functional form.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 3, §3.1-3.3 (pp. 47-51); regras [[02_crescimento_solow]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 3, Exercício 3.1 (p. 51)

Q: Cross-country data show no relation between initial income and later growth in the world sample, but a negative one once saving, population growth and schooling are held fixed. How does this sit with Solow?
- It refutes Solow, which predicts that poor countries grow faster regardless of their parameters, since capital is more productive there.
- It refutes Solow only for the poorest countries, whose capital stocks are so far below the steady state that the Inada conditions fail.
- It fits Solow, which predicts convergence of each country to its own steady state; absolute convergence would need a common one.
- It fits Solow only if the production function is AK, since with diminishing returns convergence would show up in the raw data too.
<!-- YW5zOjI= -->
> Solow says the growth rate depends on the distance from *that country's* steady state, and the steady state itself depends on $s$, $n$ and $\delta$. Countries with different parameters have different targets, so nothing forces the poor ones to grow faster. Absolute convergence is a much stronger claim, and the data reject it while accepting the conditional version.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 3, §3.3 (p. 51); regras [[02_crescimento_solow]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 3, Exercício 3.3 (p. 52); Kurlat, cap. 5, Exercício 5.2 (p. 94); Lista 1, Q3

Q: In a Malthusian economy, a permanent improvement in production technology has which long-run consequence?
- Income per person rises permanently and population is unaffected, since fertility responds to institutions and not to income.
- Income per person and population both rise permanently in the same proportion, leaving the wage-to-rent ratio unchanged.
- Income per person falls below its previous level, since a larger population presses on a fixed supply of land more than before.
- Income per person rises only for a while; population grows until it returns to subsistence, and the lasting effect is more people.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> The Malthusian mechanism is that higher income raises net fertility, and population growth erodes income per head against a fixed factor until subsistence is restored. Technology therefore shows up as population density, not as living standards — which is what the pre-1800 data look like, and why Malthus was right about the six centuries before he wrote and wrong from that decade on.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 3, §3.1 (p. 47); Aula 2 (Malthus 1798, Maddison 2001, Clark)
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 4, Exercício 4.5 *A Malthusian Model* (p. 73)

## solow

Q: In the basic Solow model without technological progress, a permanent rise in the saving rate has which effect on output per worker?
- It raises the steady-state level and produces positive growth only during the transition, after which growth returns to zero.
- It raises the steady-state growth rate permanently, since more investment means permanently faster accumulation of capital.
- It leaves both the level and the growth rate unchanged, since higher saving is exactly offset by higher break-even investment.
- It raises the level and the growth rate together, since the two move in the same direction whenever investment rises.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> This is the level-versus-rate distinction, the single most tested point of the lecture. A higher $s$ shifts $sf(k)$ up, moving the intersection with $(n+\delta)k$ to the right, so $k^*$ and $y^*$ rise. Along the way $y$ grows, but diminishing returns exhaust the impulse and long-run growth is zero again. Sustained growth requires technical progress, not thrift.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 4, §4.2 (p. 57); regras [[02_crescimento_solow]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 4, Exercício 4.1 *An Earthquake* (p. 72); Lista 1, Q4

Q: An economy sitting at its Solow steady state loses half its capital stock overnight, with the labour force unchanged. What happens on impact and afterwards?
- Output per worker falls and stays permanently lower, since the destroyed capital is saving that can never be recovered.
- Output per worker falls, then grows back to the original steady state; on impact the wage falls and the return on capital rises.
- Output per worker falls, then grows to a level above the original steady state, since capital is more productive at the margin.
- Output per worker is unaffected on impact and declines slowly, since capital enters production only with a lag of several periods.
<!-- YW5zOjE= -->
> The steady state depends on $s$, $n$ and $\delta$, none of which the disaster touches, so the economy returns to the same $k^*$ — faster than usual, because at low $k$ the gap between $sf(k)$ and $(n+\delta)k$ is wide. On impact each worker has less capital: the marginal product of labour falls and that of capital rises, so wages drop and returns jump. This is Lista 1, Q4.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 4, §4.2 (p. 57) e §4.4 (p. 63)
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 4, Exercícios 4.1 (p. 72) e 4.2 *The Black Death* (p. 72); Lista 1, Q4

Q: A student writes the discrete-time Solow law of motion as $k_{t+1} = (1-\delta)k_t + s f(k_t) - n k_t$. What is wrong with it and when does it matter?
- Nothing is wrong; this is the exact law of motion, and the version with $(1+n)$ in the denominator is the approximation.
- The depreciation term should be $\delta k_{t+1}$, and the difference matters whenever depreciation is large next to investment.
- It replaces division by $(1+n)$ with subtraction of $n k_t$; the two agree only to first order, so the error is second-order.
- The saving term should be $s f(k_t)/(1+n)$ alone, and the error matters only when the economy is far below its steady state.
<!-- YW5zOjI= -->
> The exact law divides total capital next period by next period's labour force: $k_{t+1} = [(1-\delta)k_t + sf(k_t)]/(1+n)$. Writing $-n k_t$ instead uses $1/(1+n) \approx 1-n$, which is fine for $n$ near 0,01 and visibly wrong for high population growth. The continuous-time equation $\dot k = sf(k)-(n+\delta)k$ is the limit of the exact version, not of the shortcut.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 4, §4.2 (p. 57); regras [[02_crescimento_solow]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 4, Exercício 4.1 (p. 72)

P: A closed economy has $Y = K^{1/3}L^{2/3}$ with no technological progress. Its saving rate is $s = 0{,}24$, population grows at $n = 0{,}01$, capital depreciates at $\delta = 0{,}05$, and the economy sits at its steady state.

Q: What are steady-state capital and output per worker?
- $k^* = 8{,}00$ and $y^* = 2{,}00$
- $k^* = 4{,}00$ and $y^* = 1{,}59$
- $k^* = 16{,}00$ and $y^* = 2{,}52$
- $k^* = 2{,}00$ and $y^* = 1{,}26$
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> From $sf(k^*) = (n+\delta)k^*$ with $f(k)=k^{1/3}$: $k^* = [s/(n+\delta)]^{1/(1-\alpha)} = (0{,}24/0{,}06)^{3/2} = 4^{3/2} = 8$. Then $y^* = 8^{1/3} = 2$. The exponent $1/(1-\alpha) = 3/2$ is where most errors happen — using $1/\alpha$ or $1/(1+\alpha)$ produces the other options.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 4, §4.2 (p. 57); regras [[02_crescimento_solow]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 4, Exercício 4.3 *Korean Unification* (p. 73); Lista 2, Q1(b)

Q: In that same economy, what are the steady-state capital-output ratio and the net return on capital $r = f'(k^*) - \delta$?
- $K/Y = 2{,}00$ and $r = 0{,}0833$
- $K/Y = 4{,}00$ and $r = 0{,}0833$
- $K/Y = 2{,}00$ and $r = 0{,}0333$
- $K/Y = 4{,}00$ and $r = 0{,}0333$
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> $K/Y = k^*/y^* = 8/2 = 4$, which also equals $\alpha/(n+\delta)$ — a useful check. The gross marginal product is $f'(k) = \alpha k^{\alpha-1} = (1/3)\,8^{-2/3} = (1/3)(1/4) = 1/12 \approx 0{,}0833$, and the net return subtracts depreciation: $0{,}0833 - 0{,}05 = 0{,}0333$. Reporting the gross figure as $r$ is the standard slip.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 4, §4.4 (p. 63); regras [[03_solow_evidencias]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 5, Exercício 5.6 *Interest Rates* (p. 96)

## ouro

Q: A Cobb-Douglas economy with $\alpha = 1/3$, $n = 0{,}01$, $\delta = 0{,}05$ and $s = 0{,}24$ has $f'(k^*) = 1/12$ at its steady state. Where does it sit relative to the golden rule, and what would reaching it take?
- It is above the golden rule, since $f'(k^*)$ exceeds $n+\delta$; cutting the saving rate would raise consumption immediately and forever.
- It is exactly at the golden rule, since $s$ lies below $\alpha$; no change in $s$ can raise steady-state consumption from there.
- It is below the golden rule, since $f'(k^*)$ exceeds $n+\delta$; reaching it needs $s = 1/3$, which costs consumption today.
- It is below the golden rule, since $f'(k^*)$ falls short of $n+\delta$; reaching it needs a lower saving rate and costs nothing today.
<!-- YW5zOjI= -->
> The golden rule is $f'(k_{ouro}) = n+\delta = 0{,}06$. Here $f'(k^*) = 1/12 \approx 0{,}0833 > 0{,}06$, and since $f'$ is decreasing, a higher marginal product means a *smaller* capital stock: $k^* < k_{ouro}$. With Cobb-Douglas the golden-rule saving rate is $s_{ouro} = \alpha = 1/3 > 0{,}24$, so getting there means saving more, hence consuming less during the transition. The sign of the comparison of $f'$ against $n+\delta$ is inverted by most students.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 4, §4.3 (p. 61); regras [[03_solow_evidencias]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 5, Exercício 5.8 *National Accounts and The Golden Rule* (p. 97)

Q: Why is capital above the golden-rule level treated as an inefficiency while capital below it is not?
- Only the case of excess capital violates the Inada conditions, and a model whose conditions fail cannot deliver a well-defined optimum.
- Cutting saving from above the golden rule raises consumption today and forever, while climbing to it from below costs consumption now.
- Only the case of excess capital leaves the marginal product below depreciation, so the extra capital produces nothing at all.
- Below the golden rule the economy is not yet at a steady state, so the comparison of consumption levels is undefined in that region.
<!-- YW5zOjE= -->
> Over-accumulation is dominated in the strong sense: there is a change that makes every period better off, and no preference parameter is needed to call it an improvement. Under-accumulation involves a genuine intertemporal trade-off, and Solow — which has no utility function — simply cannot say whether it is worth taking. That silence is exactly the gap the microfoundations of the following lectures fill.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 4, §4.3 (p. 61); regras [[03_solow_evidencias]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 4, Exercício 4.4 *An AK Model* (p. 73)

Q: With Cobb-Douglas technology the golden-rule saving rate equals the capital share. Taking a capital share near one third and an investment rate near one fifth of output, what follows for such an economy?
- It saves more than the golden rule, so a permanent cut in the saving rate would raise consumption in every period from now on.
- It saves exactly the golden-rule amount, so the capital share and the investment rate coincide once depreciation is netted out.
- It saves less than the golden rule, so it is dynamically inefficient and the shortfall is a pure welfare loss with no offsetting gain.
- It saves less than the golden rule, so it is dynamically efficient, and reaching the rule would mean lower consumption for a while.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> Since $0{,}20 < 1/3$, the economy is on the low-capital side. That is not an inefficiency: no reallocation makes every period better off, and whether to save more depends on how the present is traded against the future. Option C reproduces the most common confusion — treating any departure from the golden rule as dynamic inefficiency.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 4, §4.3 (p. 61); regras [[03_solow_evidencias]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 5, Exercício 5.8 (p. 97)

## fatores

Q: A plague kills a third of the labour force, leaving the capital stock intact. What happens to the wage and to the return on capital at the resulting higher capital per worker?
- The wage rises and the return falls, since $w = f(k) - kf'(k)$ rises in $k$ while $f'(k)$ falls.
- The wage falls and the return rises, since $w$ tracks $f'(k)$ directly and $k$ is now higher.
- Both rise, since $y = f(k)$ is higher and $w$ and $f'(k)$ are both increasing in $k$.
- Both fall, since total output $Y = F(K,L)$ shrinks and $w$ and $f'(k)$ share a smaller total.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> Each factor is paid its marginal product. Labour is now the scarce factor and capital the abundant one, so $w$ rises and $f'(k)$ falls — the historical record after the Black Death, with real wages in England rising for a century. Note that aggregate output does fall; it is output *per worker* and the wage that rise.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 4, §4.4 (p. 63); regras [[03_solow_evidencias]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 4, Exercício 4.2 *The Black Death* (p. 72), itens (c)-(d)

Q: Why is the exponent on capital calibrated from the capital share in national income rather than estimated by regressing output on inputs?
- Because regression estimates are biased upward whenever the capital stock is measured with error, and national accounts are error-free.
- Because the exponent is a preference parameter, and preferences cannot be recovered from data on production and factor payments alone.
- Because with competitive factor markets and constant returns each factor is paid its marginal product, so the share equals the exponent.
- Because the regression would need instruments for both inputs, and no valid instrument for aggregate labour has ever been proposed.
<!-- YW5zOjI= -->
> Under those two assumptions, total capital income is $f'(k)k$ and its share of output is exactly $\alpha$ for Cobb-Douglas. The parameter is therefore readable straight off the accounts, with no identification problem to solve — which is why the literature uses roughly one third rather than a regression coefficient. The share is an implication of the theory, not an independent measurement.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 4, §4.4 (p. 63) e cap. 5, §5.4 (p. 86); regras [[03_solow_evidencias]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 5, Exercício 5.5 (p. 95); Lista 2, Q2(b)

Q: On a balanced growth path with labour-augmenting technical change, what happens to the return on capital, and why does it matter?
- It grows at the rate of technical change, which is what makes the capital share rise slowly over long horizons.
- It stays constant, because capital per efficiency unit is constant, and this matches the Kaldor fact of a stable return.
- It falls steadily toward the depreciation rate, which is the sense in which the model predicts an end to accumulation.
- It rises with the capital-output ratio, which is the mechanism behind the wide dispersion of returns across countries.
<!-- YW5zOjE= -->
> On the balanced path $\tilde k = K/AL$ is constant, so $f'(\tilde k)$ and hence $r = f'(\tilde k)-\delta$ are constant too, along with the capital-output ratio and the factor shares. That the model reproduces Kaldor facts 3, 4 and 5 simultaneously is the main reason it survived as the workhorse.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 4, §4.5 (p. 69); regras [[03_solow_evidencias]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 5, Exercício 5.6 *Interest Rates* (p. 96)

## tec

Q: On the balanced growth path of a Solow model with labour-augmenting progress at rate $g$ and population growth $n$, which set of growth rates is right?
- Capital per efficiency unit is constant, output per worker grows at $g$, and aggregate output grows at $n+g$.
- Capital per efficiency unit grows at $g$, output per worker is constant, and aggregate output grows at $n$.
- Capital per efficiency unit is constant, output per worker is constant, and aggregate output grows at $n+g$.
- Capital per efficiency unit grows at $g$, output per worker grows at $g$, and aggregate output grows at $n+2g$.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> The whole point of the efficiency-unit transformation is to make $\tilde k$ and $\tilde y$ constant. Since $y = A\tilde y$ and $A$ grows at $g$, output per worker grows at $g$; multiplying by a labour force growing at $n$ gives $n+g$ for the aggregate. Saying that $\tilde y$ grows at $g$ is trap number 4 of the lecture notes.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 4, §4.5 (p. 69); regras [[03_solow_evidencias]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 4, Exercício 4.4 (p. 73)

Q: Why does a balanced growth path with a general production function require technical change to be labour-augmenting?
- Because only labour-augmenting change leaves the marginal product of labour growing at the same rate as the real wage over time.
- Because capital-augmenting change would violate constant returns to scale once the capital stock grows without any bound.
- Because labour is the only factor whose supply grows exogenously, so it is the only one that can carry a trend in the long run.
- Because only that form holds the capital-output ratio constant as capital and output grow; with Cobb-Douglas the forms coincide.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> Balanced growth requires $K$ and $Y$ to grow at the same rate with a constant return on capital. For an arbitrary $F$, only the Harrod-neutral form is consistent with that, which is the content of the Uzawa theorem. The Cobb-Douglas case hides the restriction, because there any $A$ can be rewritten as labour-augmenting by relabelling the exponent.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 4, §4.5 (p. 69); regras [[03_solow_evidencias]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 4, Exercício 4.4 *An AK Model* (p. 73)

Q: The Solow model with exogenous technical change is often said to explain capital accumulation but not growth. What is the precise content of that statement?
- The model has no steady state once $A$ grows, so the long-run behaviour of output per worker is simply left undetermined.
- Long-run growth of output per worker equals $g$, fed in from outside; the model pins down the level, not the trend.
- Long-run growth of output per worker equals $s$ times $\alpha$, so it is set by parameters the model never explains.
- The model explains growth of $Y$ but not of $Y/L$, since $n$ is exogenous while capital accumulation is endogenous.
<!-- YW5zOjE= -->
> Everything the model determines endogenously — $\tilde k^*$, the capital-output ratio, the transition speed — concerns the position of the economy relative to a trend whose slope was assumed. Policies that change $s$ move the level of that path; nothing in the model moves its slope. That limitation is what endogenous growth theory was built to address.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 4, §4.5 (p. 69); regras [[03_solow_evidencias]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 5, Exercício 5.1 (p. 93)

## ptf

Q: Firms discover a way to redirect capital previously tied up guarding their plants into production, with the technology parameter literally unchanged. What does standard growth accounting report?
- It reports nothing, since the capital stock and the labour force are unchanged and the residual is computed from those two alone.
- It reports a fall in the residual, since output rises without any measured increase in the inputs that produce it in the accounts.
- It reports a rise in the residual, since output grows while measured inputs do not, and the residual absorbs everything unmeasured.
- It reports a rise in measured capital, since the guarded capital was excluded from the accounts and is now recorded for the first time.
<!-- YW5zOjI= -->
> The residual is $g_A = g_Y - \alpha g_K - (1-\alpha) g_L$: any output growth not matched by measured input growth lands there by construction. Guard capital was already counted as capital, so freeing it changes no input aggregate while output rises — and the statistician reports "TFP growth" although $A$ never moved. This is exactly the Gotham question of Lista 2, and the reason the residual is called a measure of our ignorance.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 5, §5.4 (p. 86), eq. (5.4.2); regras [[03_solow_evidencias]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 5, Exercício 5.9 *Disease and TFP* (p. 98); Lista 2, Q2(d)

Q: In the decomposition $y = Ak^{\alpha}$ with $\alpha$ near one third, the elasticity of steady-state output to the saving rate is about one half. What does that number do to the accumulation story?
- It supports it, since a saving rate three times as high then delivers roughly a ninefold difference in income per worker.
- It supports it, since the observed spread of saving rates across countries is itself of the order of thirty to one.
- It is silent on it, since the elasticity refers to the transition path and not to the comparison of steady states.
- It undercuts it, since observed spreads in saving and population growth imply income gaps far smaller than the observed ones.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> The elasticity is $\alpha/(1-\alpha) \approx 0{,}5$, so tripling $s$ multiplies $y^*$ by $\sqrt{3} \approx 1{,}7$ — nothing close to the thirty-to-fifty-fold spread in world income. Since saving rates themselves vary by a factor of three or four, capital accumulation can account for only a small part of the gap, and the rest is loaded onto $A$.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 5, §5.2-5.3 (pp. 77-80); regras [[03_solow_evidencias]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 5, Exercícios 5.1 (p. 93) e 5.3 (p. 94)

Q: If income differences across countries were driven mainly by capital per worker, what counterfactual does the model imply, and how does the evidence answer it?
- The marginal product of capital would be enormous in poor countries and capital would flow there; observed flows are far too small.
- The capital share would be far larger in poor countries than in rich ones; the measured shares are in fact roughly similar everywhere.
- Poor countries would show a falling capital-output ratio over time; the measured ratios are instead stable in almost every country.
- Growth rates would be identical across countries in the long run; the observed rates instead differ by several points per year.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> With $\alpha = 1/3$ and diminishing returns, a country with one thirtieth of the capital per worker would have a marginal product roughly a hundred times higher. Nothing like that return differential is observed, and capital does not stampede toward poor countries — the Lucas paradox. The natural reading is that $A$, not $k$, carries most of the difference.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 5, §5.2-5.3 (pp. 77-80); regras [[03_solow_evidencias]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 5, Exercício 5.3 (p. 94); Lista 2, Q2(c)
