## medir

Q: During a downturn a country's measured unemployment rate falls, while its employment-to-population ratio also falls. What is the coherent reading?
- The economy is recovering, since a falling unemployment rate means by construction that more people are finding work.
- Discouraged workers left the labour force, shrinking the unemployment rate's denominator faster than employment fell.
- The two ratios come from different surveys, so a divergence between them carries no economic information at all.
- The labour force grew while employment stayed flat, which mechanically pushes the unemployment rate downward.
<!-- YW5zOjE= -->
> Someone who stops searching is counted as out of the labour force, not as unemployed, so a wave of discouragement lowers both the numerator and the denominator of the unemployment rate. The employment-to-population ratio has no such escape hatch, which is why the two series must be read together. Option D has the sign backwards: a larger labour force with flat employment raises unemployment.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 7, §7.1 (p. 127); regras [[05_trabalho_lazer]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 7, Exercício 7.4 *Puerto Rico* (p. 147)

Q: Two countries report the same unemployment rate, but one has a monthly separation rate three times as high as the other. What else must be true?
- Nothing else must be true; the unemployment rate summarises labour market performance on its own.
- The high-separation country must have a larger labour force, since more separations need more workers to absorb them.
- The high-separation country must have a lower vacancy rate, since its firms post fewer openings per worker.
- The high-separation country must have a proportionally higher job-finding rate, so its spells are much shorter.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> Steady-state unemployment is the ratio of the separation rate to the sum of the separation and finding rates. Holding that ratio fixed while tripling separations forces the finding rate to triple too, which means the average spell is a third as long. Two economies can share an unemployment rate and be completely different places to lose a job.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 7, §7.1 (p. 127) e §7.5 (p. 142); regras [[05_trabalho_lazer]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 7, Exercício 7.7 *The Beveridge Curve* (p. 150); Lista 4, Q2(b)

Q: Aggregate hours fall sharply in a recession. Which margin carries most of that adjustment, and why does the answer matter?
- Mostly the extensive margin, people moving into and out of employment, so a model built only on hours per worker misses most of it.
- Mostly the intensive margin, hours per worker, so the labour supply elasticity of those already employed is the parameter that matters.
- Roughly equally from both margins, so the distinction rarely changes anything when modelling the aggregate economy.
- Mostly the extensive margin, but only where employment protection is weak, so the split is institutional rather than economic.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> Most of the cyclical variation in aggregate hours comes from changes in the number of people working, not from changes in the hours of those who keep their jobs. This is why the static consumption-leisure model, which is entirely about the intensive margin, has to be supplemented by something that handles participation and job loss.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 7, §7.1 (p. 127) e §7.3 (p. 137); regras [[05_trabalho_lazer]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 7, Exercício 7.3 (p. 147)

## estatico

Q: A household has logarithmic utility in consumption and no income besides wages. The government raises the wage tax rate and spends the revenue abroad. What happens to hours worked?
- Hours fall, because the after-tax wage is lower and leisure has become cheaper relative to consumption.
- Hours rise, because the household is poorer and has to work more to sustain any given consumption level.
- Hours do not change, because the income and substitution effects are exactly equal and opposite in this case.
- Hours fall, but only when the preference for leisure is strong enough relative to the taste for consumption.
<!-- YW5zOjI= -->
> With logarithmic consumption utility and no non-wage income, total resources are proportional to the after-tax wage, and that proportionality makes the two effects cancel exactly. The cancellation does not depend on how curved the leisure term is. It is the reason this preference class is the one compatible with stable hours over a century of rising real wages.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 7, §7.2 (p. 131); regras [[05_trabalho_lazer]] · [[formulario-aula-05]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 7, Exercício 7.5(c) *Prescott's Calculation* (p. 148); Lista 4, Q1(b)

Q: A household with logarithmic consumption utility also receives a fixed lump-sum transfer. A higher wage tax now does reduce its hours, which it would not do with no transfer. What does the transfer change?
- It turns leisure into a luxury good, so the household takes proportionally more of it as the tax rate climbs.
- It pushes the reservation wage above the market wage, which drives the household out of the labour force.
- It adds a second distortion, so the two instruments compound and the combined effect exceeds either alone.
- It breaks the proportionality between wage and resources, so the income effect no longer fully offsets substitution.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> Total resources are the after-tax wage times the time endowment, plus the transfer. The transfer does not shrink when the wage does, so resources fall proportionally less than the wage, the income effect is weakened, and the substitution effect wins. The sign of the tax effect on hours is therefore a property of the fiscal package, not of the tax rate.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 7, §7.2 (p. 134); regras [[05_trabalho_lazer]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 7, Exercício 7.5(b)-(c) (p. 148); Lista 4, Q1(b)

Q: How does a higher wage tax affect participation for a household that also receives a lump-sum transfer?
- It raises the reservation wage, so households near the margin stop working altogether.
- It lowers the reservation wage, since the transfer makes staying out of work less attractive than before.
- It leaves the reservation wage untouched, since lump-sum transfers never enter the participation decision.
- It raises the reservation wage only where leisure behaves as an inferior good at low consumption.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> The reservation wage is the value the household places on its time when it is not working at all, and at that point its only consumption is the transfer. A wage tax lowers what any market wage is worth after tax, so the market has to offer more to clear the same bar. The tax therefore bites on the extensive margin as well as the intensive one.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 7, §7.2 (p. 131); regras [[05_trabalho_lazer]] · [[formulario-aula-05]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 7, Exercício 7.4 (p. 147); Lista 4, Q1(a)

Q: In the leisure-consumption plane a higher wage tax rotates the budget line. Around which point does it rotate, and why?
- Around the point where consumption and leisure are both zero, since that is the origin of the two axes.
- Around the point where the household works no hours, since someone earning no wage pays no tax at any rate.
- Around the household's chosen bundle, since the tax is set so as to leave the previous optimum still affordable.
- Around the point where the line meets the consumption axis, since that intercept is fixed by total resources.
<!-- YW5zOjE= -->
> At zero hours the household consumes exactly the transfer, whatever the tax rate is, so that point is common to every budget line in the family. Identifying it is what makes the graphical answer work: with no transfer the pivot sits on the axis and the optimum does not move, while with a positive transfer the pivot is above the axis and the optimum slides toward more leisure.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 7, §7.2 (pp. 131-137), Fig. 7.2.2
> Similar: Lista 4, Q1(b) · [[Resolucao/lista4_resolucao|Resolução L4]]

## elast

Q: The Frisch elasticity of labour supply is typically much larger than the uncompensated one. What separates the two?
- The Frisch elasticity holds the wage fixed and lets hours vary, while the uncompensated one does the reverse.
- The Frisch elasticity describes the extensive margin while the uncompensated one describes the intensive margin.
- The Frisch elasticity is measured over long horizons and the uncompensated one over the business cycle alone.
- The Frisch elasticity holds consumption fixed, stripping out the income effect the uncompensated one contains.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> Both measure the response of hours to the wage; they differ in what is held constant. Holding consumption fixed neutralises the wealth channel and leaves only substitution, which is why the Frisch number is the larger of the two and why it is the relevant one in dynamic models where wages move temporarily.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 7, §7.3 (p. 137) e §7.4 (p. 140); regras [[05_trabalho_lazer]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 7, Exercício 7.5(j)-(l) (p. 149)

Q: One economy taxes wages at 34 percent and another at 53 percent, both rebating the revenue lump-sum, and a calibration finds the high-tax economy works about 22 percent fewer hours. What does that conclusion rest on most heavily?
- On the assumption that consumption and leisure are complements rather than substitutes in preferences.
- On the two economies running different production technologies, which the calibration then attributes to taxes.
- On the labour supply elasticity the preferences imply, which sits far above the range micro studies estimate.
- On the rebate being lump-sum, since a distortionary rebate would open a still wider gap between the two.
<!-- YW5zOjI= -->
> The calculation is arithmetic once the preferences are fixed, so everything rides on those preferences. The implied Frisch elasticity in this class of calibrations is above two, while micro estimates cluster between roughly 0,4 and 1. With an elasticity in the estimated range, the same tax gap explains only a fraction of the hours gap and the rest has to come from somewhere else.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 7, §7.3 (p. 137) e Exercício 7.5 (pp. 148-149)
> Similar: Lista 4, Q1(b) · [[Resolucao/lista4_resolucao|Resolução L4]]

Q: Does rebating wage-tax revenue to households as a lump-sum transfer make the tax more or less distortionary at the margin of hours?
- More, because the rebate removes the income effect that would otherwise offset the substitution effect.
- Less, because the household is compensated and its total resources are restored to their original level.
- Neither, because a lump-sum transfer by definition cannot affect any margin of household choice.
- Less, because the rebate lowers the reservation wage and pulls additional households into employment.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> This is the counterintuitive part. The tax alone makes the household poorer, and a poorer household works more, partly undoing the substitution effect. Handing the money back cancels that impoverishment while leaving the relative price of leisure distorted, so the substitution effect stands alone. A lump-sum transfer has no effect on relative prices but a very large one on the household's resources.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 7, §7.2 (p. 134); Exercício 7.5(e) (p. 149)
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 7, Exercício 7.2 *Military Service* (p. 146)

## dmp

Q: The matching function is assumed to have constant returns to scale. What does that assumption buy?
- It guarantees that the number of matches can never exceed the number of unemployed workers or of vacancies posted.
- It makes the matching process efficient, so that no mutually beneficial meeting is left unrealised.
- It ensures that the relation between unemployment and vacancies slopes downward rather than upward.
- It makes the finding and filling rates depend only on the ratio of vacancies to searchers, not on their levels.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> Dividing matches by unemployment or by vacancies leaves an expression in the ratio alone, which is what makes the model tractable and what lets a single variable summarise the state of the market. The first option is a separate consistency requirement that constant returns does not deliver, and the third follows from the steady-state condition rather than from the returns to scale.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 7, §7.5 (p. 142); regras [[05_trabalho_lazer]] · [[formulario-aula-05]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 7, Exercício 7.7 (p. 150); Lista 4, Q2(c)

Q: Market tightness rises. What happens to a worker's job-finding rate and to a firm's job-filling rate?
- Both rise, since a more active market mechanically produces more meetings on both sides at once.
- The finding rate rises and the filling rate falls, since the same searchers are now spread over more vacancies.
- Both fall, since congestion on the two sides of the market slows down every meeting that would occur.
- The finding rate falls and the filling rate rises, since more vacancies mean sharper competition among workers.
<!-- YW5zOjE= -->
> Tightness is vacancies per searcher, so the two sides read it in opposite directions: it is exactly what makes searching easy for a worker and hard for a firm. The two rates are linked by the identity that the finding rate equals tightness times the filling rate, so their elasticities with respect to tightness sum to one in absolute value.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 7, §7.5 (p. 142); regras [[05_trabalho_lazer]]
> Similar: Lista 4, Q2(c) · [[Resolucao/lista4_resolucao|Resolução L4]]

Q: Why does the steady-state relation between unemployment and vacancies slope downward?
- Because firms post fewer vacancies when unemployment is high, expecting weaker demand for the output they sell.
- Because higher unemployment holds wages down, which makes each vacancy cheaper for the firm to maintain.
- Because higher unemployment cuts the separations to replace and lets each vacancy fill faster, so fewer are needed.
- Because the labour force is fixed, so any rise in unemployment has to be matched by a fall in vacancies.
<!-- YW5zOjI= -->
> The steady-state condition equates hiring to separations. Raising unemployment lowers employment and therefore lowers the separations to be replaced, and at the same time it raises the matches any given stock of vacancies produces. Both forces cut the vacancies required, which is why the curve falls. The first two options describe behaviour the steady-state condition does not contain.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 7, §7.5 (p. 145), Fig. 7.1.4 (p. 129)
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 7, Exercício 7.7 (p. 150); Lista 4, Q2(b)

Q: A single firm posts one extra vacancy. Which effects does it fail to take into account, and in which direction do they run?
- It ignores the gain to unemployed workers, who find jobs faster, and the loss to firms whose vacancies fill more slowly.
- It ignores only the loss it imposes on other firms, so the market unambiguously ends up with too many vacancies posted.
- It ignores only the gain it confers on unemployed workers, so the market unambiguously ends up with too few vacancies.
- It ignores nothing that matters, since it faces exactly the same slower filling rate that it imposes on everyone else.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> Both externalities are present and they pull in opposite directions, so the direction of the net inefficiency cannot be signed without knowing how the surplus of a match is split. The last option confuses a private cost with an external one: the firm does bear the slower filling rate, but only on its own single vacancy, and it takes the market ratio as given when deciding.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 7, §7.5 (pp. 142-146); regras [[05_trabalho_lazer]]
> Similar: Lista 4, Q2(d) · [[Resolucao/lista4_resolucao|Resolução L4]]

Q: Matching efficiency deteriorates while the separation rate is unchanged. What happens in the unemployment-vacancy plane?
- The economy slides down and to the right along an unchanged curve, as firms cut back on their posting.
- The curve rotates about its midpoint, steepening where unemployment is low and flattening where it is high.
- The curve is unaffected, since matching efficiency governs the speed of adjustment and not the steady state.
- The whole curve shifts outward, so a given unemployment rate now coexists with more vacancies than before.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> Matching efficiency is a parameter of the flow technology, so it moves the curve rather than the economy along it. With each vacancy producing fewer meetings, more of them are needed to replace the same separations. Note that a higher separation rate shifts the curve outward too, so observing a shift does not by itself say which of the two moved.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 7, §7.5 (p. 145); Exercício 7.7(a) (p. 150)
> Similar: Lista 4, Q2(e) · [[Resolucao/lista4_resolucao|Resolução L4]]

## ge2

Q: The lecture adds a third representative agent, a firm whose only business is buying capital in one period and renting it out in the next. What does its optimisation deliver?
- The condition that capital depreciates fully between periods, which is what lets the two-period model close.
- The condition that investment equals saving, which is otherwise imposed nowhere else in the model.
- The condition tying the rental rate on capital to the interest rate, which would otherwise be free.
- The condition that firms earn zero profit, which the household's budget constraint requires to hold.
<!-- YW5zOjI= -->
> Without this agent there are two returns in the economy, the rental rate earned by holding capital and the interest rate earned by lending, and nothing forces them into line. The investment firm is the arbitrageur that links them. Full depreciation is an assumption of the two-period version, not a result, and zero profit comes from constant returns in the production firm.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 9, §9.1 (p. 165); regras [[06_equilibrio_geral]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 9, Exercício 9.2 *Storage* (p. 179)

Q: Combining the first-order conditions of all three agents makes every price drop out and leaves two relations. What do those two relations say?
- That the household's budget balances in each period and that capital accumulates according to investment spending.
- That the household's willingness to substitute matches what the technology can deliver, on both margins.
- That factor prices equal marginal products, which simply restates the content of the firm's problem.
- That the interest rate equals the rental rate net of depreciation, on both margins at the same time.
<!-- YW5zOjE= -->
> The prices vanish because each of them appeared twice, once in a household condition and once in a firm condition. What survives connects preferences directly to technology with nothing in between. That is the sharp form of the statement the welfare theorem then proves is no coincidence: the price system carried the technological information to an agent that never saw it.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 9, §9.1 (p. 165); regras [[06_equilibrio_geral]] · [[topics-index]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 9, Exercício 9.5 (p. 181)

Q: What does the definition of a competitive equilibrium require beyond every agent optimising?
- That prices are such that every market clears, with prices being solved for rather than handed down.
- That the resulting allocation is Pareto optimal, which the definition imposes as one of its requirements.
- That the government's budget balances, without which the household's resources are not well defined.
- That every agent shares the same information set, which is what makes a representative agent legitimate.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> An equilibrium is a list of quantities and a list of prices satisfying two things at once: individual optimisation and market clearing. The prices are endogenous objects, not data. Option B inverts the logic of the whole lecture, since Pareto optimality is a theorem about equilibrium and not part of its definition.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 9, §9.1 (p. 165); regras [[06_equilibrio_geral]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 9, Exercício 9.5 *Differences* (p. 181)

Q: The production firm's profit enters the household's budget constraint and equals zero in equilibrium. Why does it vanish?
- Because the household owns the firm, so any profit is offset by an equal and opposite payment elsewhere.
- Because free entry is assumed, and entry competes any positive profit away over successive periods.
- Because the firm's problem is static, and a static firm has no way to accumulate profit across periods.
- Because constant returns mean that paying each factor its marginal product exhausts output exactly.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> This is Euler's theorem for homogeneous functions applied to the production side: under constant returns, total factor payments at marginal products use up total output, leaving nothing over. It is a property of the technology, not a consequence of entry or of the time structure. The term is still written in the budget constraint because it must be zero in equilibrium, not by assumption.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 9, §9.1 (p. 165); cap. 4, §4.4 (p. 63)
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 5, Exercício 5.5 (p. 95)

## tbe

Q: The competitive equilibrium of this economy is Pareto optimal. What exactly does that establish?
- That the distribution of resources is fair, since no participant has grounds to complain about their share.
- That the allocation maximises total output, which is the operational content of the word efficiency.
- That no one can be made better off without making someone worse off, which says nothing about fairness.
- That the allocation maximises the sum of utilities across all the households in the economy.
<!-- YW5zOjI= -->
> Pareto optimality is a weak criterion by design: an allocation giving everything to one household can satisfy it. That weakness is what makes it broadly acceptable, since it needs no interpersonal comparison. Option D is a different criterion altogether, utilitarian rather than Paretian, and it does require comparing utilities across people.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 9, §9.2 (p. 168); regras [[06_equilibrio_geral]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 9, Exercício 9.1 (p. 179); cap. 2, Exercício 2.3 (p. 41)

Q: The proof supposes an allocation that is feasible for the planner and strictly better for the household, then derives a contradiction. What is the contradiction?
- The supposedly feasible allocation turns out to demand more resources than the technology can produce.
- The household turns out to prefer the equilibrium allocation, contradicting the premise that the other was better.
- The firms turn out to be making negative profits, which contradicts the assumption that they optimise.
- The prices turn out not to clear the markets, which contradicts the definition of a competitive equilibrium.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> The argument runs in two steps. Because the household chose freely and did not choose the alternative, that alternative must have been unaffordable. Because the firms were already maximising, they earned at least what the alternative quantities would have yielded. Combining the two and cancelling the prices leaves a claim on resources the economy cannot meet, so the allocation was never feasible.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 9, §9.2 (p. 168), Proposição 9.1
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 9, Exercício 9.5 (p. 181)

Q: What is the essential difference between the planner's problem and the household's?
- The planner maximises a weighted sum of utilities across households, while the household maximises only its own.
- The planner is constrained only by what the economy can produce; the household faces a budget line built from prices.
- The planner chooses prices as well as quantities, while the household has to take the prices as given.
- The planner optimises over an infinite horizon, while the household optimises over just two periods.
<!-- YW5zOjE= -->
> The planner has no prices at all, only the technology. That is precisely why the theorem is interesting: two problems with entirely different constraint sets turn out to have the same solution, which means the budget line was carrying the technological information all along. With one representative household, option A collapses into maximising that household's own utility.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 9, §9.2 (p. 168); regras [[06_equilibrio_geral]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 9, Exercício 9.12 *Optimal vs Fixed Savings Rates* (p. 185)

Q: A household cannot borrow against its future income. What follows for the welfare theorem?
- The theorem still holds, since a borrowing constraint changes the timing of consumption but not its total.
- The theorem still holds, provided the constraint actually binds for only a minority of households.
- The theorem fails, but only because the constraint renders the household's problem non-convex.
- The theorem fails, because a complete set of markets is exactly what the proof depends on.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> The proof needs the household to have been able to afford any feasible alternative and to have rejected it. A borrowing constraint means some feasible allocations were never available, so rejection carries no information about preference and the argument breaks. This is the failure the students already met by hand when a credit limit was imposed on the two-period problem.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 9, §9.2 (p. 168); cap. 6, §6.4 (p. 120)
> Similar: Lista 3, Q2 · [[Resolucao/lista3_resolucao|Resolução L3]]

## fase

Q: In the phase diagram of this economy one locus is a vertical line and the other a concave curve. What does each one represent?
- The vertical line is where output is constant, and the concave curve is where investment just covers depreciation.
- The vertical line is where the interest rate equals the discount rate, and the concave curve is the production function itself.
- The vertical line is where consumption is unchanging, and the concave curve where capital is unchanging.
- The vertical line is where the capital stock is unchanging, and the concave curve is where consumption is unchanging.
<!-- YW5zOjI= -->
> Each locus is one of the model's two dynamic equations set to zero. Setting the consumption equation to zero yields a condition on capital alone, hence a vertical line; setting the capital equation to zero yields consumption as output net of replacement, hence a concave curve. Option D swaps them, which is the most common way to get the whole diagram backwards.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 9, §9.3 (p. 172); regras [[06_equilibrio_geral]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 9, Exercício 9.9 *An Oil-Producing Economy* (p. 184)

Q: Why is the locus along which consumption stays constant a vertical line in this diagram?
- Because consumption is a jump variable, and jump variables are always drawn as vertical loci.
- Because the condition that pins it down involves the capital stock alone, with consumption nowhere in it.
- Because preferences are homothetic, which makes the consumption path independent of the capital stock.
- Because labour supply is held fixed, which removes consumption from the economy's resource constraint.
<!-- YW5zOjE= -->
> Setting consumption growth to zero leaves a requirement on the marginal product of capital, which fixes one value of capital and imposes nothing on consumption. Every level of consumption is therefore compatible with it, and the locus is vertical. Option A states a fact about consumption that is true but is not the reason for the shape.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 9, §9.3 (p. 172); regras [[06_equilibrio_geral]] · [[topics-index]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 9, Exercício 9.9 (p. 184)

Q: Starting from a capital stock below its steady-state level, exactly one initial consumption level puts the economy on a converging path. Why is that the one it picks?
- Because capital was inherited and cannot jump, so consumption is the only free variable and one value converges.
- Because households coordinate with one another to select the path that maximises consumption in the long run.
- Because the alternative paths violate the resource constraint and so are not feasible allocations at all.
- Because the converging path reaches the steady state fastest, and speed is what the household actually values.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> The state variable is predetermined and the control variable is free, which is what makes the saddle path the equilibrium selection rather than an assumption. The other paths are perfectly feasible period by period, which is why option C is wrong; they simply run into exhausted capital or collapsed consumption, and no optimising household would start down one.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 9, §9.3 (p. 172); regras [[06_equilibrio_geral]]
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 9, Exercício 9.9 (p. 184); Exercício 9.12 (p. 185)

Q: News arrives today that productivity will improve at a known future date, with nothing changing before then. What happens immediately?
- Nothing happens until the date arrives, since the technology is unchanged and no variable can respond to an event that has not occurred.
- Capital starts accumulating right away, as households save more in order to exploit the coming improvement.
- Consumption falls right away, since households save in anticipation of the higher return that is on its way.
- Consumption jumps up right away and capital starts falling, so the economy reaches the new path exactly on the announced date.
<!-- YW5zOjM= -->
> Consumption can jump and does so on the announcement, which lowers saving and starts capital falling. The old dynamics then govern the drift until the date arrives, and the size of the initial jump is pinned down by the requirement that the drift land on the new saddle path at exactly that moment. The economy therefore moves in the opposite direction to its eventual destination.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 9, §9.3 (p. 172); Aula 6, efeitos de antecipação
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 9, Exercício 9.8 *A New Technology* (p. 183)

## ouro

Q: Where does the steady-state capital stock of this economy sit relative to the level that would maximise steady-state consumption?
- Exactly at it, which is what the First Welfare Theorem guarantees for a competitive equilibrium.
- Below it, because the household discounts the future and will not give up present consumption for it.
- Above it, because once saving becomes a choice, households accumulate capital well past the useful point.
- It depends on the depreciation rate, which can put it on either side of that level.
<!-- YW5zOjE= -->
> Reaching the consumption-maximising stock from below requires a stretch of lower consumption in exchange for more later, and a household that discounts the future turns that trade down. The steady state therefore sits strictly on the low-capital side. Option A confuses two different optimality criteria, which is precisely the confusion the lecture is built to dispel.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 9, §9.3 (p. 172); cap. 4, §4.3 (p. 61)
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 9, Exercício 9.12 (p. 185); cap. 5, Exercício 5.8 (p. 97)

Q: How can an allocation be Pareto optimal and still hold less capital than the consumption-maximising level?
- Because Pareto optimality is defined over reallocations at a point in time, not over reallocations across periods.
- Because the consumption-maximising level is itself unreachable without a planner able to enforce it.
- Because the household values its whole consumption path, and maximising one point on it is a different objective.
- Because the welfare theorem holds only approximately once the horizon is infinite rather than finite.

> There is no contradiction because the two statements optimise different things. The Golden Rule maximises consumption in the infinitely distant future; the household maximises a discounted sum over the entire path. Nothing in the model makes impatience an error, and a planner maximising the household's own preferences would land on exactly the same capital stock.
> Ref: Kurlat (2020), cap. 9, §9.2 (p. 168) e §9.3 (p. 172)
> Similar: Kurlat, cap. 9, Exercício 9.12 *Optimal vs Fixed Savings Rates* (p. 185)

Q: What test separates a genuinely inefficient capital stock from one that merely sits below the consumption-maximising level?
- Whether consumption could be raised in every period at once, which only over-accumulation permits.
- Whether the capital-output ratio exceeds one, which is the conventional threshold for over-accumulation.
- Whether the saving rate exceeds the capital share of income, which is the condition the Solow model supplies.
- Whether the marginal product of capital exceeds the wage, which signals that capital is being underused.
<!-- YW5zOjA= -->
> Over-accumulation is dominated in the strong sense: cutting saving raises consumption today and in every later period, so calling it a failure needs no preference parameter. Under-accumulation involves a genuine trade between present and future, and whether it is worth taking depends on preferences the efficiency criterion does not contain.
