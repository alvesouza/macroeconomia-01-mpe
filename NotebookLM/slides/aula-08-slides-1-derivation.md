Build a slide deck that derives, with no algebraic gaps, the two curves of the New-Keynesian model used in Lecture 8 of a graduate macroeconomics course. Deck 1 of 4: it derives; deck 2 draws and shifts the curves; deck 3 works the shocks and multipliers; deck 4 covers the zero bound, deleveraging and optimal policy.

Source. "Livro_Benigno_2015_NewKeynesianEconomics_AS_AD_View.pdf" (= BENIGNO), sections 3 and 4, printed pages 505 to 509. Every result slide carries its address: section, equation, printed page. Where BENIGNO's text and a displayed equation disagree, follow the text and flag it on the slide.

Thesis the whole deck serves. Neither curve is assumed: demand is a household Euler equation, supply a firm's mark-up condition, and every parameter that later decides a policy question enters at an identifiable step of those two derivations.

Structure, in this order.

ONE. THE HOUSEHOLD PROBLEM, SOLVED. Preferences, equation 1, and the single intertemporal budget constraint, equation 2. Put the Lagrangian on the slide and take all four first-order conditions: consumption and hours in each period. Show that dividing the hours condition by the consumption condition cancels the multiplier and leaves the marginal rate of substitution equal to the real wage; label it as belonging to the SUPPLY side, since the firm will use it. Then divide the two consumption conditions to reach the Euler equation, equation 3. State that profits sit inside the lump-sum transfer, which is what makes Ricardian equivalence hold here.

TWO. FROM EULER TO A DEMAND CURVE. Assume isoelastic utility and log-linearise, showing both approximations used: the log of one plus the real rate, and the definition of the rate of time preference. Reach equation 4 and state it in one line — consumption growth rises with the real rate. Substitute the real rate to reach equation 5, then argue on its own slide that equation 5 is already a demand curve: a higher current price level, with the long-run level given, means lower expected inflation, a higher real rate, more saving, less consumption. Then aggregate. Derive y equal to the consumption share times c plus g from footnote 4, with the one-line derivation on the slide, because scaling g by steady-state output is not the obvious convention. Reach equation 6 and define sigma as the elasticity of substitution times the consumption share. One slide on why sigma is not that elasticity, and on the fact that only the difference between current and long-run spending shifts the curve.

THREE. TAXES IN THE DEMAND CURVE. Redo the constraint with consumption and labour taxes, give the tax-wedge version of the intratemporal condition, equation 7, and reach equation 8. One slide on the observation that the consumption-tax difference occupies the same slot as expected inflation, so a promised future consumption-tax rise is expansionary today.

FOUR. THE FIRM'S PRICING DECISION, DERIVED. Dixit-Stiglitz demand, equation 9, and profits with sales and payroll taxes, equation 10. Differentiate profits with respect to the firm's own price on the slide, and rearrange to equation 11. Show the two limits: theta to infinity gives marginal-cost pricing, and theta above one is needed for a finite mark-up. Then eliminate the real wage with equation 7 to reach equation 12, and present the aggregate mark-up, equation 13, as a table of four tax rates plus monopoly power. The consequence: the model cannot distinguish a tax wedge from a monopoly wedge, which is why a mark-up shock can be read as an oil price.

FIVE. THE NATURAL RATE. Define it as the allocation under fully flexible prices, derive equation 14, log-linearise it and reach equation 15. Sign all three coefficients and give the economics of each, especially the wealth effect by which public spending raises the natural rate through labour supply. Add a warning slide: the coefficient on g is the inverse of sigma over the inverse of sigma plus eta, proved two ways — from the log-linearisation, and from BENIGNO's statement on page 508 that output rises one-to-one with spending when eta is zero.

SIX. THE SUPPLY CURVE. Divide equation 12 by equation 14; the mark-up and productivity cancel. Log-linearise to equation 16, the marginal-cost equation. Aggregate the price index over sticky and flexible firms, show the algebra turning the relative price into alpha over one minus alpha times the price surprise, and arrive at equation 17 with kappa defined. One slide on the three derivatives of kappa, with the explicit correction that more rigidity FLATTENS the curve. One slide on the anchor: the curve passes through expected prices and the natural rate. Close on what this curve is not: a New-Classical Phillips curve in the price level, not a forward-looking one, per footnote 7.

Scope lock. Benigno sections 1 to 5 only. No Calvo staggered pricing, no stochastic DSGE, no Bellman equations, no time-series econometrics. Sections 6 to 12 belong to Lecture 9 and to decks 3 and 4.
