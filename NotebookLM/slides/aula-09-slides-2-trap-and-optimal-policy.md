Build a slide deck on the three situations in which this model stops behaving like a textbook, for Lecture 9 of a graduate macroeconomics course. Deck 4 of 4: the zero lower bound, debt deleveraging, and optimal policy as a third line on the diagram.

Source. "Livro_Benigno_2015_NewKeynesianEconomics_AS_AD_View.pdf" (= BENIGNO), sections 9 to 12, printed pages 516 to 523, figures 11 to 14. Every result slide carries section, equation and printed page.

Thesis the whole deck serves. These are one topic seen three times: what happens to the same two curves when one thing changes — a floor on the instrument, a borrowing limit on some households, or an objective function attached to the diagram.

Structure, in this order.

ONE. THE FLOOR, AND THE LOCUS IT IMPLIES. Give both justifications for the bound: the text's argument that agents could otherwise borrow to fund unbounded consumption, and footnote 11's sharper version, where the bound is the remuneration of money. Put the nominal rate at zero in equation 21 and derive the ceiling curve of figure 11. With the closed-form gap from deck 2, show that under anchored long-run prices the best attainable gap at a zero rate is sigma over one plus sigma-kappa, times the natural real rate. Box the diagnostic: non-negative natural real rate, the gap closes; negative, it cannot.

TWO. WHY THAT RATE TURNS NEGATIVE, AND THE WAY OUT. Isolate the growth term and name the two sources from page 517: poor long-run growth prospects, which is deck 3's optimism case reversed, and forced deleveraging. Show figure 12, where demand and the ceiling fall together because both contain long-run natural output. Then use deck 2's fact that the nominal rate and the long-run price level share one slot with opposite signs, and box the commitment that closes the gap at a zero rate: the long-run price level must exceed the expected level by the absolute value of the natural real rate.

THREE. FISCAL POLICY IN THE TRAP, AS A RANKING. One table from equations 22 and 23, with columns for raises output and narrows the gap, marking every instrument in both. Isolate the policy that fails the pair — a current cut in sales and payroll taxes — because it raises the natural rate by more than output. Then the three reasons for preferring long-run instruments: a current spending rise pushes prices down, harming balance sheets in a deflation; it crowds out private consumption while a future cut raises it; and it must be reversed or financed by the very taxes that are most contractionary.

FOUR. DELEVERAGING, AND A DEMAND CURVE THAT CAN SLOPE UP. Two types with different discount factors, flow constraints rather than a lifetime constraint, and the debt limit, equations 24 to 27. State the step that drives everything: constrained borrowers have no Euler equation, so their consumption is whatever the limit allows. Name what exponential utility buys: only aggregate consumption survives in equation 29, so the supply curve is unchanged. Derive equation 31, marking the interest term scaled by the saver share and the borrowers' consumption profile as a new shifter, then equation 32. One slide on the slope: differentiate with respect to prices, box the slope parameter, and table the two channels — the borrowing limit, which flattens, and Fisher, which steepens. Say which dominates, give the condition for an upward-sloping curve, and show figure 13.

FIVE. PARADOXES AND MULTIPLIERS ABOVE ONE. One slide each for thrift, toil and flexibility, naming the mechanism rather than the label, then footnote 20 alone on a slide because it is the exam trap: a credibly anchored long-run price level makes toil and flexibility disappear. Then both multiplier formulas from page 521 at the stated calibration, with the three published values in a table, and the reason they pass section 8's limit — the rate cannot rise and constrained households spend every unit of income.

SIX. OPTIMAL POLICY, AND THE PRICE OF A MARK-UP SHOCK. Present equation 33 and read its three features: the target is the efficient level, the price term is the unanticipated movement, and the weight rises with rigidity and with the elasticity of substitution across goods. Give the objection to an instrument rule — its coefficients depend on the shock process — then derive the targeting rule in three steps: substitute the supply curve, minimise over output, substitute back. Box equation 34 with its slope, its anchor, and the condition for it to be flatter than demand. Then solve supply and the targeting line together and box the optimal split: a fraction one over one plus theta-kappa of the shock into prices. Show both limits collapsing onto two of deck 3's points, evaluate the fraction at the article's parameters, and show figure 14. Close on equation 35 — that sign belongs to the mandate, not the economy.

Scope lock. Benigno sections 9 to 12. No Calvo staggered pricing, no stochastic DSGE, no Bellman equations, no time-series econometrics.
