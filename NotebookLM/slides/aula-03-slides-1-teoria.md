THESIS THIS DECK BUILDS TOWARD. Three extensions are added to the model here, and each one both strengthens it and exposes a limit. Optimising over steady states gives a benchmark that turns out not to be a recommendation. Decentralising the model gives factor prices for free. Adding technology gives sustained growth, at the cost of assuming what was to be explained.

STRUCTURE. Aim for 20 to 24 slides. Each title is a full-sentence claim. AULA3 runs as prose, so open every block with the one-line proposition it establishes.

BLOCK A - THE QUESTION THE GOLDEN RULE ANSWERS. Pose it precisely before solving: among the steady states reachable by choosing a saving rate, which delivers the highest consumption per worker. Stress that the comparison is across steady states, not paths, and that this restriction does more work than it appears to.

BLOCK B - DERIVING THE CONDITION. Write steady-state consumption per worker as output minus the investment needed to sustain the capital stock. Maximise with respect to capital per worker and obtain the condition on the marginal product of capital net of effective depreciation. Show every step. Then plot consumption per worker against the capital stock as a hump and mark the peak. Read the condition back off the drawing as the point where the slope of the production function equals the slope of the maintenance line.

BLOCK C - THE TWO SIDES OF THE PEAK. Treat them separately, since only one is interesting. Below the peak, raising saving lowers consumption today and raises it later, so the comparison involves a genuine trade-off across time. Above the peak, raising saving lowers consumption in every period at once, with no compensation ever. Name the second case dynamic inefficiency and give the observable test for it in terms of the return to capital against the growth rate of the economy.

BLOCK D - WHY THIS IS A BENCHMARK AND NOT ADVICE. Give this its own slide, placed here deliberately as a forward hook. Maximising steady-state consumption is a particular objective, and it is not the objective of a household that prefers consumption sooner rather than later. State that lecture six will derive a different condition from an explicit optimisation, that the two conditions differ, and that the difference is not an error in either. Do not develop the comparison here.

BLOCK E - DECENTRALISING THE MODEL. Introduce competitive firms hiring capital and labour. Derive the rental rate as the marginal product of capital and the wage as the marginal product of labour, then express both in per-worker terms. Show the wage geometrically as the vertical intercept of the tangent to the per-worker production function, and the return to capital as its slope. Emphasise what has been gained: the prices were not assumed, they were read off the same function that generated output.

BLOCK F - FACTOR SHARES. Compute each factor's share under a Cobb-Douglas function and show the shares equal the exponents and are constant. Connect back to the Kaldor facts: constant shares were one of the regularities the model had to reproduce. Give the benchmark capital share the course uses in calibration.

BLOCK G - TECHNOLOGICAL PROGRESS. Introduce labour-augmenting progress and explain, rather than assert, why the model needs that specific form: with any other form the ratios that the Kaldor facts hold constant would fail to be constant. Convert the model into efficiency units, rewrite the law of motion, and show that the per-efficiency-unit variables settle at a steady state while output per worker grows at the rate of technology.

BLOCK H - THE UNCOMFORTABLE CONCLUSION. State it without softening. Long-run growth in living standards now equals the assumed rate of technical progress. The model has been made consistent with sustained growth, not made to explain it. Frame the rest of the lecture as the search for where that progress comes from, and note that it does not fully succeed.
