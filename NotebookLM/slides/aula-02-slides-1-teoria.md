THESIS THIS DECK BUILDS TOWARD. Sustained growth in living standards is a recent and historically abnormal event. Before explaining it, a model has to be told what it is required to reproduce, and this deck assembles that list of requirements.

STRUCTURE. Aim for 18 to 22 slides. Each title is a full-sentence claim. Reproduce the lecture's figures rather than describing them in words.

BLOCK A - THE PICTURE THAT SETS THE PROBLEM. Open exactly as AULA2 does, with output per person for the United States and the United Kingdom across two centuries, first on a linear axis and then on a logarithmic one. Give the log axis a full slide of its own: on it, a constant growth rate is a straight line, so the eye reads rates rather than levels, and a curve that bends upward on a log scale is accelerating. This is a reading tool the whole course reuses, so establish it deliberately here.

BLOCK B - THE MALTHUSIAN WORLD. Present the mechanism as an argument with three links: higher real wages raise the surviving population, a larger population presses on a fixed factor, and the pressure returns wages to subsistence. Name the preventive checks and the positive checks explicitly, as AULA2 lists them. State the equilibrium concept: the system has a stable wage, and it sits at subsistence, so income per person is pinned regardless of technology. Explain why this makes the model a theory of population rather than of living standards.

BLOCK C - THE EVIDENCE, AND THE TIMING. Show Clark's series of English real wages across seven centuries on a log axis and mark where Malthus was writing. The mechanism described the preceding centuries well and broke almost exactly when it was published. Give the subsistence level of consumption AULA2 cites, as an order of magnitude rather than a measurement.

BLOCK D - HOW THE LONG SERIES ARE BUILT. Give Maddison's reconstruction its own block. List the indirect evidence used where no national accounts existed: average heights, livestock counts, crop yields, output of particular industries such as iron. Then state the consequence for interpretation, that uncertainty widens the further back the series runs, and that a smooth plotted line conceals this. Note which country has the best long-run data and why that matters for what we generalise from.

BLOCK E - THE KALDOR FACTS. List them one per line and attach the supporting series from the lecture to each: output per person grows at a roughly constant rate; the capital-output ratio is roughly stable; the return to capital is roughly stable; the shares of income going to capital and labour are roughly stable; growth rates differ substantially across countries. Then add the caution the course wants carried forward: these are regularities observed over a particular period and a particular set of countries, not laws, and building a model to reproduce them is a weaker achievement than explaining them.

BLOCK F - CONVERGENCE AS AN OPEN QUESTION. Show cross-country output per person on a log scale, including the Latin American series in AULA2. State absolute convergence and conditional convergence as two distinct empirical claims and say what evidence would distinguish them. Leave the question unresolved, since the evidence arrives in the next lecture, and say so explicitly so the student does not expect an answer here.

BLOCK G - THE INGREDIENTS OF THE MODEL. Assemble Solow's components without yet solving anything: a production function with constant returns to scale and diminishing returns to each factor separately, the Inada conditions and precisely what they buy, a saving rate taken as a constant, a depreciation rate, and a population growth rate. For each assumption, state in one line which Kaldor fact it is there to deliver. Flag the assumption that the rest of the course will attack: the saving rate is exogenous here, and lecture four derives it from a household's choice.
