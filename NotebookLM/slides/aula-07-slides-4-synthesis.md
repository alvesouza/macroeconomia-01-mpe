Thesis the whole deck serves. This lecture did not prove that money is neutral. It showed what you have to assume to make money neutral, and every one of those assumptions is a door that a later lecture opens.

ONE. The assumption ledger. One slide, two columns: what was assumed, and what was concluded. Assumed: prices are perfectly flexible; output and the real interest rate are set by real forces alone; money demand has a constant elasticity; the central bank controls the money stock. Concluded: a one-off rise in money raises prices proportionally; sustained money growth gives inflation equal to money growth minus the elasticity times output growth; real variables are untouched. Make the dependency explicit, so the conclusions read as consequences of the left column rather than discoveries about the world.

TWO. Neutral but not superneutral. Neutrality concerns a change in the level of money and holds here. Superneutrality concerns a change in the growth rate, and this chapter violates it. Show the chain on one slide: faster money growth raises inflation, which raises the nominal interest rate, which lowers desired real balances, which raises trips to the bank, which is a real resource cost. So the model in which money is neutral is also one in which sustained inflation has real costs. Name that as the reconciliation, not a contradiction.

THREE. Seigniorage as the fiscal bridge. One pass only, since this deck is a synthesis. Present the government budget constraint and the point that the monetary base enters it like debt except that it pays no interest. Then the Laffer logic in four steps: fast base growth, high inflation, high nominal rates, shrinking real balances, which is the tax base. Conclude that the revenue motive is self-limiting. Use the Jones chapter for the historical cases, and note where Kurlat leaves the algebra as exercise 11.6.

FOUR. The costs, and the disagreement inside them. Put the four costs on one slide: shoe leather, menu costs, relative price uncertainty, and bank seigniorage with excessive entry into banking. Then the slide that makes it interesting: shoe leather implies an optimal inflation rate of minus the real rate, the Friedman Rule, requiring deflation, while menu costs imply zero. The two point at different optima and the lecture does not pretend otherwise. Add that real central banks target a small positive rate, and name the practical reasons standing outside this model.

FIVE. Backwards into the course. Three connections, one slide each. To Lecture 1: the index-number problem reappears unchanged, because choosing a basket is still a choice and the two indices weight differently. To Lectures 4 to 6: every interest rate there was a real rate, and this lecture explains why money demand needs the nominal rate instead, it being the opportunity cost of holding money. To Lecture 3: output growth appears here only as a parameter money demand absorbs, and nothing here determines it.

SIX. Forwards, and the seam. State the course-long thread: money is neutral, but only in the long run. This lecture built the long-run half. Identify where the handover happens: the problem set item asking what occurs when the price level cannot move. The honest answer is that these chapters supply no mechanism, and supplying it is the work of the sticky-price model in Lectures 8 and 9. Say that Kurlat defers this to later chapters this course does not use, and that the course uses the Benigno article instead. Close on the question to carry into Lecture 8: if prices cannot adjust, which channel clears the money market, and what does that imply about output.
