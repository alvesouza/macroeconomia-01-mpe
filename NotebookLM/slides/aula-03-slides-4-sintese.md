THESIS THIS DECK BUILDS TOWARD. The residual is not a measure of technology. It is a measure of what the accounting exercise could not attribute, and the difference between those two readings is the whole lesson of the lecture. A student who says "productivity grew" when the correct statement is "we could not explain this part" has misunderstood the chapter.

STRUCTURE. Aim for 18 to 22 slides. Each title is a full-sentence claim. This deck argues and connects; it does not re-teach.

BLOCK A - THE SCORECARD, UPDATED. Return to the table from the previous lecture, in which the model was scored against the growth facts, and add the new rows this lecture supplies: the size of cross-country income differences, the direction of capital flows, and the constancy of factor shares. Mark each as matched, unmatched, or matched only by assumption. The unmatched rows are the agenda for the rest of the field.

BLOCK B - WHAT THE RESIDUAL MEASURES. State the definition operationally: the residual is what remains after subtracting measured factor contributions from measured output growth. Then enumerate what can therefore sit inside it, and give each item a line saying how it would appear: genuine technical change, reallocation of resources toward more productive uses, institutional change, unmeasured human capital, variation in how intensively capital is used, and plain measurement error. Present it as a table so the list reads as a partition rather than a caveat.

BLOCK C - THE PROBLEM SET AS PROOF. Use the Gotham question from the problem set as the demonstration, since it was constructed for exactly this purpose. Restate its result in one line: a change that frees capital from an unproductive use, with the technology level literally unchanged, is reported in full as productivity growth. Then generalise it. Any improvement in how well an economy allocates what it already has appears in the residual, which is why the residual responds to policy reform, to the removal of distortions, and to shifts in the composition of output.

BLOCK D - THE TWO ACCOUNTINGS, KEPT APART. Restate the distinction between decomposing growth over time and decomposing income levels across countries, with one sentence on what each is good for. Then work an example of the confusion: a claim about the sources of a country's growth used to explain why it is poorer than another. Show why the inference fails.

BLOCK E - THE GOLDEN RULE IS NOT THE OBJECTIVE. Pick up the hook from deck 1. Maximising steady-state consumption is one objective among several, and it treats consumption in every period as equally valuable. A household that discounts the future will choose to hold less capital than the Golden Rule prescribes, and doing so is optimal rather than shortsighted. State that lecture six derives the alternative condition explicitly, name the form it takes, and say that the comparison between the two is one of the natural synthesis questions for the final assessment. Do not derive it here.

BLOCK F - WHERE THE PRODUCTIVITY GAP MIGHT COME FROM. Summarise the candidate explanations as a ranked list with a confidence marker on each, and be explicit that the ranking is contested. Then state what would settle it, which is evidence that separates barriers to adoption from misallocation from human capital, and note why that separation is hard with aggregate data.

BLOCK G - CONNECTING FORWARD AND BACKWARD. Close with the two links that matter. Backward: every quantity in this lecture is a measured aggregate, so the conventions of the first lecture set a floor on the precision of everything computed here, and the residual inherits the errors of both output and capital measurement. Forward: productivity reappears in the final lectures as the shock that moves aggregate supply, where the distinction between a temporary and a permanent change in productivity has consequences the growth model cannot see. Name that destination and stop.
