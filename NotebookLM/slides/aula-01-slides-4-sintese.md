THESIS THIS DECK BUILDS TOWARD. Measuring an economy forces three choices that no amount of data can settle: what counts as production, which prices supply the weights, and whose welfare the number is meant to track. The third choice is the one that has an explicit parameter attached, and naming that parameter is the point at which the ethics stops hiding.

STRUCTURE. Aim for 18 to 22 slides. Each title is a full-sentence claim. This deck argues; it does not enumerate.

BLOCK A - THE THREE CHOICES, COLLECTED. Open by stating the three choices and pointing back to where each arose. The production boundary appeared as an imputation problem. The weighting appeared as the gap between two equally correct growth rates. The third has not yet been faced, and the rest of the deck faces it.

BLOCK B - WHAT OUTPUT PER PERSON IS SILENT ABOUT. Enumerate the omissions, but resolve each rather than listing it: household production, leisure, environmental depletion, the informal sector, distribution, and mortality. For every item, classify it as either a measurement failure that better data could fix or a definitional boundary that no data would cross. That classification is the content of the block, and it should be presented as a two-column table.

BLOCK C - THE HUMAN DEVELOPMENT INDEX. Give the three components and the actual normalisation that maps each raw indicator to a score between zero and one. Compute the index for two contrasting countries with the arithmetic visible. Then deliver the critique in the same block, because the index and its weakness belong together: the goalposts are chosen, and the three dimensions are weighted equally without argument. Demonstrate the fragility numerically by reweighting and showing two countries swap rank.

BLOCK D - THE JONES AND KLENOW MEASURE. This is the technical heart of the deck and needs at least five slides. State the thought experiment precisely: the fraction of consumption a person in the rich country would give up to be made indifferent to living a randomly assigned life in the poor country. Build the expected-utility expression with consumption, leisure, inequality and life expectancy as separate terms, and derive the decomposition so each term's margin is visible. Then present the results as a two-column table, welfare ranking beside output-per-person ranking, and highlight the countries that move most in either direction. Explain in one slide why leisure and mortality frequently push in opposite directions.

BLOCK E - THE PARAMETER WHERE THE ETHICS SITS. Isolate the curvature parameter of the utility function and state its double role: it governs how heavily inequality is penalised and how much an additional year of life is worth. Show one country's welfare score computed at three values of that parameter in a single table, and show the ranking changing. State the conclusion without hedging: choosing this parameter is taking a position on how much a society should weigh its worst-off members, and no dataset determines it. Connect it explicitly to the difference principle that KURLAT raises in exercise 2.10.

BLOCK F - INEQUALITY AS A SCALAR. Define the Lorenz curve and the Gini coefficient, draw the curve, and compute it on a five-person example. Then show two distributions with the same coefficient but different shapes, establishing that a scalar summary discards structure that matters for the measure in block D.

BLOCK G - WHY THIS LECTURE COMES FIRST. Close by connecting forward. Every model in the remainder of the course takes output, capital and consumption as measured quantities and reasons about their movement. Name three later moments where this lecture's conventions return: the growth facts the next lecture asks a model to reproduce, the productivity residual computed from these same aggregates, and the price index that reappears as inflation. Close with the question the student should ask of any macroeconomic statistic thereafter.
