THESIS THIS DECK BUILDS TOWARD. Once consumption is tied to lifetime resources rather than to current income, a striking result follows almost for free: the timing of taxation stops mattering. The result is exactly as strong as the assumptions behind it, and the second assigned question exists to break one of them.

STRUCTURE. Aim for 18 to 22 slides. Each title is a full-sentence claim. This deck argues and connects; it does not re-teach.

BLOCK A - THE PUZZLE, CLOSED. Return to the conflict from deck 1. A household having an unusually good year saves most of the excess, so its consumption share falls, producing the cross-section pattern; an economy permanently richer consumes proportionally, producing the stable aggregate share. One table, with the type of income change in one column and the predicted response in the other. Name Friedman.

BLOCK B - TRANSITORY AND PERMANENT. Generalise into the distinction that recurs for the rest of the course. Give the marginal propensity to consume out of each kind of income change from deck 2, side by side. Then the policy corollary: a one-off transfer and a permanent tax cut of equal size do very different things to spending.

BLOCK C - RICARDIAN EQUIVALENCE. Taxes enter only through their present value, so a government cutting taxes today and raising them tomorrow by what the debt costs leaves consumption unchanged in both periods and moves only saving. Show saving rising by exactly the tax cut, and interpret it: the household is saving to pay the future tax. State the headline, that the timing of taxation does not matter, with the qualifier beside it.

BLOCK D - THE ASSUMPTIONS, LISTED. One line each: the household borrows and lends freely at the same rate; it lives as long as the tax it must pay, or cares about whoever does; taxes are lump-sum; and it understands the government's budget constraint. Mark which of the four the problem set attacks.

BLOCK E - BREAKING IT. Take the borrowing constraint from the second assigned question. Show that a constrained household consumes its current resources, so a tax cut today is spent today and the equivalence fails. Then show why this matters for the rest of the course: whether fiscal policy moves demand depends on how many households are in that situation, which turns a theoretical result into an empirical question about credit access.

BLOCK F - THE OTHER BREAK, AND WHY IT IS DIFFERENT. Take the tax on the return to saving. Here the equivalence fails for a different reason: the tax changes a relative price rather than only lifetime resources, so it distorts the margin between consuming now and later. Contrast the two failures in a table, one row per case, with columns for what breaks and which margin is distorted. This contrast is a natural assessment question.

BLOCK G - CONNECTING FORWARD. Name three destinations and stop. First, the labour lecture, where the same machinery returns with leisure in place of future consumption and the same substitution-against-income contest decides how hours respond to wages. Second, the general equilibrium lecture, where the interest rate is determined by saving matching investment and the Euler equation yields the steady-state condition that differs from the Golden Rule. Third, the aggregate demand curve of the final lectures, which is this Euler equation written for the aggregate.

BLOCK H - CONNECTING BACKWARD. Return to the growth lectures. The saving rate Solow took as constant has now been derived from a choice, depending on the interest rate, on patience and on expected future income. State what this buys: the earlier model's central assumption is no longer arbitrary, and the course can now ask whether the saving an economy chooses is the saving it should choose.
