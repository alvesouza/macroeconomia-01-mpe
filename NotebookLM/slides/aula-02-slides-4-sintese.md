THESIS THIS DECK BUILDS TOWARD. The model reproduces the growth facts, but reproducing is not explaining. Set against the list it was built to match, Solow delivers the constant ratios and the convergence behaviour on its own, and delivers sustained growth in living standards only because sustained growth was assumed. Naming that gap precisely is the point of the lecture, and it is the question the next lecture inherits.

STRUCTURE. Aim for 18 to 22 slides. Each title is a full-sentence claim. This deck argues and connects; it does not re-teach.

BLOCK A - THE SCORECARD. Build one table with the Kaldor facts as rows and three columns: what the model predicts, whether the prediction matches, and whether the match was earned or assumed. Fill every cell. The stable capital-output ratio and stable return to capital are earned, following from the steady state rather than being imposed. Cross-country differences in levels are earned, following from differences in parameters. Sustained growth in output per person is not earned: with the assumptions used so far the long-run growth rate is zero.

BLOCK B - THE ZERO-GROWTH RESULT, STATED PLAINLY. Give the result its own block, since students often leave the lecture believing the opposite. In the model as built, output per worker is constant in the steady state. Show why: diminishing returns mean each additional unit of capital adds less output, so accumulation eventually only replaces depreciation. Then state the implication for policy: nothing that raises the saving rate changes the long-run growth rate, and the transition, however long, ends.

BLOCK C - LEVEL AND RATE, ONE MORE TIME. Restate the distinction as a diagnostic, not a derivation, since it is the commonest error in assessment. Give three policy claims from the press and classify each as level or rate. The rule: ask whether the change moves where the curves cross, or only how fast the economy travels there.

BLOCK D - WHAT CONVERGENCE THE MODEL ACTUALLY PREDICTS. Resolve the question deck 1 left open, to the extent this lecture can. The model predicts convergence to a country's own steady state, which is conditional convergence. It predicts absolute convergence only among countries sharing the same parameters. State what evidence would distinguish the two, and say explicitly that the empirical verdict arrives in the next lecture.

BLOCK E - THE ASSUMPTION THE COURSE WILL ATTACK. Isolate the exogenous saving rate. Explain what is unsatisfactory about it: the saving rate is the outcome of decisions by households weighing consumption now against consumption later, so treating it as a constant hides the very behaviour macroeconomics wants to study. Name the destination precisely: the fourth lecture derives saving from an optimisation problem and the sixth closes the model in general equilibrium, where the steady state chosen by optimising households is not the one maximising steady-state consumption. Do not develop either claim here.

BLOCK F - THE OTHER TWO OPEN DOORS. Name the two threads the next lecture picks up, in one slide each. First, technological progress, which is what has to be added for the model to produce sustained growth, and the fact that adding it means assuming the growth we set out to explain. Second, the confrontation with data, in which the model is asked how much of the observed gap between rich and poor countries capital differences can account for, and does not do well.

BLOCK G - HOW THIS LECTURE CONNECTS BACKWARD. Close by returning to the first lecture. Every series and every calibration here rests on measured output, capital and population. Name two places where lecture one's measurement conventions materially affect a conclusion drawn here, and state the standard of care: the qualitative results are robust, the quantitative ones inherit the uncertainty of their inputs.
