THESIS THIS DECK BUILDS TOWARD. A consumption function written on current income cannot fit both the household data and the aggregate data at once. Replacing it with a household choosing across time resolves the conflict, and every later result in the lecture is a corollary of that single move.

STRUCTURE. Aim for 18 to 22 slides. Each title is a full-sentence claim. Derive everything; assert nothing that can be shown.

BLOCK A - THE KEYNESIAN CONSUMPTION FUNCTION. Quote the fundamental psychological law as AULA4 states it and translate it into a function of current income with a marginal propensity to consume between zero and one. Then make the distinction the lecture makes carefully and that students routinely miss: the verbal claim, that consumption rises by less than income, is a statement about an elasticity, while the functional form is usually written about a marginal propensity. Put both expressions side by side and show they are different restrictions.

BLOCK B - THE EVIDENCE THAT FITS. Present the cross-section: across households at one moment, the consumption share falls as income rises. Show the fitted relationship and how strongly it supported the theory when first estimated.

BLOCK C - THE EVIDENCE THAT DOES NOT. Present the aggregate time series, where consumption stays close to a constant fraction of output over long periods. Give the figure AULA4 uses for that fraction. Then state the implication that alarmed the profession: if the share fell as economies grew, then growth would generate a chronic shortfall of demand, with output produced that nobody would buy. Devote one slide to why this consequence mattered historically and one to posing the puzzle as the question the rest of the lecture answers.

BLOCK D - THE TWO-PERIOD HOUSEHOLD. Set up preferences as the discounted sum of period utilities, with the discount factor strictly between zero and one, and say what it represents. Give the two period-by-period budget constraints exactly as numbered in AULA4, with initial wealth and taxes included. Show what saving does in each period.

BLOCK E - THE INTERTEMPORAL BUDGET CONSTRAINT. Combine the two constraints by eliminating saving, showing the substitution step explicitly. Present the result and read it back in words: the present value of consumption equals the present value of resources. Draw the constraint in the plane of first- and second-period consumption, marking the endowment point, the slope, and both intercepts. State clearly what allowing negative saving assumes, namely that the household can borrow and will repay.

BLOCK F - THE EULER EQUATION. Derive it, either by direct substitution or with a multiplier, and show every step. Then present the result as a statement about margins rather than as a formula to memorise: at the optimum, the utility lost by giving up one unit today equals the discounted utility gained from the extra units it buys tomorrow. Draw the tangency in the same plane as block E so the algebra and the geometry sit together.

BLOCK G - PUTTING A FUNCTION ON PREFERENCES. Introduce the constant-relative-risk-aversion form and its logarithmic special case, showing that the second is the limit of the first. Substitute into the Euler equation and solve for first-period consumption in closed form. Keep this expression visible for the remaining decks, since every exercise in the lecture uses it. State plainly what the curvature parameter governs, that it controls how willing the household is to have consumption differ across periods, and flag that deck 2 will show it deciding the sign of a key derivative.

BLOCK H - THE RESOLUTION, PREVIEWED. Return to block C. If consumption depends on lifetime resources rather than income today, a household having an unusually good year saves most of the excess, producing the cross-section pattern, while an economy permanently richer consumes proportionally, producing the time series.
