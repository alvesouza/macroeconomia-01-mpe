Thesis. There is a capital stock that maximises consumption in the long run, and finding it is easy. The interesting part is what the two sides of that peak mean, because only one of them describes a trade-off, and the other describes a country making itself poorer in every period at once.

Scope. Stay at the level of AULA3 and KURLAT section 4.3. The optimising household's version of this question belongs to a later lecture; if a source reaches into it, note it in one line and move on.

BEAT ONE. THE GAP THAT BECOMES THE CURVE. Start from the diagram the previous lecture ended with: the per-worker production function, the saving curve beneath it, and the straight maintenance line. Shade the vertical distance between output and the maintenance line at the crossing, and label it as consumption. Then sweep the saving rate slowly from low to high, letting the crossing slide along the horizontal axis, and trace the height of that shaded gap as a new curve on a second chart beneath. Narration says only what is being traced. The transition is the traced curve completing itself into a hump.

BEAT TWO. THE PEAK. Isolate the new chart on screen: consumption per worker on the vertical axis, capital per worker on the horizontal, one hump. Mark its maximum with a vertical dropline. Then show the derivative condition being read geometrically: draw the production function above and the maintenance line, and slide a tangent along the production curve until its slope matches the line's slope. Show the two slopes matching exactly at the capital stock under the peak. Narration states the condition in words rather than symbols, that the extra output from one more unit of capital equals what that unit costs to keep.

BEAT THREE. THE LEFT SIDE IS A TRADE-OFF. Put an economy on the left of the peak and raise its saving rate. Show two panels: consumption over time on one, and the position on the hump on the other. Let consumption dip immediately as more output is diverted to investment, then rise above its original level as the capital stock builds, settling higher. Narration names the shape, a cost now and a gain later, and states that whether it is worth taking is a question about preferences rather than about the diagram.

BEAT FOUR. THE RIGHT SIDE IS NOT. Now place an economy to the right of the peak and raise its saving rate again. Run the same two panels. This time consumption falls immediately and settles lower as well, so the path is below the original at every date. Narration names this case and states the conclusion in the strongest available terms: there is no trade-off here, and no preference over time could justify it. Show the two time paths from beats three and four side by side in one frame so the asymmetry is visible in a single glance.

BEAT FIVE. HOW YOU WOULD KNOW. Show a simple two-column comparison on screen: the return to capital in one column, the growth rate of the economy in the other, for several hypothetical economies. Highlight the row where the return falls below the growth rate. Narration gives the test in one sentence and states what makes it useful, which is that both quantities are observable, so the diagnosis does not require knowing the production function. Note briefly that real economies rarely fail this test, which is itself informative.

BEAT SIX. THE TRAP IN THE WORD RULE. Show the hump again on screen and place a marker at the peak. Then place a second marker to its left and label it as the choice an impatient society would make. Narration lands the thesis: the peak maximises consumption in a steady state, treating every future period as equally important, and a society that prefers consumption sooner will rationally hold less capital than the peak. State that a later lecture derives that alternative point from an explicit choice, and that the two conditions differ without either being an error.
