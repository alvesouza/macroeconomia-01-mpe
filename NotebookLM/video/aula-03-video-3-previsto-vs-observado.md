This is video 3 of 4 for this lecture, and it is built around a single visual object: two bars side by side, the income gap the calibrated model predicts and the income gap actually observed. Thesis. This is the beat at which the model fails, and the failure is the most productive thing in the chapter. Given real investment rates, the model predicts that rich and poor countries should be much closer together than they are, and the size of the shortfall is what the rest of the field has been working on since.

Scope. Stay at the level of AULA3 and KURLAT sections 5.2 and 5.3. If a source reaches past that level, note it in one line and move on.

BEAT ONE. THE TEST BEING SET UP. Show a small parameter table on screen with one row per input: the capital share, the depreciation rate, the population growth rate, and the investment rates of a rich and a poor country. Narration explains what is about to happen, that the model will be asked to predict the income ratio between the two countries using only the difference in their investment rates. The transition is the table folding into a single closed-form expression, which then waits with an empty result box.

BEAT TWO. THE PREDICTION. Let the expression evaluate on screen, digit by digit, producing one number. Draw it as a bar. Narration names that figure and nothing else. Then hold the frame for a moment with only the predicted bar visible, so the viewer forms an expectation before the comparison arrives.

BEAT THREE. THE FACT. Draw the observed income ratio as a second bar beside the first. Let it rise past the top of the frame and force the vertical axis to rescale, shrinking the predicted bar in the process. Narration names the observed figure and states the shortfall plainly. The rescaling is the argument: the two quantities are not close enough to require careful comparison.

BEAT FOUR. WHY MORE CAPITAL CANNOT CLOSE IT. Return to the per-worker production function with its diminishing slope. Mark the capital stock the poor country would need in order to reach the observed gap by capital alone, far out along the horizontal axis. Show how little additional output the curve delivers over that stretch. Narration draws the conclusion from the shape rather than from arithmetic, that the curvature which made the model well behaved is the same property that prevents capital from explaining large income differences.

BEAT FIVE. THE PRICE THAT SHOULD HAVE APPEARED. Suppose instead the gap really were capital scarcity. Show the tangent from the previous video drawn at the poor country's low capital stock, its slope now extremely steep, and display the implied return to capital as a figure on screen. Beside it, draw an arrow representing investment flowing from the rich country to the poor one, sized to match that return. Then show the actual direction and size of observed flows, which do not match. Narration names the puzzle and states what it rules out.

BEAT SIX. NAMING WHAT IS MISSING. Return to the two bars and insert a third element between them: a shaded block bridging the predicted bar up to the observed one, labelled as the unexplained difference. Narration is careful here, and this is the point of the whole video: the block has been named, not measured, and calling it productivity is a label rather than an explanation. Show three candidate captions attaching to the block in turn, covering technology, how well resources are allocated, and errors in measuring the inputs, so the viewer sees that one block holds several distinct things.

Closing image. End on one image the student should be able to redraw from memory: the two bars, predicted and observed, with the shaded unexplained block bridging them and its three candidate labels beneath. Hold the final frame while the narration states the question the last video takes up, which is what happens when that block is computed formally and treated as if it were a measurement.
