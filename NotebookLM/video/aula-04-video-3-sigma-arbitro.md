Thesis. Whether saving rises or falls when the interest rate rises is not a fact about the world that the model reports. It is a contest between two effects, and one parameter decides the winner. Students who memorise a direction get the exercise wrong; students who can name the parameter get it right in every case.

Scope. Stay at the level of AULA4 and KURLAT section 6.2. Borrowing constraints and taxes on returns belong to the fourth video; if a source reaches into them, note it in one line and move on.

BEAT ONE. THE PIVOT. Start from the finished diagram of the previous video and raise the interest rate. Show the budget line rotating about the endowment point, becoming steeper, with the horizontal intercept moving in and the vertical intercept moving out. Narration states why the endowment point is the pivot, since a household that neither saves nor borrows is unaffected by the price of doing either. The transition is the old line remaining faintly on screen so the rotation stays visible.

BEAT TWO. THE FIRST FORCE. Isolate the substitution effect by drawing a compensated line, parallel to the new one but tangent to the original indifference curve. Show the optimal point sliding along that curve, away from present consumption and toward future consumption. Narration names the mechanism in one sentence: future consumption has become cheaper, so the household buys more of it. Mark on screen that this movement always points the same way, whatever the household's circumstances.

BEAT THREE. THE SECOND FORCE. Now shift from the compensated line to the actual new line, holding the substitution movement fixed so only the income effect remains. For a household that was saving, the new line lies outside the old one over the relevant region, so the household is richer and increases consumption in both periods, including today. Narration states that this pushes in the opposite direction to the first force. Show both movements as two arrows on the same diagram, pointing against each other, with the net movement drawn as their sum.

BEAT FOUR. THE PARAMETER THAT DECIDES. Show two panels side by side, identical except for the curvature of the indifference curves. In the panel with gently curved preferences, the substitution arrow is long and the net movement reduces present consumption, so saving rises. In the panel with sharply curved preferences, the household resists an uneven path, the substitution arrow is short, and the net movement raises present consumption, so saving falls. Narration names the parameter and states plainly that it, and nothing else, determines which panel applies.

BEAT FIVE. THE ANSWER AS A CURVE, NOT A SIGN. Build a new chart with the interest rate on the horizontal axis and saving on the vertical. Trace the saving curve for a low value of the curvature parameter, sloping upward. Then trace it again for a high value, sloping downward. Highlight the value at which the curve is flat, where the two effects exactly cancel, and mark it on screen. Narration states that the exercise asks for this condition, not for a number, and that an answer giving only a direction is incomplete.

BEAT SIX. BORROWERS INVERT IT. Return to the rotating budget line and place the household on the other side of the endowment point, borrowing rather than saving. Run the same rotation. Show that the new line now lies inside the old one over the relevant region, so a higher rate makes this household poorer and the income effect reverses sign. Display a two-by-two table on screen with saver and borrower as rows and the two effects as columns, filling in the signs. Narration states the general lesson, that the income effect takes its sign from which side of the endowment the household sits on.
