Thesis. If a household can borrow freely, moving a tax from tomorrow to today changes nothing it does. The result is exact, and it is exactly as strong as that one assumption, which is why the problem set spends its second question destroying it.

Scope. Stay at the level of AULA4 and KURLAT sections 6.2 to 6.4. If a source reaches past that level, note it in one line and move on.

BEAT ONE. THE EXPERIMENT. Show the budget line with the endowment point marked, and beside it a two-row table listing the tax paid in each period. Now cut the first-period tax by one unit and raise the second-period tax by one unit plus interest. Show the endowment point sliding down and to the right along the line. Narration states what changed, that the household has more money now and owes more later. The transition is the line itself being highlighted, unchanged, while the point sits at its new position.

BEAT TWO. NOTHING MOVES. Keep the diagram and show the tangency point staying exactly where it was, with the indifference curve untouched. Then show the vertical distance between the endowment and the chosen point growing by precisely the amount of the tax cut. Narration names what that distance is, which is saving, and states the result: consumption is identical in both periods, and the household has simply saved the tax cut in order to pay the higher tax later. Display the horizontal intercept as an expression on screen and show the two tax terms combining into a single present value that has not changed.

BEAT THREE. WHY IT HAD TO HAPPEN. Show the algebraic expression for lifetime resources with the two taxes appearing only inside one discounted sum. Highlight that sum. Narration draws the general principle from the picture: any two tax schedules with the same present value describe the same budget line, and the household chooses from the line. Then list the assumptions on screen as four short captions, and let one of them, free borrowing and lending, begin to flash.

BEAT FOUR. THE KINK. Impose a floor on how much the household may borrow. Show the budget line being truncated: to the right of a certain point it simply stops, and the feasible set acquires a corner at the endowment side. Narration explains what the bound represents in economic terms, a lender unwilling to advance more than a certain amount against future income. Show two households on the same kinked set, one whose desired point is still on the sloped portion and one whose desired point lay beyond the corner.

BEAT FIVE. THE CONSTRAINED HOUSEHOLD BEHAVES DIFFERENTLY. Focus on the second household, now sitting at the corner. Show its indifference curve cutting the constraint rather than touching it tangentially, and display the Euler condition beside the diagram now holding as an inequality rather than an equality. Then rerun the tax experiment from beat one on this household. This time the extra money today is consumed today, the corner moves, and the chosen point moves with it. Narration states the conclusion: the equivalence has failed, and it failed because of credit, not because of the arithmetic.

BEAT SIX. A DIFFERENT WAY TO BREAK IT. Return to the unconstrained household and replace the second-period lump-sum tax with a tax on the return to saving, calibrated to raise the same revenue. Show the budget line now rotating rather than staying put, because the price of moving consumption across time has changed. Display a two-row comparison table: the lump-sum tax shifts the line and leaves the consumption ratio intact, the tax on returns rotates it and changes that ratio. Narration names the margin each one distorts and states which is the more efficient instrument.
