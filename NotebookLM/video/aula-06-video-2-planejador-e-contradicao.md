Thesis. The theorem is not a claim that markets are good. It is a claim that a competitive price system carries exactly the information a benevolent planner would need, so the two problems have the same answer. Proving it is worth the effort because the proof tells you precisely which assumptions are load-bearing, and that list is where every policy argument in the rest of the course begins.

Scope. Stay at the level of AULA6 and KURLAT section 9.2, the two-period case. Do not attempt the second welfare theorem and do not discuss distribution beyond one sentence.

BEAT ONE. TWO PROBLEMS, SIDE BY SIDE. Split the screen. On the left, the decentralised household: maximise utility subject to a budget line, taking prices as given. On the right, the planner: maximise the same utility subject to the technology itself, with no prices anywhere. Show the two constraint sets highlighted in different colours. Narration names Pareto optimality carefully, that no one can be made better off without someone else being made worse off, adding that this says nothing about fairness.

BEAT TWO. THE PLANNER'S SET IS THE TECHNOLOGY. Clear the left half and enlarge the planner's problem. Show its three constraints substituted into the objective one by one, until one expression remains, in the capital carried forward and the two labour inputs. Narration emphasises that the planner faces no budget line, only what the economy can physically make. Draw the resulting feasible set as a shaded region in the consumption plane, bounded by a concave frontier.

BEAT THREE. THE PLANNER'S OWN CONDITIONS. Take the derivatives with respect to the two labour inputs and the capital carried forward, and let three conditions appear. Then rearrange them on screen until two of them are visibly identical to the two equations that closed the previous video. Narration states the result flatly: the planner, who never saw a price, wrote down the same equations. Show the equilibrium point sitting on the frontier at the tangency.

BEAT FOUR. THE PROOF, SET UP AS A TRAP. Now run the contradiction. Place a second point, marked with a hat, somewhere the argument supposes is feasible and strictly better. Narration walks three visible steps. First: if it is better and the household did not choose it, it was unaffordable, so show the hatted point above the budget line. Second: firms already earned at least as much as they would at the hatted quantities, so show the profit inequality. Third: substitute the second into the first and let the prices cancel.

BEAT FIVE. WHERE THE HATTED POINT LANDS. Show what survives the cancellation: an inequality saying the hatted allocation demands more than the technology can deliver. Animate the hatted point moving outside the shaded feasible region as the algebra resolves. Narration states the contradiction: a point supposed to satisfy the planner's constraints violates one of them, so no such point exists. Display the proposition in final form, stated in KURLAT as Proposition 9.1.

BEAT SIX. THE LIST THAT MATTERS MORE THAN THE THEOREM. Clear the diagram to a two-column table. On the left, the settings where the result survives: goods differentiated by date, exogenous technical progress, uncertainty with complete markets, households with different preferences and wealth. On the right, the five failures: monopoly power, externalities, asymmetric information, incomplete markets, borrowing constraints. Light the last row and connect it to the credit-constraint problem the students already solved. Narration lands the thesis: policy analysis starts by naming which row on the right is active.
