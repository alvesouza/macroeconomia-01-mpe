Build a narrated video overview. This is video 3 of 4 for this lecture, and it is built around a single visual object: the same country's output drawn as two bars of different height, one converted at the market exchange rate and one at purchasing power parity. Every beat develops that object. The circular flow, the index-number fork and the welfare rankings belong to the other three videos.

Sources. "Aula_MPE_Macro1_2026_Slides_1.pdf" (= AULA1) and chapter 1, section 1.2, of "Livro_Pablo_Kurlat_-_A_Course_in_Modern_Macroeconomics_2020_-_libgen.li.pdf" (= KURLAT). Question 1 of "Lista_MPE_Macro1_2026_Lista1.pdf" (= LISTA1) supplies the closing case.

Output language: Brazilian Portuguese (pt-BR). On-screen labels in Portuguese, with the English term in parentheses where it is the standard one.

Thesis. Converting one country's output into another country's currency is not a currency operation, it is a second index-number problem wearing a different hat. The exchange rate prices only the goods that cross borders, and most of what people buy does not cross.

Scope. Stay at the level of AULA1 and KURLAT section 1.2. If a source reaches past that level, note it in one line and move on.

BEAT ONE. THE NAIVE CONVERSION. On screen, show one country's output as a single bar in its own currency. Beside it, show an exchange rate as a simple multiplier, and let the bar rescale into the second currency. Narration states the operation plainly and names only the resulting figure. The transition is a question mark appearing over the rescaled bar, standing for the assumption just made: that one unit of the second currency buys the same bundle in both places.

BEAT TWO. TESTING THE ASSUMPTION. Show a two-column price table on screen, the same goods priced in both countries after conversion at the market rate. Choose the rows deliberately: a laptop, a litre of fuel, a haircut, a bus fare, a restaurant meal, a month of rent. Let the rows sort themselves so the tradeable items cluster near equal prices and the non-tradeable items fall far below. Narration points at the two clusters rather than reading the cells, and names the property that separates them, which is whether the good can be shipped.

BEAT THREE. WHY THE CLUSTERS SPLIT. Draw a simple two-sector diagram: one sector producing goods that can be traded, one producing services that cannot. Show an arbitrage arrow running between countries in the traded sector and no arrow in the non-traded sector. Then show wages inside each country linked by a vertical connector, so that the low productivity of the poorer country's traded sector pulls down wages, and those wages set the price of its haircuts. Narration walks that chain in order. The transition is the non-traded price falling as the wage connector is dragged down, which is the mechanism made mechanical.

BEAT FOUR. BUILDING THE PARITY RATE. Return to the price table and show a basket being assembled from it on screen, with quantity weights attached to each row. Show the total cost of that basket in each currency, and the ratio of the two totals appearing as a new rate. Narration names that rate and states what it means: the number of units of one currency that buys the same basket as one unit of the other. Then flag the problem the viewer should already suspect, that the weights of the basket had to be chosen, which is the same fork as the previous video.

BEAT FIVE. THE TWO BARS. Show the payoff frame: the same country's output as two bars side by side, one built with the market rate and one with the parity rate, with the gap between them shaded and labelled. Repeat the frame for a second, richer country where the gap nearly vanishes. Narration states the systematic direction, that the market rate understates real income where incomes are lower, and that the correction shrinks as countries converge. Highlight the shaded gap in both frames so the contrast is spatial.

BEAT SIX. THE CASE THE COURSE ACTUALLY SETS. Show the structure of the problem set question as a four-panel storyboard: nominal output in two years, real output at each of two price bases, conversion at the market rate, conversion at parity. Do not compute; show the panels as empty frames with their labels, so the viewer sees the shape of what is being asked. Narration states that the two halves of this lecture, the index fork and the parity wedge, are the two halves of that question.

Closing image. End on one image the student should be able to redraw from memory: the two-column price table with its traded and non-traded clusters, and directly beneath it the two bars with the shaded gap between them. Those two pictures are the whole argument for parity conversion. Hold the final frame while the narration states the question the last video takes up, which is whether a bigger bar means a better life.
