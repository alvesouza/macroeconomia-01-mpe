Thesis. Everything studied separately so far was one side of a market. General equilibrium is the act of writing down who is on the other side of each of those markets and insisting that the two sides agree. Nothing new is optimised; the novelty is entirely in the closing.

Scope. Stay at the level of AULA6 and KURLAT section 9.1, the two-period case. Do not prove optimality and do not draw dynamics. Where a source reaches into the infinite-horizon model, say in one line that it is coming and move on.

BEAT ONE. THREE BOXES AND FOUR MARKETS. Draw three empty boxes on screen, labelled household, production firm and investment firm, with four labelled channels between them: goods, labour, capital rental and credit. Leave every box empty. Narration states the simplification that makes this possible, that each box holds one representative agent, and flags the third box as the one the student has not met: a firm whose only business is buying capital today and renting it out tomorrow. The transition is the household box filling in first.

BEAT TWO. THE HOUSEHOLD, AND THE TWO CONDITIONS IT ALREADY KNOWS. Fill the household box with its problem and let two conditions emerge beside it, one at a time. The first equates the willingness to trade leisure for consumption with the wage; the second equates the willingness to trade consumption across periods with the interest rate. Show each condition being lifted from the earlier lecture it came from, with a caption naming which. Narration makes the point that nothing here is new: these are the two decisions already solved separately, now held simultaneously.

BEAT THREE. THE PRODUCTION FIRM, AND WHY IT IS ONE LINE OF ALGEBRA. Fill the second box. Show the profit expression and let two conditions drop out, each setting a marginal product equal to a factor price. Show them attaching to the labour channel and the capital-rental channel respectively. Narration notes what makes this so short: the firm is static, rents everything period by period, and earns zero profit under constant returns. Show that zero appearing in the household's income.

BEAT FOUR. THE THIRD BOX, AND WHAT IT PRICES. Fill the investment firm. Show it buying capital at one date and receiving rental income at the next, discounted. Let its single condition appear and land on the credit channel, linking the rental rate of capital to the interest rate. Narration states what this box exists to do: it is the arbitrage tying the return on holding capital to the return on lending. Show the two-period version and note that with partial depreciation the condition carries an extra term.

BEAT FIVE. MARKET CLEARING, WRITTEN OUT. Now light up each of the four channels in turn and show the clearing condition written on it: goods in each period, capital accumulating from one period to the next, labour adding up to the time endowment. Narration states the definition of equilibrium as the lecture gives it: a list of quantities and a list of prices such that every agent optimises and every market clears. Emphasise that prices are being solved for, not given.

BEAT SIX. THE COLLAPSE. Take the five conditions on screen and combine them, visibly, letting the prices cancel. Two equations survive. Show them appearing alone on a cleared screen: one setting the consumption-leisure trade-off equal to the marginal product of labour, the other setting the intertemporal trade-off equal to the marginal product of capital. Narration lands the thesis: prices have disappeared, and what is left says the household's willingness to substitute equals the economy's ability to transform. The next video proves that is no accident.

Closing image. End on one image the student should be able to redraw from memory: the three boxes, the four channels, and the two surviving equations written underneath with the prices struck through. Hold the frame while the narration poses the question the next video answers, which is whether an all-knowing planner, told to maximise the household's welfare directly, would have chosen anything different.
