Thesis. Two equations govern this economy, and each of them is zero along one curve. Draw the two curves, mark where each variable is rising and where it is falling, and the entire long-run behaviour of the economy becomes something you read off a picture rather than solve.

Scope. Stay at the level of AULA6 and KURLAT section 9.3, under the lecture's two assumptions: fixed labour supply and constant relative risk aversion. Do not run the technology-news experiment, which is the fourth video, and do not derive the saddle path analytically.

BEAT ONE. THE TWO EQUATIONS, AND WHAT THEY MOVE. Open with a clean split screen and no axes yet. On the left, the intertemporal condition in growth-rate form, saying how consumption changes between periods. On the right, the resource constraint, saying how the capital stock changes. Narration names the asymmetry that organises everything: capital is a state variable, inherited and unable to jump, while consumption is chosen fresh each period and can. The transition is the equations sliding aside as empty axes appear, capital horizontal, consumption vertical.

BEAT TWO. THE FIRST CURVE IS VERTICAL. Set the consumption equation to zero. Show the growth factor set to one, the exponent falling away, and what remains: a condition on the marginal product of capital alone, equal to depreciation plus the rate of time preference implied by the discount factor. Narration draws the consequence: consumption does not appear, so the locus is vertical. Draw it and label the capital stock where it sits. Add arrows on either side, consumption rising to the left where capital is scarce, falling to the right.

BEAT THREE. THE SECOND CURVE IS CONCAVE. Set the capital equation to zero. Show the algebra collapsing to consumption equal to output minus the capital that must be replaced. Draw the curve: rising steeply from the origin, flattening, peaking, then declining. Narration explains the shape from diminishing returns and says what the curve means: every pair of capital and consumption at which capital would stay put. Add vertical arrows, capital accumulating below the curve and decumulating above.

BEAT FOUR. FOUR QUADRANTS, FOUR PAIRS OF ARROWS. Let the two curves intersect and shade the four regions they create. Place a pair of arrows in each region and give each quadrant a one-line reading on screen: low consumption so capital rises, low capital so consumption rises, and the three variants. Narration walks the four, then poses the problem: from almost anywhere the arrows carry the economy away from the intersection.

BEAT FIVE. ONE PATH IN, TWO PATHS OUT. Drop a starting capital stock on the horizontal axis, below the steady state, and try three different initial consumption choices from it. Show the one too high curving up and left until capital is exhausted; the one too low curving down and right until consumption collapses; then the single value between them that rides into the intersection. Trace it in a distinct colour and name it the saddle path. Narration lands the mechanism: capital could not be chosen, so consumption had to be, and exactly one value works.

BEAT SIX. THE PEAK IS THE GOLDEN RULE. Return attention to the concave curve and mark its highest point. Show the condition defining it, the marginal product of capital equal to depreciation alone, beside the condition defining the vertical line, which carries an extra impatience term. Since the marginal product falls in capital, that term puts the vertical line strictly left of the peak. Narration lands the thesis: the equilibrium is Pareto optimal and still holds less capital than would maximise steady-state consumption. The second audio explains why that is not a failure.

Closing image. End on one image the student should be able to redraw from memory: the two curves, the four quadrants with their arrows, the saddle path drawn through the intersection, and the peak of the concave curve marked to the right of the vertical line with both conditions written beneath. Hold the frame while the narration states what the last video does, which is shift both curves at once and ask what the economy does before anything has actually happened.
