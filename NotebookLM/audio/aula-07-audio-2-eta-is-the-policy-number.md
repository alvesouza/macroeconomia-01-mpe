Output language: English, conversational register. Keep the spoken output in English throughout, whatever language the uploaded sources are in.

Thesis, and the conversation serves only this. There is one number in this lecture that a central bank cannot observe, cannot vote on, and cannot avoid depending on: the income elasticity of money demand. Get it wrong and you miss your inflation target by a predictable amount, in a predictable direction, even with perfect control of the money stock.

Four segments. Do not add a fifth.

SEGMENT ONE. WHY A GROWING ECONOMY HAS LOWER INFLATION. Start with the result and make it feel surprising. Two countries print money at the same rate; the faster-growing one ends up with less inflation. Have a host resist that, then build the mechanism. A growing economy carries out more transactions, so it wants more real money. That appetite absorbs newly created money instead of letting it bid up prices. So inflation is money growth net of what growth soaks up. Give the formula in words once: inflation equals money growth minus the elasticity of money demand times output growth. Have the other host ask whether that elasticity is a detail of functional form or something that matters.

SEGMENT TWO. WHERE THE ELASTICITY COMES FROM. Now earn the number instead of asserting it. The trip-cost model says real balances move with the square root of output, because a household whose income doubles does not double its cash; it goes to the bank more often. There are economies of scale in managing cash, so the elasticity is one half. Contrast the older Cambridge formulation, where money demand is proportional to income and the elasticity is one. Have the hosts put the two side by side and notice this is not a quibble about parameters: one says cash management has scale economies, the other says it does not. Leave open which is right empirically.

SEGMENT THREE. THE COST OF BEING WRONG. This segment justifies the episode, so give it the most room, as a story rather than a calculation. A central bank wants inflation of two per cent in an economy growing at three per cent. Believing the elasticity is one half, it sets money growth at three and a half per cent, which is exactly right if the belief is right. Now suppose money demand is really proportional to income. The appetite for real balances grows faster than assumed, more new money is willingly absorbed, and inflation comes in at half a per cent. Have a host state the rule in words: the inflation error equals the error in the elasticity times the growth rate, with its sign set by which way you erred. Then have the hosts disagree about the conclusion, one against targeting monetary aggregates at all, the other for measuring money demand better, and let it stand.

SEGMENT FOUR. THE SAME LOGIC WHEN TECHNOLOGY MOVES THE DEMAND. Generalise, because this is the part that survives beyond the exercise. Output growth is not the only thing that shifts money demand. Suppose the cost of a trip to the bank falls steadily, as cash machines spread and banking moves onto a phone. In the trip-cost model that cost sits under the same square root as output, so it enters with the same elasticity. Cheaper trips mean people hold lower real balances, so money demand grows more slowly than output alone would suggest, so a fixed money growth rule is now too loose and inflation rises. Have a host state the unifying principle plainly: inflation is the gap between the growth of nominal money supplied and the growth of real money demanded, and anything that slows the second raises inflation. Close by connecting back to the doubt the sources raise about whether money demand is stable enough to build a rule on. Financial innovation is that instability, which is why this argument is about institutions and not only algebra.

Skip entirely: the costs of inflation, the Friedman rule, seigniorage and the inflation tax, the money multiplier and the banking system, and price index measurement. Do not cover the fixed-price case or anything about sticky prices, which belongs to later lectures. Naming any of that here would turn an argument into a summary.

One instruction that belongs to audio and not to slides: every number in this episode must arrive attached to a decision, never as part of a chain of arithmetic read aloud. Say the target, say the growth rate, say the chosen money growth, say what inflation turned out to be, and stop. Never dictate an algebraic manipulation; the derivation lives in the written material and the listener cannot follow symbols in speech.
