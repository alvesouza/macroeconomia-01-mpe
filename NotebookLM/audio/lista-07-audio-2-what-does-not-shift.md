Produce a two-host conversation about how to move the curves of the New-Keynesian AS-AD diagram without getting the answer wrong. Sources: "Livro_Benigno_2015_NewKeynesianEconomics_AS_AD_View.pdf" (= BENIGNO, sections four, five and seven, printed pages 507 to 514, figures one to five and nine) and "Lista_MPE_Macro1_2026_Lista7.pdf" (= LISTA 7, question two).

Thesis, and the conversation serves only this. Every AS-AD question is answered by the same two-step procedure, and the hard part is never the curve that shifts. It is the curve that does not, and the fact that the equilibrium then slides along it for a reason of its own. A temporary mark-up rise moves only supply; a cut in the nominal rate moves only demand. The answer lives in the movement along the curve that stayed put.

Four segments. Do not add a fifth.

SEGMENT ONE. THE ANCHOR RULE. Before any shock, give the listener the one plotting fact that makes the rest mechanical. The supply curve always passes through the point where the price level equals the pre-set price and output equals its natural level. So to shift supply, recompute the natural level and redraw the line through the new anchor with the same slope. Demand passes through that same point only because BENIGNO sets the interest rate at the natural rate to start, which is a policy assumption, not a property of the model. Have one host ask why that distinction matters, and let the other answer: it tells you which curve a change can possibly touch.

SEGMENT TWO. A TEMPORARY RISE IN THE DESIRED MARK-UP. Follow it as a chain of causes. Firms that can reset want a bigger margin over marginal cost, so the natural level falls and supply shifts up and to the left. Ask the hosts whether demand shifts too, and let one of them say yes before the other corrects it. The shock is temporary, so the long-run natural level is untouched, and the mark-up appears nowhere in the demand curve. The fall in demand is a movement along a fixed curve. The price level rises; with the long-run price level anchored, that lowers expected inflation and raises the real rate; households save more; output falls. Name the outcome as stagflation, and stress that output falls by less than the natural level did, because the firms with frozen prices keep serving customers. Then the fact students most often miss: output ends above the new natural level, which is why prices rise, and below the efficient level, which is why welfare falls.

SEGMENT THREE. A CUT IN THE NOMINAL RATE. Now the mirror case. The rate appears only in demand, so demand shifts up by the size of the cut at every output, and supply does not move, because the natural level and pre-set prices are beyond the central bank's reach. Trace the transmission. With future prices anchored, a lower nominal rate is a lower real rate, so households bring consumption forward. Firms with frozen prices serve the extra demand; hiring raises the wage households require, so the other firms raise prices, and rising prices push the real rate partly back up. That feedback is why output rises by only about two thirds of the demand shift under BENIGNO's calibration. Have a host ask whether this is the old liquidity effect, and answer no: there is no money market in the story at all.

SEGMENT FOUR. THE AUTOPSY OF FOUR WRONG ANSWERS. Have the hosts take turns proposing a mistaken answer to LISTA 7 question two and dismantling it. First, shifting demand after the temporary mark-up shock. Second, reporting a single output gap when there are two with opposite signs. Third, saying more rigid prices make supply steeper, when rigidity flattens it and sends more of a rate cut into output. Fourth, explaining the rate cut through the money supply. For each, name the sentence in BENIGNO that settles it. Close with the contrast the section leaves to the reader: a permanent mark-up rise would shift demand down as well, because households would expect to be poorer.

Skip entirely: the choice of the best interest-rate response and its welfare weights, the fiscal multipliers, and the zero lower bound. Stay at the level of the uploaded article; if a source goes further, register it in one sentence and move on.

One instruction that belongs to audio and not to slides: narrate each shock as a sequence of events, one cause per step, as if the diagram were being drawn in the listener's head. Before each result, have one host predict the direction aloud so the other can confirm or correct it. Never read an expression aloud.
