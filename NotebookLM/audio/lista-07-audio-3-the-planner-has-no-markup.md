Produce a two-host conversation about the difference between natural and efficient output, and why a rise in firms' desired mark-up moves only one of them. Sources: "Livro_Benigno_2015_NewKeynesianEconomics_AS_AD_View.pdf" (= BENIGNO, sections four point one to four point four, printed pages 507 to 509, and section seven, page 513) and "Lista_MPE_Macro1_2026_Lista7.pdf" (= LISTA 7, question three).

Thesis, and the conversation serves only this. The market and a benevolent planner solve the same labour-supply equation, except that the market has one extra term: the mark-up. Anything that appears in both problems moves natural and efficient output together. Anything that appears only in the market's problem moves only natural output. That is the whole of question three, and the whole reason monetary policy ever faces a trade-off.

Four segments. Do not add a fifth.

SEGMENT ONE. TWO DEFINITIONS THAT SOUND ALIKE. Natural output is what the decentralised economy produces when every firm can reset its price. It is still an economy with monopoly power and taxes. Efficient output is what a planner maximising household utility would choose, given only preferences, technology and public spending. Have one host propose that natural means optimal, and let the other take that apart: natural output is where prices are stable, not where welfare is highest. Add the point students miss: price stickiness appears in neither definition. Stickiness explains why actual output differs from the natural level; it does not explain why the natural level differs from the efficient one.

SEGMENT TWO. THE WEDGE, BUILT FROM THE LABOUR MARKET. This is the segment the episode exists for; give it the most room. The planner hires labour until the household's marginal rate of substitution between leisure and consumption equals what an hour produces. The market stops earlier. Each firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve and charges a mark-up over marginal cost, so the real wage it pays is labour's product divided by one plus the mark-up. The household works until its marginal rate of substitution equals that wage, not the product. So it works too little and the economy produces too little. Have the hosts restate this as a surplus left on the table: at natural output, one more unit would be worth more to society than it costs the household. The unrealised gain is a triangle whose area grows with the square of the distance between the two levels, which is why the welfare loss in BENIGNO's section eleven is quadratic in the efficient gap. Say that BENIGNO's equation thirteen folds taxes on labour, sales and consumption into the same wedge, so a tax rise and a rise in monopoly power are one shock with two names.

SEGMENT THREE. THE TEST OF WHAT APPEARS WHERE. Now apply the thesis as a sorting exercise. The hosts take a shock and ask whether it enters the planner's problem. Productivity does: it raises what labour produces for planner and market alike, so both levels rise by the same amount. Public spending does too. The mark-up does not: the planner's problem contains no prices, no market power and no taxes. So a mark-up rise lowers natural output and leaves efficient output where it was. Have one host insist that efficient output must fall because firms really are producing less; let the other explain that the firms producing less is exactly the fall in natural output, and it is a welfare loss precisely because the planner's target did not move. Explain that "temporary" only means the long-run natural level and demand stay put; efficient output would not move at any horizon.

SEGMENT FOUR. WHY THE DISTINCTION DECIDES POLICY. Close the loop to the rest of the course. Prices respond to the gap against natural output, because firms price off marginal cost. Welfare responds to the gap against efficient output. When a shock moves both levels together, closing one gap closes the other, and one interest rate does everything. When a mark-up shock splits them, no interest rate can close both, and none can move the natural level back at all. That requires removing the wedge through tax policy or competition policy. Have the hosts disagree about whose job that is. One says the central bank should lean hard against the output loss; the other says monetary policy should not chase a gap it cannot close.

Skip entirely: the log-linearisation steps, the fiscal multipliers, the liquidity trap, and the exact welfare weights of the central bank's loss. Stay at the level of the uploaded article; if a source goes further, register it in one sentence and move on.

Let the listener hear the gap between the two prices before hearing its name. Never read an expression aloud; say what it decides.
