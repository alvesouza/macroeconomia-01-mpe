Produce a two-host conversation about how a central bank chooses where to sit after a shock that creates a trade-off. Sources: "Livro_Benigno_2015_NewKeynesianEconomics_AS_AD_View.pdf" (= BENIGNO, section eleven, equations thirty-three to thirty-five and figure fourteen, printed pages 521 to 523) and "Lista_MPE_Macro1_2026_Lista7.pdf" (= LISTA 7, question four).

Thesis, and the conversation serves only this. Optimal monetary policy is a consumer's choice problem in disguise. The loss function plays the preferences, the supply curve is the budget line, and its slope is the relative price. The optimum is a tangency, which is why the central bank ends up splitting a mark-up shock between output and prices instead of absorbing it on one side. The supply curve is the constraint because it is the one relation the bank's single instrument cannot move.

Four segments. Do not add a fifth.

SEGMENT ONE. READING THE BOWL. LISTA 7 writes the loss as a weight on the squared output gap from a target plus a weight on the squared price surprise. Have the hosts read its shape before any algebra. It is zero at one bliss point, where output is at target and there is no price surprise. It is symmetric, so a boom above target is as bad as a recession below it. The central bank stabilises; it does not push output up. It is convex, so small misses are cheap and large ones expensive, and when the two targets conflict the bank spreads the miss across both. Then two readings that come from BENIGNO: only unexpected price movements are costly, because they create price dispersion between firms that adjusted and firms that did not. And in the welfare-based version the output target is the efficient level, not the natural one. The ratio of the two weights is the bank's conservatism.

SEGMENT TWO. WHY THE SUPPLY CURVE IS THE CONSTRAINT. This is the segment the episode exists for; give it the most room. Ask the question the list asks: why is this problem subject to the supply curve and not to demand? Let the answer come in steps. The bank has one instrument, the nominal rate, and it appears only in demand. Moving the rate slides demand along supply, so the bank can reach any point on the supply curve and only such points. Supply is private behaviour the bank cannot override within the period: the firms that adjust price off marginal cost, and the rest are stuck with prices set before the shock. Demand is not a constraint because it contains the free instrument: for any point on supply there is a rate that puts demand through it. Have one host insist that demand must constrain something; let the other show it only tells the bank which rate implements the point it already chose.

SEGMENT THREE. THE TANGENCY. Now the choice. At the optimum the lowest reachable ellipse of the loss just touches the supply line. The rate at which the bank is willing to trade output for price surprises then equals the rate at which supply lets it. That condition is the targeting rule, BENIGNO's inflation-targeting line. It never mentions the shock, which is why BENIGNO prefers it to an instrument rule tuned shock by shock. Work the mark-up shock under BENIGNO's welfare weights: only about one tenth of the shock is let into prices, and the rest is absorbed as lost output. Say what that means for the rate: the bank raises it, but by less than strict price stability would require. Then the edge case: if the shock moves natural and target output together, the supply line runs through the bliss point, the loss is zero, and there is nothing to trade.

SEGMENT FOUR. WHAT THE CONSTRAINT FORBIDS. Close on policy. The bank chooses a point on the supply line; it cannot move the line. So it cannot raise natural output toward the efficient level, which is a task for taxes and competition policy. The trade-off exists only because prices are sticky: with fully flexible prices the line is vertical and the bank chooses only the price level. It also exists only within the period. A bank that tried to hold output above its natural level every period would find the surprise built into the pre-set prices, the logic of Kydland and Prescott. Have the hosts disagree about the weights, one arguing as a hawk and one as a dove. Land on BENIGNO's point that a bank less concerned with prices would cut the rate after the same shock, so the sign of the response is a property of the mandate.

Skip entirely: the derivation of the welfare approximation, the fiscal multipliers, the liquidity trap, and any calibration beyond the one-tenth share. Stay at the level of the uploaded article; if a source goes further, register it in one sentence and move on.

Let the other host interrupt at each stage with the economic meaning. Never read an expression aloud; say what it decides.
