Produce a two-host conversation about the three questions an exam asks on growth evidence. Source: "Livro_Pablo_Kurlat_-_A_Course_in_Modern_Macroeconomics_2020_-_libgen.li.pdf" (= KURLAT: section 3.3 at printed page 51 with Exercise 3.3 on page 52; section 5.3 on convergence at pages 80 to 83; section 5.4 on growth accounting at pages 86 to 88; Exercises 5.3 and 5.4 on pages 94 and 95).

Thesis, and the conversation serves only this. Growth accounting is bookkeeping, not causation. It says how much of measured growth lines up with each measured input; it never says why the inputs grew, and evidence that agrees with the Solow model does not prove it. Nearly every mark lost on this material comes from a sentence that turns an attribution into a cause.

Four segments. Do not add a fifth.

SEGMENT ONE. THE SUBTRACTION, SET UP CORRECTLY. The first question type gives growth in output, capital and labour and asks for the residual. Walk KURLAT's logic: under competition each factor is paid its marginal product, so the factor shares read off the national accounts are exactly the weights the decomposition needs, and nobody estimates them by regression. Labour growth means hours, not heads: participation, unemployment and hours per worker all move. Then KURLAT's worked case, the Soviet Union from the late nineteen-twenties to the mid-sixties: fast growth, most of it from capital and labour, and a residual of about one point a year, close to the American rate. Have one host read that as proof that planning failed at technology; let the other say the accounting shows only that productivity growth was unremarkable.

SEGMENT TWO. ATTRIBUTION IS NOT CAUSE. This is the segment the episode exists for; give it the most room. KURLAT's Exercise 5.3 puts an economy on the Solow balanced growth path, with technological progress and no population growth, through growth accounting. Have the hosts work it before answering. Output and capital grow at the same rate, so the accounting hands capital accumulation a share of growth equal to the capital share, and technology the rest. Yet without technological progress the model has no long-run growth at all: capital grows only because technology does. The trap is to write that capital accumulation explains a third of growth, so saving more would raise long-run growth. The sentence that earns the mark: the accounting attributes growth to inputs, the model says what causes it, and here all of it is technology, with capital following. Then back to the Soviet case. KURLAT predicts a slowdown and asks whether that follows from the accounting alone. It does not: it needs diminishing returns from the model, plus the assumption that productivity growth stays mediocre.

SEGMENT THREE. CONVERGENCE: WHICH SAMPLE, WHICH CLAIM. The third type asks whether poor countries grow faster, as in Exercise 3.3. Give KURLAT's three answers. Across all countries, no: rich countries grow at similar rates while poor ones scatter, some catching up and some falling behind. Weighted by population, some convergence appears, driven largely by China and India. Within US states and Western Europe, strong convergence, though the US pattern faded in recent decades. Then the trap: reading the within-region result as proof of the model. KURLAT's own line settles it: finding evidence consistent with a conjecture is not proving it, and those economies may converge for other reasons, such as faster technological progress in the poorer ones. One sentence on Exercise 5.4: a capital stock built with the wrong depreciation rate is mismeasured, and the error lands in the residual.

SEGMENT FOUR. THE REHEARSAL. Have the hosts write three answers aloud, each in at most three sentences. The residual: what was subtracted, where the weights came from, and what the leftover contains. Exercise 5.3: the share the accounting gives capital, and why technology is still the whole cause. Convergence: the sample, what it shows, and why consistent is not proven. After each answer, the other host finds the one word in it that would turn attribution back into causation, and deletes it.

Skip entirely: the Golden Rule, the Kaldor facts, the speed-of-convergence algebra, and the calibrated prediction of income gaps from investment rates. Other episodes cover the residual as a measure of ignorance, the security-capital example and conditional convergence inside the Solow model; here each gets at most one sentence. Stay at the level of KURLAT; if it goes further, register it in one sentence and move on.

One instruction that belongs to audio and not to slides: before any conclusion, one host must say whether the sentence about to be spoken is an attribution or a cause, and the other must refuse a causal sentence unless a step of the model is named to support it. Never read an expression aloud; say what it decides.
