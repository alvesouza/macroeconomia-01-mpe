Output language: English, conversational register. Keep the spoken output in English throughout, whatever language the uploaded sources are in.

Thesis. Early in the course the students met a level of capital that maximises consumption forever, and were told an economy holding less is leaving something on the table. Now that saving is a choice rather than an assumption, the economy chooses less than that level, and the welfare theorem says the outcome cannot be improved upon. Both are true, and reconciling them is the episode: maximising long-run consumption was never the right objective.

Scope. Stay at the level of the uploaded lecture and KURLAT section 9.3, plus the Golden Rule material from the growth lectures. Do not re-prove the welfare theorem, which the companion episode handles, and do not describe the phase diagram in detail, since it cannot be seen. Do no algebra aloud.

Skip entirely: the anticipation experiment, uncertainty, and any discussion of population growth or technical progress in the steady state.

SEGMENT ONE. THE PROMISE THAT WAS MADE EARLIER. Open by reconstructing the Golden Rule as the students met it. In the growth model the saving rate was simply assumed, a number handed to the modeller, and one could ask which value of it produced the largest consumption in the long run. Have one host state the answer in words: the level of capital at which its marginal product just covers depreciation. Have the other recall the corollary, that an economy holding more than this is unambiguously wasteful, since cutting saving would raise consumption today and in every period after. Establish that this felt like a benchmark with normative force.

SEGMENT TWO. WHAT CHANGED. Explain the single modification this lecture makes: the saving rate is no longer assumed, it is chosen by a household that has preferences over the whole path of its consumption and discounts the future. Have one host stress how small this sounds and how much it changes. Nothing about the technology moved; only the origin of the saving rate is different.

SEGMENT THREE. THE ANSWER COMES BACK WRONG. Deliver the result. In the long run, the marginal product of capital does not equal depreciation. It equals depreciation plus a second term that comes entirely from impatience. Since the marginal product of capital falls as capital rises, that extra term means the economy settles at strictly less capital than the Golden Rule prescribes. Have one host state the apparent scandal plainly: the free-market outcome holds too little capital by the benchmark the course itself taught. Let the other host add the sharper version, that the previous episode proved this same outcome cannot be improved upon for anyone.

SEGMENT FOUR. WHY THERE IS NO CONTRADICTION. This is the core of the episode and deserves the most room. Have the hosts locate the error precisely: it is in the benchmark, not the result. The Golden Rule maximises consumption in the steady state, which is consumption in the infinitely distant future. Reaching it from below means consuming less now, for a long stretch, in exchange for more later, and a household that discounts the future answers no to that trade. Have one host insist the household is simply wrong, and let the other dismantle it: there is no external standard by which impatience is an error, the household's preferences are the only welfare criterion in the model, and a planner maximising those same preferences would choose the same thing. Land the reframing in one sentence: Pareto optimality is about the whole path, and maximising one point on it is a different objective nobody asked for.

SEGMENT FIVE. THE TWO WORDS TO CARRY. Close on the distinction between dynamic inefficiency and merely holding less capital than the Golden Rule. The first is a real failure, and it has a test: could consumption be raised in every period at once? The second passes that test and is simply a preference. Have the hosts note that any argument for forcing higher national saving must say which of the two situations it thinks it is in. Then point forward in one sentence to the money and inflation lectures, where a benchmark that looked normative again turns out to be one option among several. Give the listener one question to carry: if their country saves less than some published optimal rate, what would they need to know before calling that a problem?
