Produce a two-host conversation about the money questions an exam asks beyond the quantity theory. Source: "Livro_Kurlat_Cap10-11_Money_and_Inflation.pdf" (= KURLAT, printed pages: the money multiplier at 195 to 198, with Exercises 10.1 to 10.3 on pages 202 and 203; the Fisher equation at 208; seignorage at 215 to 217; Exercises 11.3, 11.4, 11.6 and 11.8 on pages 219 to 222).

Thesis, and the conversation serves only this. Every money question beyond the quantity theory turns on a choice the answer must not freeze. The money multiplier is two ratios chosen by banks and by the public; seigniorage is a tax whose base, real money balances, is chosen by the people being taxed; and a real interest rate is either what lenders expected or what they got. The mark goes to naming whose choice moves, and which way.

Four segments. Do not add a fifth.

SEGMENT ONE. THE MULTIPLIER IS TWO CHOICES. The question type: the monetary base is fixed and something happens; what happens to money? KURLAT's multiplier depends on the reserve ratio, which the central bank moves through the requirement, and on the share of money the public holds as cash, which it does not control. Work two exercises aloud. To shrink money without touching the base, raise the reserve requirement. When pickpockets multiply, people hold less cash and more deposits, so the multiplier and the money supply rise. Then the case that breaks the formula: once reserves pay interest and rates are near zero, banks hold excess reserves, and KURLAT reports the multiplier falling from about two to below one while the base rose almost fivefold. The trap is treating the multiplier as a constant the bank sets. The sentence: name the ratio, name who chose it, give the direction.

SEGMENT TWO. A TAX WHOSE BASE SHRINKS. This is the segment the episode exists for; give it the most room. KURLAT's government budget treats base money as debt that pays no interest, so printing it buys real resources. Ask who pays, and let one host say nobody; the other answers that everyone holding money pays, as its value falls, which is why seigniorage is also called the inflation tax. Then the limit. Faster money growth means higher inflation, a higher nominal rate and lower real money demand, so the base of the tax shrinks. Exercise 11.6 makes it exact with Cagan's money demand: revenue peaks when monthly money growth equals one over the demand parameter, a quarter a month in KURLAT's calibration, or well over a thousand per cent a year; beyond that, printing faster raises less. The trap is "more printing, more revenue". One sentence on Exercise 11.3: a growing economy collects seigniorage even at zero inflation, because the demand for money grows with it.

SEGMENT THREE. EXPECTED OR REALISED. KURLAT's Fisher equation makes the real rate the nominal rate minus inflation, an approximation that holds only when inflation is small, so it fails in exactly the hyperinflations of the previous segment. Exercise 11.8: under a nominal loan, the realised real rate turns out above the expected one. Have one host say the borrower gains because rates were high; let the other correct it: inflation came in below expectations, the repayment buys more goods than planned, and the lender gains. So lenders favour low and predictable inflation, and borrowers welcome inflation surprises. The trap is confusing the rate agreed with the rate realised. The sentence names the surprise, its sign, and whom it transfers wealth to.

SEGMENT FOUR. THE REHEARSAL. Have the hosts write three answers aloud, each in at most three sentences: pickpockets and the money supply, why seigniorage has a maximum, and who gains when the real rate surprises upward. Then one stretch item, Exercise 11.4: two economies with the same money growth this year, one holding it steady and one cutting it every year. Let the hosts argue which has lower inflation this year before agreeing it is the one cutting: there expected inflation keeps falling, so the nominal rate falls, real money demand keeps rising, and it absorbs part of the new money.

Skip entirely: the quantity equation as an identity, the income elasticity of money demand and the inflation-target arithmetic, the derivation of money demand from trips to the bank, the shoe-leather cost and the Friedman rule, and neutrality. Other episodes cover each; here none gets more than one sentence. Stay at the level of KURLAT; if it goes further, register it in one sentence and move on.

One instruction that belongs to audio and not to slides: narrate each question as a ledger of who holds what. Before any answer, one host names the agent whose choice moves, the public, the banks, the money holders or the lender, and the other refuses any answer that leaves that agent's choice frozen. Never read an expression aloud; say what it decides.
