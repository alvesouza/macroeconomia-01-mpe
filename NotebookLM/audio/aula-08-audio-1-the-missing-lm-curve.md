Produce a two-host conversation about one substitution in the history of macroeconomic teaching. Sources: "Livro_Benigno_2015_NewKeynesianEconomics_AS_AD_View.pdf" (= BENIGNO, sections one to five, printed pages 505 to 511) and "Livro_Kurlat_Cap10-11_Money_and_Inflation.pdf" (= KURLAT, the classical dichotomy).

Thesis, and the conversation serves only this. Two aggregate demand curves slope downward in the same diagram for entirely different reasons, and the difference is not a technicality: one runs through a money market that modern central banks do not operate, the other through a household deciding when to consume. Replace the first with the second and the diagram survives while the story underneath it changes completely.

Four segments. Do not add a fifth.

SEGMENT ONE. A CURVE THAT SLOPES THE RIGHT WAY FOR THE WRONG REASON. Let the first host give the textbook account with confidence: a higher price level raises money demand, the money stock is fixed, so interest rates rise, investment falls, output falls. Have the other host ask one question and let it land — where in this story is the money stock set, and by whom? Bring out that the account needs a central bank controlling an aggregate, while the banks the listener reads about announce an interest rate instead. Then let the first host resist: the curve still slopes down, so does the mechanism matter? Leave that standing; the rest of the episode answers it.

SEGMENT TWO. THE HOUSEHOLD, NOT THE MONEY MARKET. Rebuild the curve from the other end. A household chooses consumption now against later under one lifetime budget constraint, and the price of postponing is the real interest rate. Give the Euler equation in words once: consumption growth between the two periods rises with the real rate net of impatience. Then walk the slope with the nominal rate held fixed by the bank. A higher price level today, with the future level given, means less inflation between now and then, a higher real rate at an unchanged nominal rate, and so more saving and less consumption today. Have the hosts notice what never appeared in that chain: any money at all. Close on the consequence: the position of the curve is now a policy choice.

SEGMENT THREE. THE THING THE OLD CURVE CANNOT DO. This segment earns the episode, so give it the most room, and give it as a scenario rather than an equation. Nothing happens today. Firms are as productive as they were, nobody's taxes change, the central bank does not move. All that changes is that households come to believe the economy will be richer several years from now. Ask the first host what the textbook curve does. It cannot do anything, because nothing in it refers to the future. Then ask what the household-based curve does: to smooth consumption against a richer future, people spend more today, demand shifts out, and prices rise. Let the hosts sit with a boom caused by a forecast, with no shock to anything present-tense. Then have them disagree about the policy that follows. One argues the bank should lean against a belief, tightening into an economy where nothing observable has gone wrong; the other argues that acting on beliefs is how a bank loses its footing. Do not resolve it.

SEGMENT FOUR. WHAT THE OTHER CURVE GAINED, AND WHAT IT GAVE UP. Turn to the supply side for one point: firms that can reset charge a mark-up over marginal cost, marginal cost rises with output, so the price level responds to the gap between output and what the economy would produce with fully flexible prices. Then the observation behind the title: because a fraction of firms cannot move their price at all, the curve is flatter the more rigid the economy is — rigidity makes quantities adjust while prices hold still. Have a host state that direction out loud and the other check it, since the intuition runs backwards on first hearing. Finish on the accounting. This supply curve is what textbooks already teach as the New-Classical Phillips curve, in the price level, with no forward-looking term, and the author says so. The model gains a demand side that is a decision; it gives up any account of inflation as a process. Then the bridge: policy analysis turns on one subtraction, between the output a market with monopoly power produces and the output a planner would want.

Skip entirely: the fiscal multipliers, the zero lower bound, debt deleveraging, the optimal policy line, and the mark-up shock analysis. Do not cover the tax algebra of the aggregate mark-up beyond the single remark that taxes and monopoly power are indistinguishable in it. Naming any of that here would turn one argument into a survey.

One instruction that belongs to audio and not to slides: no algebraic manipulation is to be read aloud. Each equation appears once, in words, as a sentence a listener could write down, and never as a sequence of substitutions. Where the written material has a derivation, say what the derivation establishes and move on.
