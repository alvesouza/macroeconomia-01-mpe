Produce a two-host conversation about what a labour-income tax does to hours worked. Sources: "Livro_Pablo_Kurlat_-_A_Course_in_Modern_Macroeconomics_2020_-_libgen.li.pdf" (= KURLAT, section 7.2, printed pages 131 to 137), "Aula_Slides_Macro1_Aula5.pdf" (= AULA5, the section on income and substitution effects and on adding taxes and transfers) and "Lista_MPE_Macro1_2026_Lista4.pdf" (= LISTA 4, question one).

Thesis, and the conversation serves only this. With log utility in consumption, the income and substitution effects of a wage change cancel exactly, so a proportional tax on wages, which is only a lower wage, cannot move hours on its own. It moves hours through one channel: the lump-sum transfer. The transfer is income that does not depend on working, and a higher tax makes that same transfer larger relative to what an hour of work now pays. The answer is a statement about hours, with its sign and its condition.

Four segments. Do not add a fifth.

SEGMENT ONE. THE WAGE IS THE PRICE OF LEISURE. Build KURLAT's static household: a time endowment split between work and leisure, and every hour of leisure costs the wage it could have earned. A higher wage then does two things. Leisure is dearer, so the household substitutes toward work. The household is richer, so it buys more of everything, leisure included, and works less. Have one host ask which effect wins and the other refuse to answer until the preferences are named. With log consumption and no income other than wages, consumption moves one for one with the wage, the value of an extra unit of income falls exactly as fast as the wage rises, and the two effects cancel. Close with KURLAT's reason this knife edge is not a curiosity: wages multiplied over a century while hours barely moved, and balanced growth requires this cancellation.

SEGMENT TWO. A TAX IS JUST A LOWER WAGE. Now add the proportional tax. From the household's side it changes nothing except the wage it keeps. So if there were no transfer, segment one already gives the answer: hours do not respond to the tax at all. Have one host object that everyone knows taxes discourage work. Let the other take the objection apart: that is the substitution half alone, and the income half pushes the other way by exactly the same amount. Ask the hosts to run the knife-edge test on the first-order condition in LISTA 4: set the transfer to zero and watch the tax rate drop out of it entirely. Then one sentence of bridge: AULA5 asks whether taxes explain why Europeans work fewer hours than Americans, and KURLAT's version of that calculation works only because the tax revenue comes back to households as a transfer, which is this episode's channel.

SEGMENT THREE. THE TRANSFER DOES ALL THE WORK. This is the segment the episode exists for; give it the most room. The transfer raises wealth without changing the price of leisure, so it is a pure income effect, and a pure income effect means more leisure and fewer hours. Raising the tax lowers the net wage, so the same transfer now equals more hours of work. The pure income effect grows while the wage effects still cancel. Direction: leisure rises, hours fall, and only because the transfer is positive. Draw it in words: the budget line pivots around the point where the household works nothing and consumes the transfer; a higher tax flattens it around that pivot. Then the autopsy of a real graded answer. The formula was right, with the transfer sitting in the numerator of the derivative, and the sentence beneath it said the tax makes leisure cheaper and so reduces consumption. That is the substitution effect alone, and it contradicts the formula written above it. The grader marked it with a question mark.

SEGMENT FOUR. THE ANSWER IN HOURS. Close by rehearsing, in the hosts' own words, the answer that earns the mark on LISTA 4 question one, part b. It has three parts. The object: hours worked, and say it both ways, leisure up and hours down, so the grader never has to translate. The sign with its condition: hours fall when the tax rises if the transfer is positive, and do not move if it is zero. The mechanism as one causal chain: higher tax, lower net wage, the wage's two effects cancel, the fixed transfer is now worth more relative to earnings, pure income effect, more leisure, fewer hours. Have one host give the student's answer and the other rewrite it, clause by clause, until it matches the formula.

Skip entirely: the elasticities and their ordering, the dynamic labour supply over time, the participation decision, and the search model of unemployment. Stay at the level of the uploaded lecture; if a source goes further, register it in one sentence and move on.
