This is a two-host conversation, not a chapter summary. It develops one argument from beginning to end. It is audio 1 of 2 for this lecture; the second takes up the comparison with the Golden Rule and the dynamics, so leave both alone here.

Sources. "Aula_Slides_Macro1_Aula6.pdf" (= AULA6) and sections 9.1 and 9.2 of "Livro_Pablo_Kurlat_-_A_Course_in_Modern_Macroeconomics_2020_-_libgen.li.pdf" (= KURLAT). For the failure case that the students have already worked through by hand, refer to the credit-constraint question in "Lista_MPE_Macro1_2026_Lista3.pdf".

Output language: English, conversational register. Keep the spoken output in English throughout, whatever language the uploaded sources are in.

Thesis. Nobody in this economy is trying to make it work. A household maximises its own utility, firms maximise their own profits, and no one computes any aggregate. Yet the result is exactly what an all-knowing, benevolent planner would have chosen. That is not luck and not ideology; it is a theorem, and the point of learning its proof is to learn which assumptions carry it.

Scope. Stay at the level of the uploaded lecture and KURLAT sections 9.1 and 9.2, in the two-period economy. The phase diagram, the Golden Rule comparison and the infinite-horizon model belong to the companion episode. Do no algebra aloud; describe what each condition says in words.

Skip entirely: the second welfare theorem, anything about redistribution mechanisms, and any discussion of whether markets are morally good.

SEGMENT ONE. THREE STRANGERS WHO NEVER MEET. Open with the setup in plain terms. A household deciding how much to consume, how much to save and how much to work. A firm hiring labour and renting capital. And a third agent that has not appeared before in the course, whose entire business is to buy capital today and rent it out tomorrow. Have one host describe what each knows: its own preferences or technology, and a list of prices. Have the other press the point that none of them knows what the others are doing, and nobody knows the aggregate resource constraint. Establish that this is a real coordination problem with no obvious solution.

SEGMENT TWO. THE THING THAT DISAPPEARS. Explain what happens when the individual conditions are combined. Every price cancels. What is left is two statements, and neither mentions a price: the household's willingness to give up leisure for consumption matches what an extra hour of work actually produces, and its willingness to postpone consumption matches what an extra unit of capital actually yields. Have one host notice how strange this is, that quantities describing physical possibilities emerged from an exercise in which everyone only ever looked at prices. Let the other name what prices were doing: carrying information about the technology to people who never saw it.

SEGMENT THREE. THE IMAGINARY DICTATOR. Introduce the planner as a thought experiment, and be careful about what it is: benevolent, omniscient, able to command any allocation, and facing no budget line, only what the economy can physically make. Have the hosts state what Pareto optimality means, that no one can be made better off without someone else being made worse off, and have one raise the objection that matters: this says nothing about fairness. Let the other agree without hedging, and note that efficiency and equity are separate questions the theorem does not mix.

SEGMENT FOUR. THE PROOF, AS A STORY. This is the core of the episode and deserves the most room. Walk the contradiction as a narrative, not as algebra. Suppose someone claims to have found an allocation feasible for the planner and strictly better for the household. Ask what must be true of it. First, the household was choosing freely and did not choose it, so it was unaffordable at the going prices. Second, the firms were already maximising, so they earned at least as much as they would have producing those alternative quantities. Have one host put the two together and follow where they lead: the alleged allocation demands more than the economy can produce, so it was never feasible and the claim collapses. Let the other restate the punchline in one sentence, that a competitive equilibrium cannot be improved upon because prices already encoded the constraint the planner would have faced.

SEGMENT FIVE. THE LIST IS THE PAYOFF. Close on why this matters for everything that follows. Give the five conditions under which the result fails: monopoly power, externalities, asymmetric information, incomplete markets, and borrowing constraints. Have one host take the last one and connect it to the credit-constraint problem the students already solved by hand. Then state the working rule: when evaluating a policy, the first question is not whether it is good, but which item on that list is present. Give the listener one question to carry: pick any government intervention and name which of the five it answers.
