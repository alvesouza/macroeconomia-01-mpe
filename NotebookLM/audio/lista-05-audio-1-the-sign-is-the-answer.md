Thesis. In this course an unsigned derivative is not an answer. The last item of question one produces an expression that can come out positive or negative, and the whole payoff of the question is isolating the single term that decides which. Once that term is on the table the economics is a sentence, not a calculation.

Scope. Stay at the level of AULA6 and KURLAT9 section 9.1, in the two-period economy with capital held fixed. Do no algebra aloud; describe in words what each expression says.

Skip entirely: the infinite-horizon economy, the comparison with the Golden Rule, and the welfare theorem.

SEGMENT ONE. THE HABIT THE COURSE IS TRAINING. Open away from the problem set, on what a finished answer looks like in this course: a derivative that has been signed, with the deciding factor pulled out and marked, and the interpretation arriving only afterwards. Have one host argue that interpretation is the hard part and the sign is bookkeeping. Let the other reverse it — the sign is where the marks are, because confidently interpreting a derivative nobody has signed is the most reliable way to lose them. Establish the order, sign first and interpret second, and say that the rest of the episode runs that order on a single expression.

SEGMENT TWO. THE ONE TERM THAT DECIDES. Describe the result of the last item without reciting it. Productivity rises, hours respond, and the response is a ratio: a denominator built from strictly positive pieces, and a numerator that is one minus the curvature of the utility function. Have one host draw the immediate consequence, that the denominator can be ignored completely for the purpose of the sign, so the question collapses to whether that one parameter sits above or below one. Have the other name the three regimes in words: hours rise, hours do not move, hours fall.

SEGMENT THREE. THE TWO FORCES BEHIND THE SIGN. This is the core of the episode and deserves the most room. Higher productivity raises the marginal product of labour and therefore the wage. Have the hosts develop the two consequences separately and name them. Leisure has become more expensive, which pushes the household toward working more. And the household is richer at any given number of hours, and leisure is a normal good, which pushes it toward working less. Then have one host ask why a parameter describing preferences should be the thing that arbitrates, and let the other answer through how fast the marginal utility of consumption falls: if it collapses quickly, the extra consumption a higher wage would buy is not worth much, so the household takes its gain as time instead.

SEGMENT FOUR. THE AUTOPSY. Have one host present a student answer that computes the response correctly, finds that hours fall, and concludes that wages and consumption fall with them. Have the other take it apart: productivity enters those variables directly as well as through hours, and the direct channel wins for every value of the parameter, so all three rise whatever the sign of the hours response turned out to be. Then name a second and smaller error, answering in leisure when the question asked for hours. Close the segment on the notational precaution that protects the whole answer: the letter in question is the curvature of the utility function, and its reciprocal is the elasticity of intertemporal substitution. Stating both once makes the answer safe under either convention.

SEGMENT FIVE. THE KNIFE EDGE, SEEN TWICE. Close on the case where the parameter equals one. Have the hosts establish that the two forces cancel exactly, that hours do not move at all, and that this is not a coincidence but the reason logarithmic utility in consumption is the standard assumption in growth models that want hours stationary in the long run. Have one host connect it back to the earlier problem set on labour supply, where under that same assumption hours were independent of the wage and of the tax, and the entire effect had to travel through the lump-sum transfer. Same cancellation, second sighting. Give the listener one question to carry: for a reform that raises take-home wages, what would they need to know about a country before predicting that hours go up?
