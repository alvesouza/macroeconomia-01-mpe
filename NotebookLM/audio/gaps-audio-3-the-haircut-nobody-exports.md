Produce a two-host conversation about why comparing output across countries and across years is a question of whose prices you use. Sources: "Livro_Pablo_Kurlat_-_A_Course_in_Modern_Macroeconomics_2020_-_libgen.li.pdf" (= KURLAT, section 1.2, printed pages 22 to 27), "Aula_MPE_Macro1_2026_Slides_1.pdf" (= SLIDES 1, the sections on nominal and real GDP and on comparing across countries and PPP) and "Lista_MPE_Macro1_2026_Lista1.pdf" (= LISTA 1, question one).

Thesis, and the conversation serves only this. A market exchange rate is the price of traded goods, and converting GDP with it values everything a country makes as if it were traded. In a poor country the part nobody can trade, the haircut, the rent, the bus ride, is cheap, because labour is cheap and no one can ship a haircut across a border to arbitrage it away. So market rates understate what poor countries produce, always in the same direction. The same question, whose prices, also decides which way a fixed-base growth rate is biased.

Four segments. Do not add a fifth.

SEGMENT ONE. TWO WAYS TO CONVERT. KURLAT's problem: two countries' outputs are in different currencies. One fix converts at the market exchange rate. The other values both countries' quantities at the same set of prices, which defines the purchasing-power-parity rate. In the LISTA 1 example the two answers differ by a factor of more than seven. Have one host ask which number is wrong; let the other say neither is an error, the market-rate number answers a different question, what the output would buy abroad, not how much was produced.

SEGMENT TWO. WHY POOR COUNTRIES ARE CHEAP. This is the segment the episode exists for; give it the most room. Build it in three steps. Traded goods sell at roughly one price everywhere, because arbitrage forces it. Rich countries are far more productive in traded goods, and wages follow that productivity. A haircut takes about as long in a poor country as in a rich one, so its price follows the local wage, and the local wage is low. So non-traded goods are cheap exactly where incomes are low, and the gap grows with the income gap. That regularity has a name, Balassa and Samuelson, and KURLAT's footnote on the Big Mac index points at it: the burger's price includes the restaurant's rent and wages. Have one host test the claim: if this is right, the price level should rise with income per head. The other confirms that is what the data show.

SEGMENT THREE. WHY TRADE COSTS ARE THE WRONG ANSWER. The autopsy of a graded answer that explained the gap by import and export costs. Let one host defend it: trade costs exist and do make prices differ across borders. Let the other take it apart in two moves. Trade costs are a friction on traded goods, which are precisely where the market rate works best. And trade costs push prices apart in either direction, with no reason to make poor countries cheap every time. A systematic direction tied to income needs a systematic cause, and that cause is wages and non-traded goods. The verdict: the right answer names what is not traded.

SEGMENT FOUR. THE SAME QUESTION ACROSS YEARS, AND THE ANSWERS. In the LISTA 1 example one good got cheaper, the other dearer, and buyers shifted toward the one that got cheaper. Valuing the new quantities at the old prices weights heavily the good that grew, at its old high price, so it overstates growth. Valuing the old quantities at the new prices understates it. That is substitution bias, and it is why statistical agencies chain their indices; one sentence is enough, another episode covers index numbers in depth. Then rehearse, in the hosts' own words, the two answers that earn the marks on question one: for part c, which base overstates, which understates, and why buyers' substitution causes it; for part e, the market rate misprices the non-traded share of output, which is cheap where wages are low, so the parity figure is the larger one. Have one host give the answer that says the base year is only a convention, and the other say why that sentence earns nothing.

Skip entirely: the three approaches to GDP accounting, the Human Development Index and welfare measures, and the algebra of the two-sector model. Stay at the level of the uploaded lecture; if a source goes further, register it in one sentence and move on.

One instruction that belongs to audio and not to slides: have the hosts price one ordinary day, a haircut, lunch, a bus fare, a phone, in a rich and a poor country, and sort each item aloud into traded or not before drawing any conclusion. Never read an expression aloud; say what it decides.
