Produce a two-host conversation about when a central bank with one instrument can hit two targets, and when it cannot. Source: "Livro_Benigno_2015_NewKeynesianEconomics_AS_AD_View.pdf" (= BENIGNO, sections six, seven and eleven, printed pages 511 to 523).

Thesis, and the conversation serves only this. Whether monetary policy faces a trade-off is not a matter of how bad the shock is or of how the committee votes. It is settled by one question about the shock: does it move the level of output a planner would want, or only the level the market delivers? Everything else follows, including the sign of the right response.

Four segments. Do not add a fifth.

SEGMENT ONE. TWO DIFFERENT NUMBERS THAT ARE BOTH CALLED POTENTIAL OUTPUT. Open with the distinction, without which the rest is unintelligible. The natural level is what the economy produces when every firm can reset its price — a market outcome, monopoly power and taxes included. The efficient level is what a planner maximising household utility would choose, with no wedge at all. Have the first host ask why anyone needs both, and the second answer with the two roles: prices respond to the gap against the natural level, since that is what firms price off, while welfare responds to the gap against the efficient level. Then the verdict in one sentence: the two levels differ by the mark-up alone. Productivity and public spending move them together; monopoly power and distorting taxes move only one of them.

SEGMENT TWO. THE SHOCK WHERE ONE RATE DOES EVERYTHING. Take a temporary productivity gain and follow it. Supply improves, firms that can reset cut prices, the real rate falls, consumption picks up. Ask whether the economy is overheating and let the answer surprise: no — output rose by less than capacity did, because most prices are stuck, so the gap is negative while output is rising. Have a host note that good news for output was not good news for the gap. Then the policy: cut the rate and both objectives are met at once. Have the other host push on whether that was luck, and give the reason it was not. Because productivity moved the natural and the efficient level by the same amount, the two targets were never in conflict, and the single instrument was never overloaded.

SEGMENT THREE. THE SAME SHOCK, THREE DIFFERENT ANSWERS. This is the segment the episode exists for, so give it the most room, and structure it as a puzzle the hosts solve rather than a list. Three productivity stories: a gain that arrives now and fades, a gain that arrives now and lasts, and a gain that is merely expected for later. Ask the hosts to predict the right policy before working each one, and let one of them get it wrong. Temporary: cut. Permanent: do nothing at all, because demand shifts out by exactly the amount that keeps the gap closed on its own. Merely expected: tighten, into an economy where nothing has yet happened. Have them extract the principle: what the bank tracks is not the level of technology but the difference between what the economy can produce later and now, because that difference sets the real rate consistent with a closed gap. Then the moral, from whichever host was wrong — good supply news means easier policy is wrong in two cases of three.

SEGMENT FOUR. THE SHOCK THAT BREAKS IT. Now oil, or a rise in monopoly power, or a payroll tax increase, which in this model are one shock with three names. The natural level falls; the efficient level does not move. Output falls and prices rise together — the hosts should name the situation before its label arrives. Then the part worth slowing down for: output is now above the natural level, which is why prices are climbing, and below the efficient level, which is why welfare has fallen. The two gaps have opposite signs at the same moment. Have the hosts work out what that does to policy. To hold prices, raise the rate and push output further below what a planner wanted. To reach the efficient level, cut the rate and accept a larger price rise. Opposite directions, same shock. Have them disagree — one holding that the price objective should dominate because the welfare weights say so, the other that this is where a mandate rather than a model decides — then close by naming the third line the author adds to the diagram, along which the trade-off is split at the right rate.

Skip entirely: the fiscal multiplier tables, the zero lower bound, debt deleveraging, and the derivation of either curve. Do not cover the Taylor rule beyond one remark that its coefficients would have to be re-chosen for every kind of shock, which is the reason the author prefers a targeting rule.

One instruction that belongs to audio and not to slides: build one shock at a time as a sequence of events with a cause at each step, and never read an expression aloud. Where the written material derives a coefficient, say what it decides — how much of the shock reaches prices, and how much reaches output — and leave the expression on the page.
