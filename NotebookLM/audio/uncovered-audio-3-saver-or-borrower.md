Produce a two-host conversation about the questions an exam asks on consumption once the Euler equation is known. Source: "Livro_Pablo_Kurlat_-_A_Course_in_Modern_Macroeconomics_2020_-_libgen.li.pdf" (= KURLAT: section 6.2 at printed pages 109 to 113; Exercises 6.2, 6.3, 6.4 and 6.8 on pages 122 to 125).

Thesis, and the conversation serves only this. Every consumption question is answered by two facts about the household before the shock: how much the shock changes its lifetime wealth, and whether it was saving, borrowing, or doing neither. Write those two facts first and the sign and size of the response follow; skip them and the answer is a guess.

Four segments. Do not add a fifth.

SEGMENT ONE. HOW MUCH OF A WINDFALL IS SPENT. The question: what is the marginal propensity to consume? KURLAT's answer is that it depends on whether the change is temporary or permanent. In two periods a one-off bonus is spread over both, so the response is below one; a permanent raise is consumed one for one when patience exactly offsets the interest rate. Exercise 6.8 gives the same condition to an infinitely lived household and a purely temporary gain. Have one host guess half; let the other work it in words: the household spreads the windfall over every future period, so it saves almost all of it and consumes only the interest, forever. At a four per cent interest rate that is under four cents per extra dollar. The trap is quoting one propensity for every shock. The sentence: the propensity rises with the persistence of the shock, because only a lasting change moves lifetime wealth by much.

SEGMENT TWO. SAVER OR BORROWER. The question: the interest rate rises; what happens to first-period consumption? KURLAT splits it. The substitution effect always lowers it, since the present got dearer. The income effect depends on position: a saver earns more and is richer, a borrower pays more and is poorer. So for a borrower both effects push consumption down and the answer is unambiguous; only for a saver do they fight, and then the curvature of utility decides. Exercise 6.2 makes it concrete: professional athletes at twenty-six, earning at their peak, save a large share, while first-year medical residents borrow against high future pay. When rates fall, the residents' saving falls for sure; the athletes' could go either way. The trap is answering "it depends on the curvature" for a borrower. Another episode drills the saver's knife edge; one sentence on it is enough.

SEGMENT THREE. THE HOUSEHOLD THAT NEITHER SAVES NOR BORROWS. This is the segment the episode exists for; give it the most room. In Exercise 6.3 the household's best plan happens to be consuming exactly its income in each period, and then rates rise. Have the hosts predict before working it. With no saving, the income effect vanishes at the margin, only substitution remains, and first-period consumption falls. Utility rises: the new budget line still passes through the income point, so the old plan is still affordable, and the household chose to move. Then the twist that ties the course together: a Jones and Klenow welfare measure built from this year's consumption would record a fall. Let one host call that a contradiction; let the other explain that the measure looks at one year, while the household traded some of this year for more of next year and is better off over the two. The sentence that earns the mark names all three: consumption today down, lifetime utility up, and a one-year measure that cannot see the trade.

SEGMENT FOUR. THE REHEARSAL. Open with Exercise 6.4 as a warm-up: two households with the same future income and different current incomes, with patience exactly offsetting a zero interest rate. With first-period data alone, consumption rises with income by less than one for one and looks Keynesian; add the second period, when both earn the same and still consume differently, and the Keynesian function fails. Then the hosts write three answers aloud, each in at most three sentences: the propensity out of a temporary gain for a long-lived household, the borrower facing a higher rate, and the household at its income point. After each, the other host checks that the answer named the household's position before giving a sign.

Skip entirely: Ricardian equivalence and the borrowing limit, the tax on savings, the saver's knife edge with no future income, the derivation of the Euler equation, and the consumption puzzle told as history. Other episodes cover each; here none gets more than one sentence. One sentence is allowed on KURLAT's behavioural section: default options in retirement plans move saving a lot, which a fully rational household would ignore. Stay at the level of KURLAT; if it goes further, register it in one sentence and move on.

One instruction that belongs to audio and not to slides: before any sign is stated, one host must announce the household's position, saver, borrower or neither, and how much its lifetime wealth moved; the other must refuse to hear the sign until both are on air. Never read an expression aloud; say what it decides.
