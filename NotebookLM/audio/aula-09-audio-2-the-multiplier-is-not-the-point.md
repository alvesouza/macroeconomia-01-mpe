Produce a two-host conversation about a number that is quoted in every fiscal debate and answers the wrong question. Source: "Livro_Benigno_2015_NewKeynesianEconomics_AS_AD_View.pdf" (= BENIGNO, sections eight, nine and ten, printed pages 514 to 521).

Thesis, and the conversation serves only this. The multiplier on output and the multiplier on the output gap are different numbers, they differ by roughly an order of magnitude, and for one common instrument they differ in sign. A policy can be a stimulus and a mistake at once, and the regime decides which multiplier is worth computing at all.

Five segments. Do not add a sixth.

SEGMENT ONE. A STIMULUS THAT SHRINKS. Start with the result. A rise in public spending worth one per cent of output raises output by less than that, under every calibration the author tries. Have a host object with the Keynesian intuition about rounds of spending, and answer with the two leaks: prices rise with demand, the real rate rises with them, and private consumption is crowded out. Then the subtler leak: public spending absorbs resources, so households work more and supply capacity rises too. Both curves move, and part of the stimulus is answered by supply rather than absorbed as slack.

SEGMENT TWO. THE GAP IS NOT THE OUTPUT. Now the substitution behind the title. Ask what stabilisation policy is for: not to raise output as such, but to close the distance between output and the level the economy should be producing. Since spending raises both, the effect on that distance is far smaller — the author puts it near a tenth of a point per one-point rise in the spending ratio. Have a host say it out loud: the number that matters is a fraction of the number that gets quoted. Then the awkward question — why does anyone quote the first one? Answer that output is measured while the gap is inferred, and leave the tension there.

SEGMENT THREE. THE INSTRUMENT THAT GOES BOTH WAYS. Give this segment room; it is where the argument becomes uncomfortable. Take a cut in the consumption tax. It raises output, because buying today becomes cheap relative to buying later. And it widens the gap, because capacity moves the other way. Have the hosts put both facts side by side and argue about which a finance ministry should be judged on: output, which employment tracks, or the gap, which welfare and inflation track. Then the mirror case: higher sales and payroll taxes cut output and yet narrow the gap. Close on the lesson — permanent fiscal policy cannot move the gap at all, because only the difference between now and later ever reaches it.

SEGMENT FOUR. WHEN THE SAME ALGEBRA GIVES A DIFFERENT ANSWER. Now change the regime. Some households borrowed heavily and have just discovered they must reduce their debt. They cut consumption whatever the interest rate, demand collapses, and the bank cuts to zero without reaching the rate that would restore full employment. Ask what the multiplier is now, and give it as a contrast: above one, where in normal times it could never pass one, and larger still when the fall in prices is not expected to be reversed. Have the hosts find the reason — the rate cannot rise to crowd anyone out, and the households receiving the spending cannot smooth it, so they spend all of it. Both leaks from segment one are plugged. Then a warning they raise themselves: the formula grows with the debt stock and explodes exactly where the stability condition fails, so the largest numbers are the least trustworthy.

SEGMENT FIVE. FALLING PRICES AS A CONTRACTION. Finish with the result that reverses the course's intuition. With nominal debts on the books, a lower price level raises what borrowers really owe, cutting their consumption and output — so the demand curve can slope upward. Have a host walk one consequence out loud: a productivity gain, or a tax cut that encourages work, shifts supply down, pushes prices down, and deepens the slump. Have the other resist that good supply news cannot be bad, then give the footnote that resolves it: this happens only when the future price level is allowed to follow the present one down. Anchor the long-run price level credibly and the paradoxes disappear. End on the policy reading — in a balance-sheet recession the strongest instrument is the price level promised for the future, and the fiscal instruments that work best act on the future too.

Skip entirely: the derivation of either curve, the productivity and mark-up shock analysis, and the optimal targeting rule. Do not cover the Bernanke list of unconventional instruments; naming them here would turn an argument into an inventory.

One instruction that belongs to audio and not to slides: every number in this episode arrives attached to a decision and never as part of a chain of arithmetic read aloud. Name the multiplier, name what it is a multiplier of, and stop. Never dictate a formula; the algebra lives in the written material and a listener cannot follow symbols in speech.
