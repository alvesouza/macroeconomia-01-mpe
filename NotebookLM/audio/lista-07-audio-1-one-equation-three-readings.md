Produce a two-host conversation about why the New-Keynesian model has exactly the ingredients it has. Sources: "Livro_Benigno_2015_NewKeynesianEconomics_AS_AD_View.pdf" (= BENIGNO, sections one to four and eleven, printed pages 503 to 509 and 521 to 522), "Lista_MPE_Macro1_2026_Lista7.pdf" (= LISTA 7, question one), and "Livro_The_Mcgraw-Hill_Series_in_Economics_David_Romer-Advanced_Macroeconomics_4th_edition-Busine.pdf" (= ROMER, chapter 6, for contrast only).

Thesis, and the conversation serves only this. The New-Keynesian model is the New Classical method with the Keynesian friction put back, and every one of its four distinctive choices answers a specific failure of one of its two predecessors. The proof is that its supply curve is, letter for letter, the one the New Classicals wrote down; only the reading of one symbol changed.

Four segments. Do not add a fifth.

SEGMENT ONE. THE CURVE THAT STOPPED WORKING. Open with the 1970s: inflation and unemployment rising together, and the original Keynesian menu of more inflation for less unemployment failing in the data. Have one host state the New Classical diagnosis: expectations adjust, so only surprises move output, and a systematic policy is built into expectations and loses its power. Then the counter-fact the other host raises: monetary tightening that everyone saw coming still produced deep, long recessions, far longer than anyone could stay fooled when price data are published every month. End the segment with the question the rest of the episode answers: how can policy matter without anyone being fooled?

SEGMENT TWO. ONE EQUATION, THREE READINGS. This is the segment the episode exists for; give it the most room. BENIGNO's supply curve says the unexpected part of the price level moves with the gap between output and its natural level. Have the hosts read the expected price level three ways. For the old Keynesians, rigidity is simply assumed. For the New Classicals, it is a forecast that can be wrong. For BENIGNO, it is a price already posted before the shock, by a fixed share of firms. Then draw the consequence: a central bank that reacts after prices are posted moves output even if its rule is public, so systematic stabilisation works again. Have a host check that nothing was lost: in BENIGNO's second period every firm resets, the supply curve is vertical, and money is neutral. Say what makes the slope steeper or flatter and why more rigidity flattens it.

SEGMENT THREE. SOMEBODY HAS TO SET THE PRICE. Ask the puzzle directly: under perfect competition every firm takes the price as given, so whose price is sticky? Let the answer be monopolistic competition. Give it three jobs, one sentence each: a firm now chooses a price; price sits above marginal cost, so a firm stuck at its old price is glad to serve extra demand, which is why output becomes demand-determined; and the mark-up is a wedge that keeps the natural level below what a planner would choose. Close with the menu-cost argument BENIGNO cites on page 507: for the firm, not adjusting costs only a second-order amount, while for the economy the output effect is first order.

SEGMENT FOUR. FROM THE MONEY STOCK TO THE INTEREST RATE, AND FROM A CHOSEN LOSS TO A DERIVED ONE. Two shifts, handled as a pair. Demand: the old model built it from a consumption function and a money-demand curve with the money stock as the instrument, while BENIGNO builds it from the household's saving decision with the short-term interest rate as the instrument, which is what central banks set. The slope now comes from the real interest rate, and expectations about the future enter demand today. Welfare: the old analyst chose a loss function; here it is derived from household utility, so its output target is the efficient level, not the natural one, and only price surprises are costly. Have the hosts disagree. One says this is just Keynes with better mathematics; the other answers with the two things Keynes did not have: rational expectations and a welfare target that differs from the natural level. Close on the limits BENIGNO lists in his conclusion: two periods, no inflation dynamics, no open economy.

Skip entirely: the algebra of the demand and supply curves, the fiscal multiplier tables, the zero lower bound, and debt deleveraging. Keep the history to the three schools named; if a source goes further, register it in one sentence and move on.

One instruction that belongs to audio and not to slides: stage segment two as a small courtroom. Each host defends one reading of the same symbol, and the verdict is delivered by working through what each reading predicts for a policy everyone expected. Never read an equation aloud; say what it decides.
