Output language: English, conversational register. Keep the spoken output in English throughout, whatever language the uploaded sources are in.

Thesis, and the conversation serves only this. An equation that cannot possibly be false cannot explain anything. The most famous equation in monetary economics, money stock times velocity equals the price level times output, is exactly such an equation, and the whole intellectual content lies in the assumption you must add before it says anything about the world.

Four segments. Do not add a fifth.

SEGMENT ONE. THE EQUATION THAT CANNOT FAIL. Open with the puzzle rather than the definition: this relation is cited constantly as evidence that printing money causes inflation, and it cannot be evidence of anything, because it is true by construction. Velocity is never measured independently; it is defined as nominal output divided by the money stock. So the equation is an identity, satisfied by any data from any country in any century, whatever the true economics happens to be. Have one host state the consequence sharply: you cannot test it, you cannot violate it, and so you learn nothing from the fact that it holds. Then let the other host push back with the obvious objection, that surely the relation is not vacuous given how tightly money growth and inflation move together across countries, and leave that objection standing until segment four.

SEGMENT TWO. WHAT TURNS IT INTO A THEORY. Now name the price of content. Two assumptions convert the identity into the quantity theory. First, that velocity is stable, which is a claim about behaviour, not an accounting matter. Second, that output is settled by real forces independently of money, which is the classical dichotomy. With both, the price level becomes proportional to the money stock, and causation runs from money to prices. Make the structure explicit: identity plus behavioural assumption equals theory. Then have the hosts notice what this does to the original question. The identity says that if the money stock rises, prices or output must rise or velocity must fall. It never says which. Choosing which is the theory, and the choosing happens outside the equation.

SEGMENT THREE. VELOCITY IS A CONCLUSION, NOT A PREMISE. This is the segment that pays for the episode, so give it the most room. The lecture derives money demand from a cost-minimisation problem: holding money forgoes interest, trips to the bank cost a fixed amount, and the household balances the two. Out comes real money demand proportional to the square root of output over the nominal rate. Have a host perform the substitution out loud: velocity equals output over real money demand, so velocity inherits everything money demand does. Under this model velocity rises with the nominal rate, rises with output, and falls with the cost of a trip. Land the collision: the assumption that made the identity a theory is contradicted by the theory of money demand the same lecture derives. Velocity is not a constant, and it moves precisely when interest rates move, which is precisely when monetary policy is doing something. Have the hosts disagree about how damaging this is, one treating it as fatal, the other as a tolerable approximation,.

SEGMENT FOUR. WHY THE EVIDENCE STILL LOOKS SO GOOD. Return to the objection from segment one and settle it. Across countries over long periods inflation really is almost proportional to money growth, and the relation tightens markedly for high-inflation countries. Give the sources' reconciliation: velocity does move, but where the money stock grows violently, the movement in velocity is small next to the movement in money. So the quantity theory is a poor description of the mechanism and an excellent approximation at long horizons and large magnitudes. Close on the discipline this implies: state that the equation is an identity, name the two assumptions, say what each buys, and only then appeal to the evidence. Writing that the equation proves money causes inflation skips every step that matters.

Skip entirely: the costs of inflation, the Friedman rule, seigniorage and the inflation tax, the derivation of the money multiplier, and the measurement of price indices.

One instruction belonging to audio and not slides: when the velocity formula appears, have the host say it in words and then immediately say what it implies about the interest rate, rather than reciting algebra. Keep numerals minimal and never read out a multi-step calculation; the arithmetic lives in the written material.
