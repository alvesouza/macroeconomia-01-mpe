Produce a two-host conversation about what the Solow model predicts when economies differ, and about the two objects every Solow answer has when population grows. Sources: "Livro_Pablo_Kurlat_-_A_Course_in_Modern_Macroeconomics_2020_-_libgen.li.pdf" (= KURLAT, section 4.2 at printed pages 57 to 61 and section 5.3 at pages 80 to 86), "Lista_MPE_Macro1_2026_Lista1.pdf" (= LISTA 1, question four) and "Lista_MPE_Macro1_2026_Lista2.pdf" (= LISTA 2, question one).

Thesis, and the conversation serves only this. The Solow model's defining result is that every economy heads to its own steady state and stays there. So two economies below their own steady states both grow, and the gap in levels persists without widening: that is conditional convergence, never divergence. When the labour force grows, output and output per worker are two different answers. And when two economies merge, capital is spread over more workers: the rich partner loses per head while the total rises.

Four segments. Do not add a fifth.

SEGMENT ONE. CONVERGENCE TO WHAT. Start from KURLAT's stability result: capital per worker moves toward one fixed point, from above or below. Apply it to the two Koreas of LISTA 2. Both start below their own steady states, so both grow during the transition, each toward its own level, set by its own saving, population growth and technology. The levels stay far apart because those parameters differ, and per-head growth ends at zero in both, since there is no technical progress. Then the autopsy of a graded answer that predicted divergence of output levels. Have one host defend the word: the gap is huge and it never closes. Let the other answer that divergence means dynamics pushing apart, and a model whose centrepiece is a stable fixed point cannot produce it. KURLAT's section 5.3 says the same thing about the data: poor countries do not catch up unconditionally.

SEGMENT TWO. TWO LINES WHENEVER POPULATION GROWS. Take the LISTA 1 earthquake that destroys half the capital. Per worker, output falls on impact and then climbs back to the old steady state, growing faster than before during the climb. In total, the labour force kept growing throughout, so total output ends above where it stood before the earthquake. Have one host give only the per-worker story and the other ask what happened to total output; that missing half cost the marks. Then the second part: if population growth also halves, the steady state per worker is higher, total output grows more slowly in the long run, and per-worker growth is faster during the transition. The rule for every Solow question: when population grows, write one line for output per worker and one for total output.

SEGMENT THREE. WHO GAINS WHEN TWO ECONOMIES MERGE. This is the segment the episode exists for; give it the most room. LISTA 2 unifies the Koreas on the South's technology with both countries' capital and workers. Ask the list's question, where the gain comes from in each case, and make the hosts answer it three times. The North gains twice: it adopts the better technology, and it gets far more capital per worker. The South loses per head: its capital is diluted over a larger workforce. Total output rises, because capital moves to where its marginal product is much higher and the North's workers use the better technology. Have one host call the South's loss a flaw in unification; let the other say that per head and in total are different questions, the grader's margin note in effect: not per head for the South, but still a gain.

SEGMENT FOUR. WHY THE UNIFIED STEADY STATE IS LOWER, AND THE ANSWERS. The unified Korea saves like the South but its labour force grows faster, a weighted average with the North's rapid growth. More new workers must be equipped each period, so the break-even line is steeper and capital per worker settles lower. Saving and technology are the South's in both cases, so population growth is the whole difference. Then rehearse in the hosts' own words the answers the lists cost: both economies converge, each to its own level; total and per head, both horizons; the North's two gains, the South's dilution, the aggregate gain; and the one sentence naming population growth as the reason the unified level is lower.

Skip entirely: the Golden Rule, factor prices, technological progress, growth accounting, and the speed of convergence. Stay at the level of the uploaded material; if a source goes further, register it in one sentence and move on.

One instruction that belongs to audio and not to slides: have one host narrate the Solow diagram being redrawn for each case, the saving curve, the break-even line, the point where they cross, and let the other interrupt to say which of the two objects, per head or total, each move changes. Never read an expression aloud; say what it decides.
