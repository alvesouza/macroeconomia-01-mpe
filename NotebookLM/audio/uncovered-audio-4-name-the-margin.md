Produce a two-host conversation about the questions an exam asks on labour supply and general equilibrium once the basic models are known. Source: "Livro_Pablo_Kurlat_-_A_Course_in_Modern_Macroeconomics_2020_-_libgen.li.pdf" (= KURLAT: section 7.4 at printed pages 140 and 141; Exercises 7.3 and 7.5 on pages 147 to 149; section 9.2 at pages 170 to 172; Exercises 9.4 and 9.6 on pages 181 to 183).

Thesis, and the conversation serves only this. In labour and general-equilibrium questions the mark goes to naming the margin: which first-order condition a shock or a policy touches, and whose budget it moves. A temporary and a permanent wage rise touch the same margin with different income effects; a tax on consumption and a tax on wages touch the same margin; and where the welfare theorem holds, a policy can help one household only by hurting another.

Four segments. Do not add a fifth.

SEGMENT ONE. TEMPORARY OR PERMANENT WAGES. The question: wages rise; do hours rise? KURLAT's dynamic model answers in section 7.4. The wage enters the hours condition together with the marginal utility of consumption, and consumption responds far more to a permanent raise than to a temporary one. A temporary rise leaves the household barely richer, so the income effect is weak, substitution wins, and hours rise more than after a permanent rise. KURLAT's example is surge pricing for ride-hailing drivers. The elasticity that holds consumption fixed is the Frisch elasticity, the one a temporary change calls for. The trap is to use the static model, where log utility cancels the two effects, to predict the response to a one-day wage spike. One sentence on Exercise 7.5: Prescott's tax story for European hours needs an elasticity well above the range of micro estimates, which KURLAT puts between zero point four and one.

SEGMENT TWO. TWO TAXES, ONE WEDGE. Exercise 7.3: a household pays an income tax and a consumption tax. Have one host claim a consumption tax cannot affect work because it does not tax work. Let the other read the budget line: an hour of work buys the after-tax wage divided by one plus the consumption tax, so both taxes sit in the same ratio, the one the marginal rate of substitution must equal. Swap the income tax for the consumption tax that leaves that ratio unchanged, and the household chooses exactly as before while the government raises the same revenue. Then Exercise 9.6, the same logic on the saving margin: a tax on capital income sits inside the capitalists' Euler equation, so in steady state the after-tax return still equals impatience, the pre-tax rental rate rises, capital falls, and wages fall. Rebating the revenue to workers does not undo the wedge; in steady state their lost wages outweigh the transfer. The sentence names the ratio the tax enters.

SEGMENT THREE. WHO GAINS FROM A POLICY. This is the segment the episode exists for; give it the most room. Exercise 9.4: the government caps hours slightly below the competitive level. Predict first. With less labour the wage rises and the rental rate of capital falls. With one representative household that both works and owns the capital, the household is worse off: the competitive allocation was the best feasible one, so a forced change from it can only lose. Now split the economy into a capitalist who does not work and a worker who owns nothing: the same cap makes the worker better off and the capitalist worse off. Land KURLAT's sentence from section 9.2: when the economy is competitive, the only possible economic justification for a policy is that one finds the redistribution desirable. The trap is answering "wages rise, so households gain" in the representative version, where higher wages and lower rents are a transfer from the household to itself and the only real change is that it can no longer work the hours it wanted.

SEGMENT FOUR. THE REHEARSAL. Have the hosts write three answers aloud, each in at most three sentences: hours after a temporary and after a permanent wage rise, with the elasticity named; the consumption tax that replicates an income tax, with the ratio named; and the hours cap, first for one household and then for two. After each, the other host names the margin touched.

Skip entirely: the search model and the Beveridge curve, the transfer channel under log utility, the proof of the welfare theorem, the modified golden rule, and the measurement of unemployment. Other episodes cover each. Stay at the level of KURLAT; if it goes further, register it in one sentence and move on.

One instruction that belongs to audio and not to slides: stage each question as a negotiation between named people, a worker, a capitalist, a tax office, so the listener hears who pays and who receives. Before any result, one host predicts its direction aloud and the other confirms or corrects it. Never read an expression aloud; say what it decides.
