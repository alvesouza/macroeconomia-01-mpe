Produce a two-host conversation about answering measurement questions the way an examiner marks them. Source: "Livro_Pablo_Kurlat_-_A_Course_in_Modern_Macroeconomics_2020_-_libgen.li.pdf" (= KURLAT: section 1.2 at printed pages 22 to 25 and Exercises 1.2 and 1.5 on pages 27 to 29; section 11.1 at pages 205 and 206; chapter 2 at pages 31 to 39 and Exercises 2.1 and 2.2 on pages 40 and 41).

Thesis, and the conversation serves only this. A measurement question never asks for a number alone. Behind every index sits a choice of weights, and the marks beyond the arithmetic go to three sentences: which weights produced this number, which way another choice would move it and why, and what the measure leaves out and how people's own choices would price it.

Four segments. Do not add a fifth.

SEGMENT ONE. WHICH YEAR'S PRICES. The typical question is KURLAT's Exercise 1.2: real growth at the first year's prices, then at the last year's, then chained, and explain the difference. Use KURLAT's Expandia example, where computers double and get cheaper while wheat grows a little and gets dearer: early-year prices give about seventy per cent growth, late-year prices about fifty-five. The sentence that earns the mark is KURLAT's own rule: an earlier base year gives the higher rate when the sectors expanding most are those whose relative price is falling. Then the trap: treating the chained figure as the true one. Have one host defend it; let the other show that chaining averages the two growth rates geometrically, year by year, so it re-chooses the weights every year instead of removing the choice. For Exercise 1.5, one force each way is enough.

SEGMENT TWO. WHICH BASKET. The second shape of question compares inflation by the GDP deflator with inflation by the consumer price index. In KURLAT's section 11.1 the deflator falls out of real GDP and weighs each good by its share of production; the price index reprices a fixed basket of what households consume. In Expandia the deflator says prices fell by more than a quarter although wheat got dearer, because computers dominate the production weights. The trap is to call the two indices the same thing measured twice. The sentence: they differ exactly where a country produces something different from what it consumes. KURLAT's example is an oil exporter, whose deflator jumps with the oil price while the consumer index barely moves. Have one host predict which moves more first.

SEGMENT THREE. WHAT THE NUMBER LEAVES OUT. This is the segment the episode exists for; give it the most room. Exercise 2.1 asks which countries look better under the Human Development Index and which under the Jones and Klenow measure, and why. The index turns life expectancy, schooling and log income into scores between zero and one and takes their geometric average; KURLAT calls the scaling and the equal weights somewhat arbitrary. Jones and Klenow instead ask what multiple of American consumption would make someone, not knowing who they would be, indifferent to a year in the other country, and every weight comes from choices people make. Then the reading: France looks better than its GDP because of longer lives, more leisure and less inequality; Singapore and other rich East Asian economies look worse because they consume little of what they produce. The trap is to blame inequality or health for the East Asian result; the mark goes to consumption against production.

SEGMENT FOUR. PRICE IT FROM A CHOICE, THEN THE REHEARSAL. Exercise 2.2 asks for one variable GDP misses and which observed choices would reveal how much people value it. KURLAT shows the method three times: risk from insurance, life from airbags and dangerous jobs, leisure from hours chosen. The mark goes to a choice where people trade consumption for that one thing at a price one can observe, such as what buyers pay for a home on a safer street. Have one host offer migration between countries as the evidence and the other explain why it fails: a move changes wages, language and family at once, so it cannot price any one of them. Then the hosts write three answers aloud, each in at most three sentences: the base-year rule, the deflator against the price index, and why a high-investment country looks poorer on welfare than on GDP.

Skip entirely: the three approaches to GDP, purchasing power parity and non-traded goods, the production boundary, and the risk-aversion algebra of Exercises 2.7 to 2.10. Other episodes cover the first three. Stay at the level of KURLAT; if it goes further, register it in one sentence and move on.

One instruction that belongs to audio and not to slides: run the episode as a mock oral exam. One host reads each question as an examiner would and marks every answer aloud, full, half or none; the other answers first with the tempting version and then earns the mark. Never read an expression aloud; say what it decides.
