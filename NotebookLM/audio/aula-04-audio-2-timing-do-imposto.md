Thesis. There is a result in this chapter which says that when a government cuts taxes today and raises them tomorrow to pay for it, households change nothing. The result is exactly true given its assumptions, and the assumption carrying almost all the weight is that households can borrow. The episode establishes the result properly and then breaks it in the way the problem set does.

Scope. Stay at the level of the uploaded lecture and of KURLAT sections 6.2 to 6.4. If a source reaches past that level, note it in one sentence and move on.

Skip entirely: the Keynesian consumption puzzle and permanent income, which the companion episode covers; the derivation of the Euler equation; and general equilibrium, where the interest rate stops being given. Do not attempt any calculation aloud.

SEGMENT ONE. A POLICY THAT SOUNDS LIKE FREE MONEY. Open with the proposal as a politician would put it: taxes are cut this year, the shortfall is borrowed, and the debt is repaid by higher taxes later. Have one host give the ordinary reasoning, that households have more money now, so they will spend more now, so demand rises. Let the other accept every factual step and then ask what a household that understands the plan would actually do. End the segment on that question without answering it.

SEGMENT TWO. THE HOUSEHOLD DOES THE ARITHMETIC. Walk through the reasoning slowly. The household receives extra money today. It also knows a larger tax is coming, and the amount coming is exactly what the money it received today would grow into at the going interest rate. So its lifetime resources are unchanged. Since consumption depends on lifetime resources, consumption does not move in either period, and the entire tax cut is saved to meet the future bill. Have one host state the conclusion as starkly as possible, that the timing of taxation does not matter, and let the other register how counterintuitive that is before it gets qualified.

SEGMENT THREE. THE OBJECTIONS, TAKEN IN ORDER. This is the segment that makes the result usable rather than merely clever. Have the hosts raise objections and answer each. That households are not that far-sighted, answered by noting the result needs them to be approximately right rather than perfectly rational. That the tax may fall on a later generation, answered by pointing to what has to be assumed about bequests. That taxes are not lump sums, which is genuine and gets developed later in the episode. And that households may be unable to borrow, which is the one to hold back, since it is where the argument is going.

SEGMENT FOUR. THE HOUSEHOLD THAT CANNOT BORROW. Develop the constraint as the problem set does. Some households cannot borrow against income they expect to receive, either because lenders will not advance it or because they have no security to offer. Ask what such a household does when it is handed a tax cut today. It spends it, because it was already consuming less than it wanted to and the constraint is what was stopping it. So the equivalence fails, and it fails for a reason that has nothing to do with rationality or foresight. Have one host draw the practical consequence, that whether a tax cut moves spending becomes an empirical question about how many households are in that position, and let the other note which groups are most likely to be.

SEGMENT FIVE. A SECOND WAY TO BREAK IT, AND WHY IT DIFFERS. Close with the other case from the problem set, in which the government taxes the return to saving instead of levying a fixed amount later, raising the same revenue either way. Establish that this one also breaks the equivalence, but for a different reason: it changes what saving is worth, so it alters the price of moving consumption between periods rather than only the household's total resources. Have the hosts name the distinction clearly, one instrument moving resources and the other distorting a margin, and say which is the more efficient way to raise a given sum. Give the listener one question to carry, about how they would tell, from data alone, which of the two failures is operating in a real economy.
