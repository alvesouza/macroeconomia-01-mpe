Produce a two-host conversation about one distinction in monetary theory. Sources: "Livro_Kurlat_Cap10-11_Money_and_Inflation.pdf" (Kurlat chapters 10 and 11, printed pages 189 to 222) and "Aula_MPE_Macro1_SlidesAula7_2026.pdf" (the lecture on money and inflation).

Output language: English, conversational register. Keep the spoken output in English throughout, whatever language the uploaded sources are in.

Thesis, and the conversation serves only this. The lecture is usually summarised as showing that money does not matter. That summary is wrong in a precise and examinable way. A change in the quantity of money does nothing real. A change in the rate at which money grows does something real, and the same model that delivers the first result proves the second.

Four segments. Do not add a fifth.

SEGMENT ONE. THE CLAIM EVERYONE REMEMBERS. Start with the clean result, because it is genuinely elegant. Double the money stock once, unexpectedly, permanently. Every price doubles, nothing real moves, and the model is finished with the question. Have a host give the adjustment story, which beats the algebra: everybody suddenly holds more money than they want and tries to spend it down, but they cannot all succeed because somebody has to hold it, so the only way the market clears is for money to lose value, which is what a higher price level is. Then have the other host raise the condition that this argument quietly needs, namely that prices are free to move immediately, and note that the whole result rests on it.

SEGMENT TWO. THE DISTINCTION THAT GETS EXAMINED. Now separate two claims that sound identical and are not. The first is about the level of the money stock: change it once and nothing real happens. The second is about its growth rate: keep increasing money faster forever and still nothing real happens. Have the hosts name them as two different propositions, the second much stronger than the first, and then state the punchline of the episode: this lecture endorses the first and refutes the second. Let one host be sceptical that a model this classical could contain a real cost of inflation at all, and set up segment three as the answer.

SEGMENT THREE. WHY FASTER MONEY GROWTH IS NOT FREE. This segment justifies the episode, so give it the most room, built as a chain the listener can repeat. Faster money growth means higher inflation. Higher inflation means a higher nominal rate, since the nominal rate is the real rate plus inflation. A higher nominal rate raises the opportunity cost of holding money, so households hold smaller real balances. To live on smaller balances they go to the bank more often, and every trip consumes real resources. So sustained inflation burns time and effort that could have gone elsewhere. Have a host point out what makes this elegant: the cost was derived from the same trip-cost model that produced money demand in the first place, so it is not an extra assumption bolted on to make inflation look bad. Then bring in the human version from the sources, the author's father in Argentina going to the bank twice a day during the high inflation of the late nineteen eighties, just to hold exactly enough cash for the day and no more. That is what the formula is describing.

SEGMENT FOUR. WHERE THE ARGUMENT LEADS, AND WHERE IT STOPS. Follow the logic to its conclusion and then mark its limit. If holding money has an opportunity cost, the obvious remedy is to abolish the cost by driving the nominal interest rate to zero, which requires inflation equal to minus the real interest rate, that is, mild deflation. Have a host note that this is a real proposal with a real name and that the sources themselves treat it as a theoretical extreme rather than practical advice. Then let the other host raise the competing consideration: changing posted prices also consumes resources, and that argument points to stable prices rather than falling ones. Have the hosts disagree about which cost should dominate and do not manufacture agreement, because the sources do not. Close on how to read a model: this lecture did not prove money is irrelevant. It showed which intervention is neutral and which is not, and remembering only the slogan means answering the wrong question.

Skip entirely: the derivation of the money multiplier and the banking system, the measurement of price indices and the Fisher arithmetic, the seigniorage revenue calculation, and the problem set. Do not cover what happens when prices cannot adjust, which belongs to later lectures and would change the subject.

One instruction that belongs to audio and not to slides: when the chain in segment three is laid out, have the hosts take one link each, in turn, so the listener hears the causation as a sequence rather than a list. Keep numerals out of this episode almost entirely. It is an argument about what a model does and does not claim, and there is nothing here a listener needs to compute.
