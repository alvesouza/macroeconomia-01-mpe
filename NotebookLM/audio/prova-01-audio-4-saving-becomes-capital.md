Produce a two-host conversation about turning one household's saving rule into a model of the whole economy. Source: "Livro_Pablo_Kurlat_-_A_Course_in_Modern_Macroeconomics_2020_-_libgen.li.pdf" (= KURLAT, section 9.1 at printed pages 165 to 168, with market clearing on page 167 and equation 9.1.11 on page 168; section 9.3 at pages 174 to 178, with the comparison to Solow on page 174 and to the golden rule on page 177; exercises 9.11 and 9.12 on pages 185 and 186; and section 4.2, equation 4.2.2 and Figure 4.2.1, on page 58).

Thesis, and the conversation serves only this. A household's saving rule is not yet a theory of the economy. Market clearing, saying that what households save is next period's capital because capital is the only asset, turns the rule into a law of motion for capital. Drawn against the forty-five degree line, its crossing is the steady state. The result is Solow's equation with full depreciation and no population growth, except that the saving rate is now chosen, and nothing forces the chosen rate to equal the golden-rule rate.

Four segments. Do not add a fifth.

SEGMENT ONE. THE MISSING EQUATION. The error autopsy comes first. The graded answer had the household's solution, never set saving equal to next period's capital, wrote a wrong map from this period's capital to the next, and drew a diagram with no valid steady state. Use KURLAT's definition of equilibrium: everyone optimises taking prices as given, and markets clear. Have one host ask which equation turns a household's choice into an aggregate quantity; let the other answer: the assets households carry forward are the capital stock, since there is nothing else to hold. Then the firm side: labour is paid its marginal product, the rest of output is capital's income, so the two add up to output. Next period's capital is the saving fraction, beta over one plus beta, times this period's wealth.

SEGMENT TWO. THE MAP AND THE FORTY-FIVE DEGREE LINE. This is the segment the episode exists for; give it the most room. Describe the curve in words: next period's capital as a function of this period's; concave; a positive intercept if there is an initial asset; a slope that starts steep and falls toward zero. The steady state is where it crosses the forty-five degree line, and it is stable because the curve crosses from above, with a slope below one. One host describes the graded diagram, a curve that never crosses the line; the other tests it against the shape just derived and shows why concavity forces exactly one crossing. Then relate it to KURLAT's Figure 4.2.1, saving against break-even investment: different axes, the same crossing.

SEGMENT THREE. SOLOW, WITH THE SAVING RATE CHOSEN. Set depreciation to one and population growth to zero in KURLAT's equation 4.2.2 and the law of motion above appears; KURLAT notes that equation 9.1.11 is the rental-rate condition with full depreciation. The graded answer stopped there, which is half the answer. The other half: Solow assumes the saving rate, while here patience pins it down, and log utility makes it constant and independent of the interest rate. That is a microfoundation of Solow's fixed saving rate, the comparison KURLAT's exercises 9.11 and 9.12 ask the student to compute. Cite them by number and page only; do not read them out.

SEGMENT FOUR. THE GOLDEN RULE, AND A DISAGREEMENT. Golden-rule saving equals the capital share; equilibrium saving equals the patience fraction; they coincide only by accident. One host cites KURLAT's page 177: an impatient household keeps capital below the golden rule. Let the other object: that result belongs to a household that lives forever, whose Euler equation ties the interest rate to impatience. Here each household lives two periods and saves a fixed fraction of wealth, so the comparison is between two numbers, and if the patience fraction exceeds the capital share the economy holds more capital than the golden rule. Verdict: each is right in its own model, and the answer must say which model it is in.
