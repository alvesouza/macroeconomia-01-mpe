FIRST EXAM, QUESTION ONE. MEASURING GDP THREE WAYS, AND WHY THE THREE MUST AGREE. A LISTENING VERSION.

Macroeconomics 1, Professional Master's in Economics, Insper, third trimester of 2026. Textbook: Kurlat, 2020, chapter 1, section 1.1, GDP accounting, printed pages 15 to 21, and chapter 2 on what GDP leaves out.


HOW TO LISTEN

This track teaches the one concept behind question one of your first exam, the question on GDP. You scored 1 out of 1.5 on it. The arithmetic was right every time. The marks went somewhere else, and this track is about where.

All numbers are the ones in the exam's own correction. I call the three firms by letter. A is the input firm. B is the final-goods firm. N is the social network.


PART ONE. THE IDEA. GDP COUNTS FINAL PRODUCTION ONCE, AND YOU CAN CATCH IT AT THREE POINTS.

Start with what GDP is trying to measure. It is the value of what an economy produced in a period, counted once. The words "counted once" carry the whole chapter.

Production happens in stages. One firm's output is another firm's input. If you add up the sales of every firm, you count the input once when it is sold to the next firm, and again inside the price of the good it went into. That is double counting. GDP avoids it by measuring the value at the end of the chain.

There are three places along the chain where you can catch that same value, and that is why there are three approaches.

The first is the product approach, also called the production or value-added approach. For each producer, take the value of what it produced and subtract the value of the inputs it bought from other producers and used up. What is left is value added: the value this producer contributed. Sum value added across producers.

The second is the income approach. Every unit of value added is paid out to somebody. It goes to workers as wages, or stays with the owners as profit, which national accountants call operating surplus. It may also go out as interest, rent, or taxes on production. Sum those incomes across producers.

The third is the expenditure approach. Look at what the final production was used for. It was consumed by households, which is C. It was invested, which is I: a new machine, a new building, new software, or goods piled up in inventory. It was bought by the government, which is G. Or it was sold abroad net of what was bought from abroad, which is net exports. Sum the four.

Here is the reason the three must give the same number, and it is worth saying slowly. Value added for a producer is its sales minus its purchased inputs. That same difference is exactly what is left to pay wages and to leave as profit, so value added and income are the same number, firm by firm. And when you sum value added along a whole chain, every intermediate sale appears once as a cost to the buyer and once as revenue to the seller, so they cancel. What survives is the value of the sales to final users. That is expenditure.

So the three approaches are not three estimates that happen to come close. They are one number seen from three sides. In practice statistical agencies get small discrepancies. In an exam question built from a few firms, they must agree exactly, and if yours do not, something is misclassified.

Kurlat makes the point with his very first examples. In example 1.1, printed page 16, Amy grows lettuce and sells it to Bob for one dollar. Product: one dollar of agriculture. Income: one dollar of proprietor's income. Expenditure: one dollar of consumption. In example 1.2, the fertilizer plant sells fertilizer worth 80 cents to a farmer who sells lettuce for one dollar. Adding 80 cents and one dollar would be the mistake. The farmer's value added is 20 cents, the plant's is 80, and GDP is one dollar on all three sides.

Now the exam's starting point, item 1 a, which you got right and should be able to say in three sentences. A sells inputs worth 600 to B. B sells final goods worth 1,000 to households. Product: A adds 600, B adds 1,000 minus 600, which is 400; total 1,000. Income: A pays out 600 in wages and profit, B pays out 400; total 1,000. Expenditure: consumption of 1,000. Adding 600 and 1,000 to get 1,600 would count A's input twice.

Self-test for part one. Pause after each question.

Question one. In one sentence, why must value added and income be the same number for each firm?

Question two. In one sentence, why does summing value added along a chain equal spending on final goods?

Question three. What would you have got for item 1 a by summing sales, and what is the name of that error?

Answers. One: value added is sales minus purchased inputs, and that difference is exactly what is left to pay as wages and keep as profit. Two: every intermediate sale is a cost to the buyer and revenue to the seller, so it cancels, leaving only sales to final users. Three: 1,600, which is double counting of the 600 of inputs.


PART TWO. ITEM 1 B. ADVERTISING, A FREE SERVICE, AND AN ANSWER THAT SHOWED ONE APPROACH OUT OF THREE.

The idea first. Item 1 b adds a social network, N. B pays N 100 for advertising. Users of the network get its service for free.

Two classification questions decide the answer.

First, what is the advertising? It is a service bought by a firm and used up in that firm's production. That makes it an intermediate input of B, exactly like A's 600 of inputs. It is not a final good, because no final user bought it.

Second, what is the free service the users enjoy? It has a price of zero and no market transaction takes place. GDP is built from market transactions, so the free service is not in GDP. The users really are better off. That welfare is real and unmeasured, which is one of the limits of GDP that Kurlat discusses in chapter 2, Beyond GDP, from printed page 33.

Now walk all three approaches, because that is what the item asked for.

Product. A still adds 600. B's value added is its sales of 1,000, minus 600 of inputs from A, minus 100 of advertising from N: that is 300. N's value added is 100, since it bought no inputs. The sum is 600 plus 300 plus 100, which is 1,000.

Income. A pays out 600, as before. B's profit falls by 100, so B pays out 300. N earns 100. The sum is 1,000.

Expenditure. Households still spend 1,000 on B's goods. Nothing else is sold to a final user. The sum is 1,000.

All three say 1,000, and GDP is unchanged from item 1 a. What moved is the distribution of income: 100 moved from B to N. That is the comparison the item wanted: same total, different split.

Now the autopsy. What you wrote: the number, 1,000, and nothing showing the three approaches. The grader gave 0.3 out of 0.5. The answer was not wrong; it was incomplete. The item named three objects, the product, income and expenditure approaches, and the answer delivered one of them.

Why that costs marks even with the right number: in a question like this the number is the easy part. The three approaches are where the classification is tested. Showing B's value added falling to 300 is the proof that you treated advertising as an input. Showing N's income of 100 is the proof that you know where the money went. Without them, a correct 1,000 could have come from a lucky guess or from ignoring N altogether.

The check that catches it: before you leave a GDP item, count the approaches on your page. If the statement says "three approaches", or "product, income and expenditure", there must be three totals, and they must be equal. Write them as three short lines, one per approach, each ending in the same total.

Self-test for part two. Pause after each question.

Question one. Why is the advertising an intermediate input and not a final good?

Question two. Why does the free service users enjoy not enter GDP?

Question three. Between items 1 a and 1 b, what changed and what did not?

Answers. One: it was bought by a firm, B, and used up in B's production; no final user bought it. Two: it has a price of zero, there is no market transaction, and GDP counts market production; the welfare is real but unmeasured. Three: GDP stayed at 1,000 on all three approaches; the distribution changed, with B's value added and profit falling by 100 and N's rising by 100.


PART THREE. ITEM 1 C. SOFTWARE BUILT FOR OWN USE IS INVESTMENT, NOT CONSUMPTION.

The idea first. In item 1 c, N uses its 100 of advertising revenue to pay workers who build software that N itself will use. Nobody buys the software. Nothing else in the economy changes.

The question is what kind of thing that software is. Ask the question Kurlat asks of every new product: will it be used to produce things in future periods? Software that runs a network for years is a durable productive asset, like a machine. Kurlat's example 1.3, printed page 17, gives the rule: investment takes different forms, and what they have in common is that they involve producing something that will be used for production in future periods. National accountants call this gross fixed capital formation, and software is investment in intellectual property. Because nobody pays a market price for it, it is valued at what it cost to produce: 100 of wages.

Now the three approaches.

Product. A adds 600 and B adds 300, as in item 1 b. N now produces two things: advertising worth 100, and software worth 100. N's value added is 200. The total is 600 plus 300 plus 200, which is 1,100.

Income. A pays 600 and B pays 300. N pays 100 in wages to the software workers. N also has an operating surplus of 100: it received 100 for advertising, produced software valued at 100, and paid 100 in wages, so 100 remains. N's income side is 200. The total is 1,100.

Expenditure. Households still spend 1,000 on consumption; the statement says nothing else changes, so C is still 1,000. The software is investment of 100. The total is 1,000 plus 100, which is 1,100.

GDP rises by 100 because a new final good was produced. It is final because nobody used it up as an input this period. And it is an investment good, not a consumption good.

The autopsy. What you wrote: 1,100, the right number, with the extra 100 attributed to "wages and consumption". The grader gave 0.2 out of 0.5, marking a conceptual error, and the three approaches were again missing.

Why it fails. "Wages" belongs to the income approach. "Consumption" belongs to the expenditure approach. Putting them in one phrase mixes two approaches in a single sentence, which is the first sign the classification has not been done. And "consumption" is simply the wrong category. If consumption had risen to 1,100, households would have to be buying 100 more of something. They are not. The only new thing produced is a durable asset that N keeps and uses. In the expenditure approach that is I, not C.

The check that catches it has two parts. First, the expenditure check: for every component you add to GDP, name who bought it, and for what. If the buyer is a household buying for its own use, it is C. If the "buyer" is a firm keeping a productive asset, even one it made for itself, it is I. Here nobody bought anything new for consumption, so C cannot move. Second, the agreement check: write all three approaches. The moment you write the expenditure line, C equal to 1,000 and I equal to 100, the word "consumption" has nowhere to go.

One more point the correction makes, because it shows you understand the convention rather than just the answer. If the software were treated as an ordinary running cost, an intermediate expense, then N's value added would stay at 100: the 100 of wages would be a cost, its operating surplus would fall to zero, and GDP would stay at 1,000. The reason GDP is 1,100 is that modern national accounts capitalise software. That is a convention, and saying "the software is capitalised as investment in intellectual property, valued at cost" is the sentence that earns the mark.

Self-test for part three. Pause after each question.

Question one. What single question decides whether something produced is investment?

Question two. Why can consumption not be 1,100 in item 1 c?

Question three. What would GDP be if the software were expensed as an intermediate cost, and why?

Answers. One: will it be used to produce things in future periods? If yes, it is investment. Two: the statement says nothing else changes; households buy the same 1,000; the new output is a durable asset kept by N, so it is I. Three: 1,000, because N's wages would be a cost against its 100 of advertising revenue, leaving value added of 100 and an operating surplus of zero.


PART FOUR. ANALOGOUS CASES, WORKED IN WORDS.

Each case below uses the same two moves. First, classify: final or intermediate, and if final, which of C, I, G or net exports. Second, write the three approaches and check they agree. I will run both moves every time.

Case one. Inventories. This is Kurlat's example 1.3, part three, printed pages 17 and 18. A paper company produces paper worth 40,000 and puts it in its warehouse because no customer has bought it yet. Its workers were paid 50,000.

Classify. The paper was not sold to a final user. But it was produced this year, and it will be used later. Goods held for later use are inventories, and an increase in inventories is investment.

Three approaches. Product: 40,000 of manufacturing. Income: wages of 50,000 and a corporate profit of minus 10,000, which sums to 40,000. Expenditure: inventory investment of 40,000. All three agree. Notice the loss: the income side can include a negative profit and still balance.

The follow-up is the part people forget. Next year, when the paper is sold to final users, consumption rises and inventory investment is negative by the same amount. The paper is counted in GDP once, in the year it was produced, not again in the year it was sold.

A variant to try on your own. A car maker produces a car for 20,000 in December and sells it to a household in January. In December's year, it is inventory investment of 20,000. In January's year, consumption rises by 20,000 and inventory investment falls by 20,000, so the sale adds nothing to that year's GDP, except the dealer's own value added if there is one.

Case two. Used goods. A household sells a five-year-old car to another household for 8,000.

Classify. The car was produced five years ago and was counted then. Selling it again creates nothing new. One household's spending is matched by the other household's sale of an existing asset. It is a change of ownership, and like Warren buying shares in Kurlat's example 1.4, printed page 18, it is zero on all three approaches.

Now add a dealer. The dealer buys the car for 7,000 and sells it for 8,000. The car itself is still not new production. But the dealer produced a service: finding a buyer, inspecting the car, handling the paperwork. Its value added is the margin, 1,000. Product: 1,000 of trade services. Income: 1,000 of wages and profit at the dealer. Expenditure: the buyer's 8,000 is 7,000 for an existing asset changing hands and 1,000 for a consumption service, so only 1,000 enters C. All three say 1,000.

Case three. Imputed rent. Kurlat's example 1.5, part four, printed page 19. Claire lives in a house she bought last year. A similar house would rent for 7,000 a year.

Classify. No money changes hands. But if Claire rented the house to a tenant, the 7,000 of rent would plainly be a consumption service in GDP. If GDP fell every time a tenant bought the house she lived in, GDP would move with the pattern of ownership, not with production. So the accounts impute the rent: they treat Claire as a landlord renting to herself.

Three approaches. Product: 7,000 of housing services. Income: 7,000 of imputed owner-occupier income. Expenditure: 7,000 of consumption. Kurlat notes on printed page 21 that imputed rent on owner-occupied housing is almost 8 per cent of United States GDP.

The contrast that makes it stick. Kurlat's example 1.5, part two: Bob watches a television he bought last year. That adds zero. A television is a durable good, but the convention counts it as consumption when it is bought and does not impute a flow of television services afterwards. Housing gets special treatment because it is so large and so long-lived.

And the contrast with item 1 c. Housing services are imputed because of a deliberate exception. Most non-market activity, cooking at home or caring for your own children, is left out, as Kurlat says on printed page 21. Own-account software is in GDP for a different reason: it is a capital good produced by a firm, capitalised and valued at cost.

Case four. The lawyer. A lawyer charges 5,000 for drafting a contract.

If a firm hires the lawyer to draft a supply contract, the legal service is used up in the firm's production. It is an intermediate input, exactly like the advertising in item 1 b. Product: the lawyer adds 5,000, and the firm's value added falls by 5,000. Income: the lawyer earns 5,000, and the firm's profit falls by 5,000. Expenditure: final sales are unchanged. GDP is unchanged; income moves from the firm to the lawyer.

If a family hires the same lawyer to draft a will, the service goes to a final user for its own use. It is consumption. Product: the lawyer adds 5,000. Income: the lawyer earns 5,000. Expenditure: C rises by 5,000. GDP rises by 5,000.

The same service, at the same price, adds to GDP in one case and not in the other. What decides it is who buys it, and for what.

Case five. Two government cases, from Kurlat's examples 1.7 and 1.8, printed pages 19 and 20. A public schoolteacher is paid 85,000. There is no market price for the teaching, so it is valued at cost: 85,000 of public education on the product side, 85,000 of wages on the income side, 85,000 of government spending on the expenditure side. But when a retiree collects a 20,000 pension, nothing is produced. It is a transfer, and it is zero on all three approaches. Government outlays are in G only when they buy production.

Self-test for part four. Pause after each question.

Question one. A firm produces goods worth 300 that it cannot sell this year. Which component of expenditure records them?

Question two. You pay 8,000 to a dealer for a used car the dealer bought for 7,000. How much enters GDP?

Question three. Why do the national accounts impute rent to owner-occupiers?

Question four. An accounting firm audits a company. Final good or intermediate?

Answers. One: investment, as an increase in inventories; and when the goods are sold next year, consumption rises and inventory investment falls by the same amount. Two: 1,000, the dealer's margin; the car is an existing asset. Three: so that GDP does not change when housing shifts between tenant occupancy and owner occupancy. Four: intermediate, because a firm buys it and uses it up in its own production; GDP is unchanged and income shifts to the auditors.


PART FIVE. THE CHECK BEFORE YOU HAND IN, AND THE TOPIC IN FIVE LINES.

The check, in four steps. Say it to yourself before you leave any GDP item.

Step one. For every transaction, classify: intermediate or final. Intermediate means a producer bought it and used it up this period.

Step two. For every final item, name the component: C if a household buys it for its own use, I if it is a new durable productive asset or an addition to inventories, G if the government buys production, net exports if it crosses the border. Transfers and sales of existing assets are nothing.

Step three. Write all three approaches, one line each, with a total at the end of each line.

Step four. Check that the three totals agree. If they do not, go back to step one, because something is misclassified.

Applied to the exam, steps three and four are exactly what items 1 b and 1 c were missing: the three lines for 1 b, and in 1 c the expenditure line with C equal to 1,000 and I equal to 100, which leaves no room for the word "consumption".

The whole topic in five lines. This is the part to replay.

One. GDP is the value of final production, counted once.

Two. Product, income and expenditure are one number seen from three sides, and in an exam they must agree.

Three. A good or service bought by a firm and used up is intermediate; the same thing bought by a household is consumption.

Four. Anything new that will produce in future periods is investment: machines, buildings, inventories, and software, even software a firm builds for itself.

Five. Zero-price services, transfers and sales of existing assets are not in GDP; owner-occupied housing is, by imputation.
