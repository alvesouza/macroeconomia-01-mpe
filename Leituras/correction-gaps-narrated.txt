Two point two. The concept: income and substitution effects on hours.

Start from the household. It values consumption c and leisure l. In List 4, utility is the natural log of c, plus b times l to the power one minus gamma, all over one minus gamma. It has one unit of time, so hours worked, which I will call n, equal one minus l. It earns the wage w on every hour, pays a fraction tau of wages in tax, and receives a lump-sum transfer T from the government. So consumption equals one minus tau, times w, times n, plus T.

Now think about what a change in the net wage does. The net wage is one minus tau, times w. Call it w tilde.

A higher net wage does two things at once. First, it makes leisure more expensive: every hour you do not work now costs you more consumption. That pushes you to work more. This is the substitution effect. Second, it makes you richer: at the same hours, you now have more to spend, and a richer household wants more of everything, including leisure. That pushes you to work less. This is the income effect.

The two effects point in opposite directions, and which one wins depends on the utility function. So the formula decides, and the intuition has to agree with it.

The special fact about log utility: with no non-labour income, the two effects cancel exactly. Two steps show why.

Step one. The first-order condition says the marginal utility of leisure equals the net wage times the marginal utility of consumption. With log utility, the marginal utility of consumption is one over c. So b times l to the power minus gamma equals w tilde over c.

Step two. If T is zero, consumption is just w tilde times n. Substitute: b times l to the power minus gamma equals w tilde over the quantity w tilde times n, which is one over n. The net wage has cancelled; hours are pinned down by b and gamma alone. In words: a higher wage makes leisure dearer in exactly the proportion that it makes you richer.

Now bring the transfer back. The instructor's boxed equation is this: one minus n, raised to the power gamma, minus b times n, equals b times T, divided by the quantity one minus tau times w.

The knife-edge test: set T to zero, the right-hand side vanishes, and neither tau nor w appears. Labour supply is independent of the wage and the tax.

So when T is positive, the whole effect of the tax works through T. And T is unearned income. It raises the household's wealth without changing the price of leisure. That is a pure income effect on hours. Raising tau lowers the net wage, which makes the same transfer larger relative to the household's earning capacity. The pure income effect strengthens. The household takes more leisure, and hours worked fall.

Check it against the equation. The left-hand side, one minus n to the gamma, minus b n, falls as n rises. A higher tau raises the right-hand side when T is positive. To restore equality, the left-hand side must rise, so n must fall. Hours fall. Leisure rises. The derivative you wrote, positive for leisure and with T in the numerator, says exactly this. It is zero when T is zero.

Now you can hear what the question mark was asking. Your sentence told a substitution-effect story: leisure is cheaper, so take more of it. With log utility that effect is cancelled, and the formula on your own page says the tax matters only because of T. The formula was right, and the sentence beneath it contradicted it.

Two point four. The search and matching model: the objects.

Now the second half of chapter 7: unemployment exists because workers and firms take time to find each other.

List 4 question 2 sets up the Diamond, Mortensen and Pissarides model, abbreviated DMP. There are U unemployed workers, V vacancies posted by firms, and E employed workers. The labour force L equals U plus E and is constant.

The number of new matches each period is given by the matching function: m equals mu times V to the power alpha, times U to the power one minus alpha. Alpha is between zero and one, and mu is positive.

Read it as a production function for matches, with searchers on both sides as inputs and constant returns to scale.

Mu is matching efficiency. The professor's margin note defines it by the flow it governs: it increases the inflow from unemployment to employment. Better information, or skills that fit the jobs on offer, mean a higher mu.

The unemployment rate u is U over L, the vacancy rate v is V over L, and market tightness theta is V over U, vacancies per unemployed worker. Two rates follow, and they are the heart of the model.

The job-finding rate, f, is matches per unemployed worker: m over U. Divide the matching function by U and you get f equals mu times theta to the power alpha. Because alpha is positive, f rises with theta. When vacancies are plentiful relative to searchers, each worker finds a job faster.

The vacancy-filling rate, q, is matches per vacancy: m over V. Divide by V and you get q equals mu times theta to the power alpha minus one. Because alpha is less than one, that exponent is negative, and q falls with theta. When there are many vacancies relative to searchers, each vacancy takes longer to fill.

And f equals theta times q. Your answer to item 2 c got all of this right, with five ticks.

Tightness is good news for workers and bad news for firms. That is the whole of item 2 d.


Two point five. The Beveridge curve: what you wrote, and the concept.

List 4 item 2 b asked for the job-creation-equals-job-destruction equation, the Beveridge curve, its graph, and why it slopes the way it does.

The diagnosis verified your algebra independently: the curve matches the key and the sign of the slope is right. The item still got a cross.

The reason is inferred; the grader wrote none. The page jumped straight to algebra. It never wrote the steady-state condition as the job-creation-equals-job-destruction condition, and it gave the sign of the derivative in place of an economic explanation. The professor's key writes the words explicitly: the Beveridge curve means job creation equals job destruction. The graph on your page also carried an annotation, "mu falls", that belongs to item e, not item b.

So here is the concept, built in words first.

The law of motion of employment. Employment next period equals employment this period, minus the jobs destroyed, plus the jobs created. Destruction is s times E: each period a fraction s of the employed lose their jobs, for reasons outside the model. Creation is the number of matches, mu times V to the alpha times U to the one minus alpha. The professor underbraces the two terms and names them: destruction and creation. Naming them is part of the answer.

Steady state means employment does not change. So destruction equals creation. Divide by L, and write E over L as one minus u. The condition is: s times one minus u, equals mu times v to the power alpha, times u to the power one minus alpha.

Say it in words before solving it: the flow out of employment equals the flow into employment.

Solve for v. The Beveridge curve is: v equals the quantity s times one minus u, over mu, raised to the power one over alpha, all times u raised to the power minus the quantity one minus alpha, over alpha.

Why it slopes down, in flow terms. Raise unemployment at a point on the curve. Two things happen.

First, on the creation side: more unemployed workers means more searchers, and more searchers means each vacancy produces more matches. So fewer vacancies are needed to produce any given number of matches.

Second, on the destruction side: higher unemployment means fewer people employed, so fewer jobs are destroyed each period. The outflow that creation has to replace is smaller.

Both mean fewer vacancies are needed to keep the flows in balance. So along the curve, higher unemployment goes with lower vacancies. The curve slopes down.


Two point six. The congestion externality: what you wrote, and the concept.

List 4 item 2 d: one firm posts one extra vacancy. What happens to f and q, who gains and who loses, and does the firm take this into account?

You wrote, in Portuguese, that the firm takes the lower q, but its loss is infinitesimal, so it does not consider that small loss. The item got a cross, and here the diagnosis is certain about why: wrong agent.

A loss to the firm itself would be internalised by definition: the decision-maker bears it and weighs it. The content of the word externality is that the cost lands on somebody else.

The concept. One extra vacancy raises tightness theta. So the job-finding rate f equals mu theta to the alpha rises, and the vacancy-filling rate q equals mu theta to the alpha minus one falls. Now read off who gains and who loses. The question asked for a list, so give a list.

Workers gain: every unemployed worker now finds a job faster.

Other firms lose: every other firm's vacancies are now harder to fill. This is the congestion externality. The posting firm crowds the market for the others.

The posting firm gains, privately. It posts because its own expected profit from the vacancy is positive.

And one more thing, from the professor's margin: matches increase by less than one. The marginal match from one extra vacancy is the derivative of m with respect to V, which is alpha times q, and alpha times q is less than q. The extra vacancy does not produce one extra match. The shortfall is the friction: vacancies to employment is not one to one.

Does the firm take these effects into account? No. It weighs only its own expected profit. It internalises neither the congestion it imposes on rival firms nor the benefit it confers on workers.

The professor's key, in his words: congestion externality; the firm posting the vacancy is better off, but others are harmed; workers also benefit from a higher chance to find a job.


Two point seven. Shift of the curve versus movement along it.

List 4 item 2 e: a fall in mu. Parts one and two got ticks. You derived the elasticity correctly: the derivative of the log of v with respect to the log of mu is minus one over alpha, which is negative. Part three got a cross. You wrote that it raises the number of vacancies and shifts the curve, and that the movement along the curve comes from the variation of v given the relation of vacancies to unemployment. The direction is right. The distinction that was asked for is not made.

The concept. A movement along the curve changes tightness at a given matching technology: over the cycle the economy slides between points on the same curve, and mu has not changed.

A shift changes the matching technology itself. When mu falls, every pair of u and v now yields fewer matches. To keep creation equal to destruction at the same unemployment rate, more vacancies are needed. So at every u, v is higher: the whole curve moves out, away from the origin. By the elasticity, a one per cent fall in mu raises v at a given u by one over alpha per cent.

The professor's words: a fall in mu is a shift in all v and u values and possible states, rather than just changing how theta is split between u and v.

Empirically, an outward shift of the Beveridge curve is the standard diagnostic of deteriorating matching efficiency; a movement along it is the business cycle.

Three point two. The index-number concept: substitution bias.

Look at the prices. Good one went from 8 to 2: it became much cheaper. Good two went from 4 to 6: it became dearer. Now look at the quantities. Good one went from 5 to 12. Good two went from 4 to 3. Households substituted toward the good whose price fell. That is the normal response to a change in relative prices.

Valuing the new quantities at the old prices, the Laspeyres approach, prices the large new quantity of good one, 12 units, at its old high price of 8. The expansion of the good that got cheap is weighted as if it were still expensive. So it overstates growth: 92.8 per cent.

Valuing at the new prices, the Paasche approach, compares 2025 output of 42 with 2024 quantities valued at 2025 prices, which is 34. Now the expansion of good one is weighted at its new low price of 2. So it understates growth: 23.5 per cent.

The general statement: when relative prices move and quantities substitute toward the goods whose prices fell, the base year is not neutral. Old-price weights bias measured growth upward; new-price weights bias it downward. That is substitution bias, and it is why statistical agencies chain their indices, updating the weights every year. That is Kurlat's point in chapter 1.


Three point three. The purchasing power parity concept, built from the ground.

Now Colombia. Colombian prices in pesos: good one costs 40, and Colombia produces 1 unit; good two costs 8, and Colombia produces 2 units. The exchange rate is 25 pesos per real.

At the market exchange rate, divide each peso price by 25 and value Colombia's own quantities: 1.6 times 1, plus 0.32 times 2, which is 2.24 reais.

At purchasing power parity, throw the exchange rate away and value Colombia's quantities at one common set of prices, here Brazil's 2024 prices of 8 and 4: 8 times 1, plus 4 times 2, which is 16.

Two correct measurements of the same output, differing by a factor of more than 7. Why?

The market exchange rate is set in the currency market by trade and capital flows. Arbitrage acts through traded goods: if a tradable were far cheaper in Colombia once converted, someone would buy it there and sell it in Brazil. So the exchange rate roughly lines up the prices of traded goods.

But most of what an economy produces is not traded: housing, haircuts, local transport, restaurant meals, government services. No arbitrage can ship a haircut from Bogota to Sao Paulo, so nothing equalises its price.

And there is a systematic pattern in which way non-traded prices differ. Non-traded goods, and above all labour, are cheaper in poorer countries. The reason has a name: the Balassa-Samuelson effect. It runs in four steps.

Step one. Rich and poor countries differ in productivity much more in traded goods, manufactures and modern industry, than in non-traded services. A barber in a rich country is not many times more productive than a barber in a poor country. A factory worker often is.

Step two. Traded-goods prices are roughly equalised by arbitrage. So wages in the traded sector reflect productivity there: high in the rich country, low in the poor one.

Step three. Within a country, workers can move between sectors, so wages tend to equalise across sectors. The barber in the rich country earns something close to the factory wage there, and so does the barber in the poor country.

Step four. So services, whose productivity is similar everywhere, are expensive where wages are high and cheap where wages are low. The general price level, converted at the market rate, is lower in poor countries.

So converting a poor country's output at the market rate values its large non-traded output at prices that are low by international standards, and understates real production. PPP values every country's quantities at the same prices, which is why cross-country comparisons, and the development accounting of chapter 5, use PPP data.

Why trade costs are the wrong answer. Import and export costs are a friction in traded goods. But traded goods are exactly the goods where the market exchange rate works best, because they are the goods that arbitrage connects. A transport cost opens a band within which traded prices can differ; it does not explain why the whole price level is systematically lower in poor countries, and it does not produce a sevenfold gap. The mechanism runs through the goods that are not traded at all.

Four point two. The concept: each economy goes to its own steady state.

Recall the Solow engine. Saving adds capital per worker; depreciation removes it; population growth spreads it over more workers.

The steady state is where these balance: saving per worker equals n plus delta, times k. Solve, and k star equals s times A over n plus delta, all raised to the power one over one minus alpha.

The crucial property: this steady state is stable. Below k star, saving exceeds what holds k constant, so k rises; above, it falls. And the further below its steady state, the faster an economy grows.

Now the word convergence. There are two meanings, and the exam tests whether you keep them apart.

Absolute convergence would say poor countries grow faster than rich ones, full stop, so everyone ends at the same level. The model does not predict it, and the data reject it.

Conditional convergence says that each country converges to its own steady state, fixed by its own saving rate, population growth, technology and capital share. A country grows fast when it is far below its own steady state, not when it is poor in some absolute sense.

Apply it to Korea. Both economies start below their own steady states: the North at 7.11 against 8, the South at 100 against 125. So the model predicts that both grow during the transition, each toward its own y star. The gap in levels persists, 8 against 125 in the long run, because s, n, A and alpha differ. It does not widen because of any divergent dynamics. In the long run, per capita growth is zero in both, because there is no technical progress.

"Divergence" is the wrong word for a model whose defining result is that k goes to a stable fixed point. Divergence would mean the gap keeps widening, and in Solow without technical progress nothing keeps widening.

A second distinction lives here: level versus growth rate. A permanent change in s or n changes the level of the steady state, not the long-run growth of output per worker, which is zero without technical progress. The parameters decide how rich a country is, and how fast it grows only during the transition.


Four point three. Solow comparative statics: the fall in n.

n falls to n prime, less than n. The new steady state is k star prime equals s over the quantity n prime plus delta, raised to one over one minus alpha. It is greater than the old k star, because the denominator is smaller.

In words: with slower population growth, fewer new workers arrive each period who must be equipped with capital, so the same saving raises capital per worker further.

The diagram. The standard Solow picture with two straight lines from the origin: the original n plus delta times k, and a flatter n prime plus delta times k. Both cut the same concave saving curve, the flatter one further to the right. The economy starts at the post-earthquake capital, half the old steady state, and moves rightward, past the old steady state, to the new and higher one. Beneath it, the time path: capital per worker rising from the post-earthquake level to a level above where it started.

His margin note, three lines: higher levels, lower steady-state growth, higher growth during convergence.

Unpack each. Higher levels: k and y are permanently higher. Lower steady-state growth: aggregate output grows in the long run at the population growth rate, which has fallen. Higher growth during convergence: the economy has further to travel, and in Solow growth rises with the distance from the steady state.

One and a half points for four lines and one diagram. The diagram alone carries most of the credit. Never leave a Solow comparative static blank.

PART EIGHT. THE NINE RULES. A CHECKLIST TO RUN BEFORE HANDING IN ANY ANSWER.

The first four come from the diagnosis of Lists 1 and 4. The last five come from the diagnosis of Lists 2, 3 and 6. Listen to them as a routine: run them on every answer, in this order, before you move to the next item.

Rule one. Answer every object the question names. "GDP and GDP per capita" is two answers. "Which agents gain and which lose" is a list, not a sentence. This alone accounted for 4 of the 11 items rejected on Lists 1 and 4.

Rule two. Never let the words contradict the formula. Before writing the intuition, set every other parameter to its knife-edge value and ask what the formula then says. On List 4, set T to zero and the effect vanishes, so the story must be about T.

Rule three. An externality is always about somebody else. If your sentence about an externality has the acting agent as the one harmed, it is wrong.

Rule four. Circle intermediate results and copy from the circle. Both arithmetic losses on List 1 were values that changed between the line computing them and the line using them.

Rule five. List the objects before writing. Underline every noun the question asks for: c one, c two and a; each country and the aggregate; two examples. Tick each one off at the end. This alone would have saved 9 of the 27 items.

Rule six. After every derivative, set the deciding parameter to a knife-edge value and read the sign aloud before writing the sentence. Three of the lost items are sentences that contradicted a correct formula.

Rule seven. A proof is followed by a conclusion that agrees with it. If you have shown that X enters only through Y, the next line says X does not matter, never that X matters.

Rule eight. Explain means name the mechanism in one causal chain, in the professor's arrow style. F falls, so money demand falls, so at a given mu real balances are too high, so inflation rises.
