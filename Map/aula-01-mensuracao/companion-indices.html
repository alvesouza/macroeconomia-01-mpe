The Index Bench

Two goods, two years. Real growth has no single answer — it has a Laspeyres answer, a Paasche answer, and a Fisher answer between them. The gap is one number: the share-weighted covariance between price growth and quantity growth. Move the sliders until that covariance changes sign and watch the ordering reverse.

The factor-reversal test: only the Fisher row has P×Q equal to the nominal value ratio exactly. That is why it is called the ideal index, and why chained national accounts use it.
Year 0 — the base
Year 1 — the shock
Gross price change of each good. Quantities respond with elasticity ε below.
ε > 0 is ordinary substitution. ε < 0 forces quantities to move with prices — the covariance turns positive and the ordering flips.
Extra quantity growth applied to both goods — real growth that has nothing to do with relative prices. It shifts every index equally and leaves the gap alone.
What the algebra says

With base-period value shares si0, the two quantity indices are QL = Σ s q̂ and QP = Σ s p̂q̂ ⁄ Σ s p̂, so their difference is Covs(p̂,q̂) ⁄ Es[p̂]. Demand curves slope down, so that covariance is normally negative and the early base gives the larger growth number. Nothing else is going on.
