The Trip to the Bank

Money pays no interest, so holding it is a pure loss — unless converting bonds into cash costs something. That one friction produces a square root, and the square root produces an income elasticity of one half: a household whose income doubles does not double its cash, it goes to the bank more often.

The household's problem
The opportunity cost of holding cash. It is the nominal rate — inflation erodes money and bonds equally, so what matters is the interest forgone.
The cost of one trip. Cash machines, debit cards and phone banking all cut it — which shifts money demand down with no change in income or interest rates, and is why money-demand equations fitted in the 1970s broke down in the 1980s.
Why the elasticity is one half, and why it matters

Take logs of M⁄P = √(FY⁄2i): every exponent is ±½. The Cambridge alternative, M⁄P = kY, has an income elasticity of one. The difference is not a parameter quibble — one says cash management has scale economies, the other says it does not. A central bank that believes ½ when the truth is 1 misses its inflation target by exactly (ε̂ − ε)·gY.
