# Narration — Aula 1: Measuring the Economy

## BeatOpen - 85 s

How much richer is the United States than Brazil?

Here is the answer, for twenty twenty-three, from the World Bank. Eighty-two thousand dollars
per person, against ten thousand. The United States is eight times richer.

[pause]

Here is the answer again. Same year, same two countries, same institution. Eighty-two thousand
against twenty-one thousand. Not eight times. Three point nine.

[pause]

And here is a third answer, which we will compute ourselves in the last act of this video, once
we know how to price the fact that Brazilians live shorter lives and face a far wider spread of
incomes. That one comes back to roughly eight — for completely different reasons than the first.

Same year. Same two economies. Three numbers, and not one of them is a mistake.

The exchange rate says eight. Correcting for prices halves it to four. And then correcting for
who actually lives inside the average puts it straight back to eight.

[pause]

Every one of those numbers is the output of a construction, and every construction is a choice
somebody made. This video is about which choices, and about which direction each one bends the
answer. By the end, you will be able to look at any macroeconomic number and ask the only
question that matters: what was built, and which way does it lean?

## BeatFirmIdentity - 140 s

Start with one firm, in one year, and write down where its money goes.

Call its sales revenue R. Out of that revenue it pays for the things it bought from other firms
to make its product — the steel, the electricity, the fertiliser. Call that M, for intermediate
inputs. It pays wages, the wage bill W times L. It pays interest and rent. Some of its machinery
wears out over the year; that is depreciation, D. It hands indirect taxes to the government, net
of subsidies, call that T. And whatever is left over after all of that is profit, which we call
Pi.

Now, that is not an assumption. Profit is *defined* as what is left over. So revenue equals
intermediate purchases, plus wages, plus interest and rent, plus depreciation, plus taxes, plus
profit. It has to. There is nowhere else for a dollar to go.

[pause]

Watch what happens when we move one term. Subtract intermediate purchases from both sides.

On the left we now have revenue minus the cost of bought-in inputs. That object has a name:
**value added**. It is what this firm added to what it was handed.

On the right we have wages, interest and rent, depreciation, taxes, profit. Every one of those
is a payment to somebody — to a worker, to a lender, to a landlord, to the government, to an
owner.

[pause]

So read the line again. Value added, on the left, is production. Payments to people, on the
right, is income. They are equal — and not because two government agencies measured carefully
and happened to agree. They are the same sum, regrouped.

And now you can answer the question that puzzles everybody the first time they see the income
column. Why does depreciation sit on the income side, when it is income accruing to nobody?
Because gross domestic product is *gross*. The production measure never subtracted the wear on
the machines, so the income measure has to add it back, or the two sides stop matching. Subtract
it from both and you get net domestic product instead.

## BeatTelescope - 110 s

Let us try the obvious thing, and watch it fail.

A fertiliser plant makes fertiliser and sells it to a farmer for eighty cents. The farmer uses it
to grow lettuce, and sells the lettuce for one dollar. How much did this little economy produce?

The obvious answer is: add up what everybody sold. Eighty cents, plus one dollar. One dollar
eighty.

[pause]

But look at what is on the table at the end of the day. A head of lettuce. Worth one dollar.
Nobody can eat the fertiliser — it is *inside* the lettuce. We counted it twice. Once as itself,
and once again as part of the thing it became.

Now do it with value added instead. The fertiliser plant bought nothing, so its value added is
the whole eighty cents. The farmer sold for a dollar and bought eighty cents of fertiliser, so
his value added is twenty cents. Eighty plus twenty. One dollar — the value of the only thing
that actually left the production system.

[pause]

And this is not a lucky example. Take any chain of production, however long, where each firm's
inputs are the previous firm's output. Write down the sum of value added along the chain. Each
firm's revenue enters once with a plus sign, and then again with a minus sign, as the next firm's
purchase. Everything in the middle cancels. The sum telescopes, and what survives is the revenue
of the last stage — the value of final output.

That is the theorem. "Avoid double counting" is not a rule of thumb somebody imposed on the
accounts. It is what a telescoping sum does.

## BeatExpenditure - 140 s

We have two of the three approaches. Production equals income, because they are the same sum
regrouped. Now the third.

Every unit of final output that gets produced is bought by somebody, and there are only four
somebodies available. Households, which gives us consumption, C. Firms accumulating capital,
which gives investment, I. The government, G. And foreigners, which gives exports, X.

So output is consumption, plus investment, plus government purchases, plus exports. And then
there is a fifth term. Minus imports.

[pause]

Be careful with that minus sign, because it invites a wrong reading — that imports somehow reduce
a country's output. They do not. It is there for a bookkeeping reason. When we measure
consumption, we measure what households *paid*, and some of what they paid for was made abroad.
The same is true of investment, of government purchases, and even of exports, which often contain
imported components. So the first four terms already contain foreign-made content that was never
produced here. Subtracting imports removes exactly that content, and nothing else.

[pause]

Kurlat's Example one point six, on page nineteen, is the one students get wrong, so let us run it.
A manufacturer imports ten dollars of components. It puts half of them into a car, and sells the
car for twenty. The other five dollars of components sit in the warehouse. Meanwhile a gardener
grows two dollars of lettuce and exports it.

Production side. The car's value added is twenty minus five, so fifteen. Plus two for the lettuce.
Seventeen.

Expenditure side. Consumption is the twenty-dollar car. Exports are two. Imports are ten. And
investment is five — the components sitting in the warehouse, which are an **inventory
investment**.

Twenty, plus five, plus two, minus ten. Seventeen. The two sides agree, and they agree only
because that warehouse entry is there.

[pause]

One trap worth burning in. Next year, when those components are finally used and sold, the sale
adds to consumption and the inventory change subtracts the same amount. Net effect on next year's
output: zero. Gross domestic product records the year a thing was *produced*, not the year it was
sold.

## BeatConventions - 165 s

Everything so far has been a theorem. Now come four things that are not theorems. They are
conventions — decisions somebody made, each defensible, none of them forced. And almost every
accounting question you will ever be asked is a question about one of these four.

[pause]

One. **Transfers are not government purchases.** The government pays a pension of twenty thousand
dollars. How much does that add to output? Nothing at all. No entry changes, in any of the three
columns. Purchasing power moved from one pocket to another; nothing was produced. Only government
spending that actually *buys* a good or a service enters. Hold on to that, because in the ninth
session the lump-sum transfer in our policy model does exactly this — it moves resources without
moving output.

Two. **Government output is valued at what it cost.** A kindergarten teacher paid eighty-five
thousand, and a free public concert that cost eighty-five thousand to put on, contribute the same
amount — even if the class is full and four people came to the concert. There is no market price
for either, so the accounts use what went in as the value of what came out. And that has a
consequence worth saying out loud: measured government productivity growth is zero, by
construction. Not as a finding. As an artefact of the convention.

Three. **Durable goods are consumed when you buy them. Houses are not.** A television bought for
five hundred dollars is five hundred dollars of consumption this year and nothing ever again, even
though you watch it for a decade. A house, by contrast, is investment when it is built, and then
every year afterwards it produces a flow of housing services, valued at the rent an equivalent
house would fetch — including when you own it and pay rent to nobody. That imputation is nearly
eight per cent of American output. The inconsistency is deliberate: housing is huge and
long-lived, and without the imputation, measured output would *fall* every time a renter bought
their own home.

Four. **Non-market production is excluded.** Two neighbours who pay each other twenty-five dollars
to mow each other's lawns generate fifty dollars of output. The same two people mowing their own
lawns generate nothing. This is the largest single conceptual gap between this number and anything
you would want to call welfare — and in the last act of this video, we are going to put a price
on it.

## BeatGNP - 110 s

One last distinction in the accounting, and it is the one our international comparisons will need.

Gross domestic product is defined by **territory**. Everything produced inside the borders,
whoever owns the factory. Gross national product — nowadays called gross national income — is
defined by **ownership**. Everything earned by the country's residents, wherever they earned it.

So to get from one to the other: add what residents earn abroad, and subtract what foreigners earn
here.

[pause]

Usually that gap is small. Sometimes it is enormous. Ireland is the standard case. A great deal of
Irish production happens inside foreign-owned firms, so the profit is produced in Ireland and
accrues to shareholders somewhere else. Irish national income runs something like fifteen to
twenty per cent below Irish domestic product. If you want to know how Irish *residents* are doing,
the domestic number will mislead you — which is exactly why the Human Development Index, in the
last act, uses national income and not domestic product.

[pause]

And finally, a discipline that will save you hours later in this course. Keep stocks and flows
apart. Capital is a stock, a level at a moment in time. Investment is a flow, a rate over a
period. Wealth is a stock; saving is a flow. Government debt is a stock; the deficit is a flow.

They are joined by one line. Capital next period equals capital this period, less what wore out,
plus what was invested.

Look at that line, because it is the entire engine of the next session. And notice that the
depreciation in it is the same D that appeared, three beats ago, on the income side of a single
firm's accounts.
## BeatExpandia - 125 s

Now the second act, and a genuinely harder problem. We know how to add up output in one year.
How do we compare two years?

Here is Kurlat's country of Expandia, on page twenty-three. It produces exactly two things.

In the first year: ten tons of wheat, at fifty dollars a ton. And one computer, at a thousand
dollars. Nominal output, one thousand five hundred dollars.

In the second year: eleven tons of wheat, and wheat has gone *up* to sixty dollars. And two
computers, and computers have come *down* to six hundred. Nominal output, one thousand eight
hundred and sixty.

[pause]

Agriculture grew ten per cent. Manufacturing grew one hundred per cent. Prices went one way in
one sector and the other way in the other. So what was aggregate growth?

The standard answer is: hold prices fixed, so that only quantities move. Fine. Which prices?

[pause]

Try first-year prices. Value the second year's quantities — eleven tons and two computers — at
fifty dollars and a thousand dollars. Five hundred and fifty, plus two thousand. Two thousand
five hundred and fifty, against a first-year total of one thousand five hundred. That is growth
of seventy per cent.

Now try second-year prices. Value the *first* year's quantities at sixty dollars and six
hundred. Six hundred plus six hundred, twelve hundred. And the second year is already
eighteen sixty at those prices. Eighteen sixty over twelve hundred. Growth of fifty-five per
cent.

[pause]

Seventy per cent, or fifty-five per cent. Same country. Same data. Same arithmetic. A gap of
fifteen percentage points, decided entirely by which year you chose to call the base.

That is not a rounding problem you can hope goes away in real data. It is the problem. So the
question becomes: can we say, before computing anything, which of the two will be larger, and
why?

## BeatCovariance - 185 s

We can. And the proof is worth every line, because it tells you the direction of the error in
every price index you will ever read.

Write the two answers as growth *factors*. The first one, at base-year prices, is base-year
prices times new quantities, over base-year prices times old quantities. Economists call that
a **Laspeyres quantity index**. The second, at final-year prices, is final prices times new
quantities over final prices times old quantities. That is a **Paasche quantity index**.

Now some notation that makes them comparable. Let s-i be the share of good i in base-year
spending. Let q-hat-i be the growth *factor* of good i's quantity — new divided by old. And
p-hat-i the growth factor of its price.

[pause]

Divide through, and the Laspeyres index becomes something very simple: the share-weighted
average of the quantity growth factors. Sum of s-i times q-hat-i. Nothing else.

Do the same to the Paasche index. Numerator: sum of s-i times p-hat-i times q-hat-i.
Denominator: sum of s-i times p-hat-i.

Look at those two carefully. The Laspeyres index is a plain weighted average of quantity
growth. The Paasche index is the *same* average — but reweighted, by how much each good's price
grew.

[pause]

And once you see it as a reweighting, you know exactly what the difference has to be. Write
E for the share-weighted average. The Paasche index minus the Laspeyres index equals the average
of p-hat times q-hat, minus the average of p-hat times the average of q-hat, all divided by the
average of p-hat.

That numerator is a **covariance**. The covariance, across goods, between price growth and
quantity growth.

[pause]

The denominator is an average of positive numbers, so it is positive. Which means the sign of
the whole difference is the sign of that covariance, and we get a boxed result: Paasche is below
Laspeyres if and only if price growth and quantity growth covary negatively across goods.

So: when is that covariance negative? When the goods whose prices fell are the goods whose
quantities rose. Which is what a downward-sloping demand curve *says*. It is not an empirical
accident. It is the central claim of price theory.

[pause]

Check it on Expandia. Wheat is a third of base-year spending, computers two thirds. Price factors
are one point two and nought point six; quantity factors one point one and two. The average price
factor is nought point eight, the average quantity factor one point seven, and the average of the
products is one point two four. So the covariance is one point two four minus one point three six.
Minus nought point one two. Divide by nought point eight: minus nought point one five.

Fifteen percentage points, with the right sign, predicted before we computed either index.

## BeatFisher - 175 s

So neither base year is defensible. One of them systematically overstates, the other
systematically understates, and we have just proved which is which. What do we do?

Kurlat's answer, on page twenty-four, is to stop choosing. Take the geometric mean of the two
growth factors — multiply them together and take the square root — and then chain that forward,
year by year. That object is the **Fisher ideal index**.

For Expandia: one point seven times one point five five, square root, minus one. Sixty-two point
three per cent. Between fifty-five and seventy, as it must be.

[pause]

Now, why the *geometric* mean? Why not just average the two? Three reasons, and none of them
works for the arithmetic mean.

First, it is bracketed. A geometric mean always lies between the two numbers, so the chained
growth rate can never escape the range the two base years give you.

Second, time reversal. Run the index backwards, from the second year to the first, and the
Fisher index inverts exactly — you get precisely the reciprocal. Laspeyres and Paasche each fail
this: reverse them, and each one turns into the other.

[pause]

Third, and this is the property that earns the word "ideal". The Fisher *price* index, times the
Fisher *quantity* index, equals the ratio of nominal values, exactly.

Watch why, because it is two lines. Take the Laspeyres price index times the Paasche quantity
index. Write them out. The denominator of the first and the numerator of the second are the same
sum — new prices times old quantities — and they cancel. What is left is new prices times new
quantities, over old prices times old quantities. The nominal value ratio.

Now do it the other way round. Paasche price times Laspeyres quantity. This time the mismatched
sum is old prices times new quantities, and it cancels too. Same answer.

So the product of the two Fisher indices is the square root of the value ratio times itself.
Which is the value ratio.

[pause]

That identity is why chained accounts stay internally consistent: the deflator implied by a Fisher
quantity index is itself a Fisher price index. And there is a price for it, which you should know
before you meet it in a data release. Chained real output is a product of one-period factors, so
it depends on the whole path, not on one base year — and chained real components no longer add up.
Consumption plus investment plus government plus net exports equals output in nominal terms, and
only approximately in chained real terms. That is why statistical agencies publish a line called
"residual".

## BeatBias - 160 s

Now run the whole argument again — but on prices instead of quantities. This is where the
theorem starts paying rent.

A Laspeyres *price* index is the share-weighted average of price growth, holding the basket at its
base-year composition. That is what a consumer price index is. A fixed basket, priced repeatedly.

Swap the roles of p-hat and q-hat in the covariance result we proved two beats ago, and out comes
the mirror image: the Paasche price index minus the Laspeyres price index is the covariance
between quantity growth and price growth, divided by average quantity growth. Same covariance.
Same sign. Negative.

[pause]

So we have an ordering, and it is a theorem, not a regularity: Laspeyres inflation exceeds Fisher
inflation exceeds Paasche inflation, whenever demand curves slope down.

That ordering has a name. **Substitution bias**. And now you can say exactly what causes it. A
fixed basket never lets the consumer walk away from what became expensive. It keeps buying the
old quantities of the thing whose price went up, so it charges the consumer for a basket they
stopped buying.

[pause]

How big? Suppose the consumer substitutes with an elasticity epsilon, and let the *variance* of
log price changes across goods be v. Then to second order, the gap between the Laspeyres index
and the Fisher index is about one half, times epsilon, times v.

Read that formula, because it says something you would not guess. The bias does not grow with the
*average* inflation rate. It grows with the **dispersion** of price changes, and with the
willingness to substitute. A year of high but uniform inflation carries almost no substitution
bias. A year of moderate inflation in which some sectors spike and others fall carries a lot.

And when epsilon is zero — a consumer who cannot substitute at all, fixed proportions — the
covariance vanishes and the bias is exactly zero. The bias is not a measurement failure. It is
the shadow of substitution.

[pause]

The empirical anchor: the Boskin Commission, in nineteen ninety-six, put the total bias in the
American consumer price index at about one point one percentage points a year. Roughly nought
point four of that is the substitution bias we just proved. The rest is quality change and new
goods — which our theorem does *not* cover, because those are failures of the basket to describe
the goods, not failures of the weights.

## BeatDeflatorCPI - 135 s

Which brings us to the two price indices you will actually meet, and to the one question about
them that gets asked in every exam.

The GDP deflator is nominal output divided by real output. Its basket is everything the country
**produces**. It uses current quantities, so it is a Paasche index. It excludes imports — they are
not domestic output — and it includes capital goods. And it gets revised as data arrive.

The consumer price index prices a **fixed** basket of what households **buy**. Base-year
quantities, so it is Laspeyres. It includes imports, because consumers buy them, and it excludes
capital goods. And it is essentially never revised.

[pause]

Now the question. An oil-importing country sees the crude price spike. What happens to each index?

The consumer price index rises. Households buy petrol, petrol is in the basket, the basket got
more expensive.

The deflator *falls*. Imported oil is not domestic production, and in the expenditure identity
imports enter with a minus sign. So a more expensive import, holding everything else fixed, pushes
the deflator **down**.

Two price indices for the same economy in the same quarter, moving in opposite directions, and
neither is broken. They are answering different questions.

[pause]

And Brazil is the mirror case, which Kurlat mentions in one clause on page twenty-four. Brazil is
a commodity *exporter*. When commodity prices boom, the price of what Brazil **produces** rises
faster than the price of what Brazilians **buy**.

Here are the two series. In twenty twenty-one, Brazilian consumer price inflation was eight point
three per cent. The deflator was thirteen. In twenty ten: five per cent against eight point four.
The wedge is not noise and it does not average out — it opens in every commodity boom, and it
opens in the direction the theory says it must for an exporter.

[pause]

So when somebody tells you Brazilian inflation was a certain number last year, the honest question
is: the inflation of what you bought, or the inflation of what we sold?

## BeatLogs - 145 s

Third act. Before we can compare countries over time, we need to be able to do arithmetic with
growth rates — and about half the mistakes in the next two sessions are arithmetic rather than
economic.

There are three ways to say the same thing. Output this year equals output last year times one
plus g. The ratio of the two is one plus g. Or: the difference of the logarithms is the logarithm
of one plus g.

That last object — the change in the log — is the **log growth rate**. Call it g-tilde. And the
relation between the two is exact: g-tilde is the log of one plus g.

[pause]

Everybody then says "and for small g these are the same". They are not the same. They are close,
and the point of this beat is to know how close.

Expand the logarithm as a series: g, minus g squared over two, plus g cubed over three, and so on.
So, to a very good approximation, the gap between the ordinary growth rate and the log growth rate
is **g squared over two**.

That single term tells you everything about when you may be careless.

[pause]

At one per cent growth, the gap is five thousandths of a percentage point. Nobody will ever care.

At five per cent, the log rate is four point eight eight instead of five. Two and a half per cent
relative error. Still fine.

At fifty per cent growth, the log rate is forty point five. You are out by nineteen per cent of the
answer.

And at two hundred per cent — which is a perfectly ordinary monthly number in a hyperinflation —
the log rate is one hundred and ten. The approximation has lost nearly half the answer.

[pause]

So: logs are excellent for annual growth in a developed economy, tolerable for a decade of Chinese
growth, and useless for hyperinflation. That is precisely why, in the seventh session, we insist on
the *exact* Fisher equation when inflation is large — and why Kurlat's Cagan money demand, exercise
eleven point six, is written in logs from the very first line.

## BeatProducts - 130 s

Now the three rules that get used, without comment, for the rest of the course.

Take any two positive variables, X and Z. In continuous time, differentiate the log of their
product. The log of a product is the sum of the logs, so the growth rate of the product is the
**sum** of the growth rates. Exactly. No approximation.

The same move gives you the other two. The growth rate of a ratio is the **difference** of the
growth rates. And the growth rate of X raised to the power a is a **times** the growth rate of X.

[pause]

In discrete time, they are only approximate, and it is worth seeing exactly what you are throwing
away. Multiply one plus g-X by one plus g-Z. You get one, plus g-X, plus g-Z, **plus the product
of the two**. That last term is the cross term, and it is what discrete time costs you.

Two applications you will use constantly.

Per capita growth. If output grows at three per cent and population at one, output per head does
**not** grow at exactly two. It grows at one point nine eight. The cross term is three hundredths
of a percentage point. You will round it away — but you should round it away knowingly.

[pause]

And growth accounting. Take output equal to productivity A, times capital to the power alpha,
times labour to the power one minus alpha. Apply all three rules at once, and the growth rate of
output is the growth rate of A, plus alpha times the growth rate of capital, plus one minus alpha
times the growth rate of labour.

That line is an identity — it assumes nothing about behaviour. In the third session we will turn
it around, solve it for the growth of A, and call what is left the Solow residual. Everything that
session says about total factor productivity rests on the three lines we just derived.

## BeatCAGR - 160 s

Here is a plausible move that is simply wrong, and it is wrong in a fixed direction.

An investment goes up fifty per cent in the first year, and down fifty per cent in the second.
What was the average annual growth rate?

Plus fifty, minus fifty, average zero. Obviously.

[pause]

Except: start with a hundred. Up fifty per cent, one hundred and fifty. Down fifty per cent,
seventy-five. You have lost a quarter of your money, and the "average growth rate" was zero.

The problem is that growth **compounds**. It multiplies; it does not add. So the only mean that can
be correct is the one that multiplies: the geometric mean of the gross factors. One point five
times nought point five is nought point seven five, square root, minus one. Minus thirteen point
four per cent a year. That is the compound annual growth rate, and it is the number that
reproduces where you actually ended up.

[pause]

And the direction of the error is not random. The arithmetic mean of a set of numbers is always at
least their geometric mean — that is the arithmetic-mean-geometric-mean inequality — with equality
only if every number is identical.

So the arithmetic mean of growth rates **always** overstates realised growth, and the gap widens
with volatility. Hold on to that convexity, because in the last act of this video it comes back,
wearing a completely different costume, as the price of inequality.

[pause]

One more piece of arithmetic, and it is the one that makes growth rates mean something. How long
does it take to double? Set one plus g to the power T equal to two, and solve. T is the log of two,
divided by the log of one plus g. The log of two is nought point six nine three, and for small g
the bottom is about g. So doubling time is roughly seventy, divided by the growth rate in per cent.

That is the **rule of seventy**, and it converts an abstract percentage into the only currency that
matters: time. Two per cent a year, and income per head doubles in thirty-five years — once in a
working life. Seven per cent, and it doubles in ten years. Three and a half doublings in the same
working life: a factor of eleven. That comparison is the entire motivation for the next session.

## BeatLogScale - 125 s

Last piece of the arithmetic, and it is about reading, not computing.

Plot the logarithm of output against time. Then the slope of what you drew is, by definition, the
growth rate. Not the change in output. The **rate** of change.

Three readings follow, and the third one is a trap that has ended careers in policy commentary.

First: a straight line on a log plot is exponential growth. Constant *proportional* growth, not
constant absolute growth.

[pause]

Second: two parallel lines are two economies growing at the *same rate*. The vertical distance
between them is a constant difference in logs — which is a constant *ratio* of incomes. Parallel
means the ratio never closes. Parallel means **no convergence**, however narrow the gap looks on
the page.

Third: a line that bends flat is growth slowing down, even while the level is still rising.

[pause]

Here is Brazil and the United States, income per head at constant purchasing power, since
nineteen ninety. Two lines, and to the eye they run more or less parallel. Which is the result.

Brazil compounded at one point two six per cent a year over that stretch; the United States at one
point five eight. Brazilian income was twenty-eight and a half per cent of American income in
nineteen ninety. In twenty twenty-three it was twenty-five point seven per cent.

Thirty-three years, and the ratio went slightly the wrong way.

[pause]

Look at that picture, and then remember what it looks like on a linear scale, where the American
line runs away and the Brazilian one looks almost flat. Same data. On the linear plot you see the
gap; on the log plot you see the *rate*, which is the thing the models in the next two sessions
are about. Read the wrong axis and you will mistake a permanent ratio for a country catching up.
## BeatPPPproblem - 110 s

Fourth act, and we are finally in a position to answer the question the video opened with.

Kurlat's Example one point fourteen, page twenty-five. American output is twenty point five
trillion dollars, shared among three hundred and twenty-seven million people. Mexican output is
twenty-three point five trillion pesos, among a hundred and twenty-seven million people.

Per person: sixty-two thousand seven hundred dollars, and a hundred and eighty-five thousand
pesos.

Those two numbers cannot be compared, because they are not in the same units. So do the obvious
thing. The exchange rate is nineteen pesos to the dollar. Divide. Nine thousand seven hundred
dollars.

Conclusion: Americans are six and a half times richer than Mexicans.

[pause]

Now here is the objection, and it is fatal.

A dollar, converted into pesos and spent in Mexico, buys more than that dollar buys in the United
States. A haircut, a bus ride, a meal, a month's rent — all cheaper. So when we see a low number
for Mexico, we genuinely cannot tell whether Mexicans produce less, or whether what they produce
simply costs less.

The market exchange rate cannot separate those two, because it is not built to. It is the price at
which currencies trade — set by trade in goods that *can* cross borders, and by financial flows.
Nobody exchanges currency to arbitrage the price of a Mexican haircut, because you cannot ship a
haircut.

[pause]

So the market rate answers "how many pesos does a dollar buy", and we asked "how much stuff does a
Mexican get". Those are different questions, and the gap between them is exactly what this act is
about.

## BeatPPPconstruct - 150 s

The repair is the same move we made in the second act, and if you see that, this whole section
becomes one idea instead of two.

In the second act, two years were not comparable because prices had moved, so we held prices fixed
and let quantities do the talking.

Now two countries are not comparable because prices differ, so we do exactly the same thing. Take
the Mexican basket — the actual quantities Mexicans consumed — and value them at **American**
prices. Good by good.

That is purchasing power parity. And notice what it is, formally: it is the base-year index of the
second act, with "base year" replaced by "base country". Laspeyres in space.

[pause]

Which means it inherits everything we proved. Value the basket at Mexican prices instead and you
get a different answer, for exactly the covariance reason from before. And comparing three
countries, you might get an American-Mexican ratio that depends on whether you route the comparison
through Canada. That is why the Penn World Table uses multilateral methods — their job is to force
the comparisons to be consistent with each other.

Do it for Mexico, and output per head comes out around eighteen thousand dollars instead of nine
thousand seven hundred. Nearly double. So the true ratio is about three and a half, not six and a
half. **Almost half of the measured gap between the United States and Mexico was never an output
gap at all. It was a price-level gap.**

[pause]

Two definitions fall out. The **purchasing power parity exchange rate** is the rate that would have
made the two conversions agree. And the country's **price level**, relative to the United States,
is the ratio of the purchasing power rate to the market rate. For Mexico that is nought point five
four: Mexican prices are a little over half American prices.

And the Big Mac index, which you have seen in the newspapers, is this same construction with a
basket of exactly one good. Its virtue is that the good is standardised worldwide. Its defect,
which Kurlat puts in a footnote, is that a Big Mac's price is mostly rent, wages and local
services. But hold on to that defect — the next beat turns it into the whole mechanism.

## BeatBalassaSetup - 130 s

Because the real question is not *that* poor countries are cheap. It is why they are **systematically**
cheap. The price level does not scatter randomly around the world. It rises, almost monotonically,
with income per head. That regularity needs a model, and the model is three lines long.

Two sectors. Tradables — things that can be shipped, so manufactures and commodities. And
non-tradables — haircuts, bus rides, construction, restaurant meals. One factor, labour, and it can
move between the two sectors. Output in each sector is productivity times labour: A-T times L-T,
and A-N times L-N.

Two assumptions.

First, the law of one price in tradables. A shipped good costs the same everywhere once you convert
at the market rate — otherwise somebody ships it. Normalise that world tradable price to one.

Second, labour is mobile between sectors *within* a country. Competitive firms pay the value of the
marginal product, and workers move until the wage is the same in both sectors. So the wage equals
the tradable price times tradable productivity, and equals the non-tradable price times non-tradable
productivity.

[pause]

Now divide one of those by the other, and watch what survives.

The wage cancels. And what is left is: the relative price of non-tradables equals A-T over A-N. The
ratio of the two productivities.

Stop and look at what is *not* in that expression. No preferences. No demand. No capital. No income.
The relative price of a haircut is a pure technology ratio.

[pause]

And once you see it, the mechanism is almost visceral. A country that is extremely productive at
making tradable goods must pay high wages in its tradable sector. Barbers do not become more
productive — a haircut takes what it takes — but they have to be paid something close to the factory
wage, or they would go and work in the factory. So haircuts are expensive in rich countries, and
they are expensive **because** the factories are good.

[pause]

## BeatBalassaPrediction - 120 s

One more line to get from a relative price to a price level. Let consumption be Cobb-Douglas, with a
share gamma spent on non-tradables. Then the consumer price index is the geometric average of the two
prices — and since the tradable price is one everywhere, the whole price level is the productivity
ratio raised to the power gamma.

Take logs, and you get the prediction. A country's price level, relative to the United States, is
gamma times the gap in tradable productivity, *minus* the gap in non-tradable productivity.

Poor countries are far behind in manufacturing and only a little behind in haircuts. So that bracket
is negative, the price level is below one, and the market exchange rate understates their real
output — and it understates it *more* the poorer the country is. Which is exactly the pattern in the
data.

[pause]

Two things the derivation shows that the slogan never does.

It is about the **ratio** of productivities, not about being poor. A country that is uniformly
unproductive in everything has the same ratio as a rich one, and shows no price-level effect at all,
however poor it is. Poverty predicts nothing; asymmetric productivity predicts everything.

And gamma scales it. A small open economy that consumes mostly imported tradables shows a weak
effect; a large continental economy full of local services shows a strong one.

And a corollary you will meet in the newspapers: a country whose tradable productivity is catching
up fast sees its price level *rise* towards the American level. So its output measured at market
rates grows faster than its real output — part of that measured growth is just the price level
converging. That is a standard reason Chinese growth at market rates in the two thousands overstated
what was really happening.

## BeatDenominator - 100 s

One more comparison trap, and it is the one that is easiest to fall into because it hides in the
denominator.

Output per **person** is not output per **worker**, and neither is output per **hour**. Three
different measures, three different questions.

And they are linked by an identity worth memorising: output per person equals output per hour, times
hours per worker, times the employment rate. Productivity, times how long each worker works, times
how many people work at all.

[pause]

Now use it. France against the United States. French output per **hour** is close to the American
level — within a few per cent. French output per **person** is roughly twenty-five to thirty per cent
lower.

The identity tells you immediately where the entire gap is. Not in the first term. It is in the other
two. French workers work fewer hours a year, and a smaller fraction of the French population is
employed.

[pause]

So what would you like to conclude? That French productivity is poor? The first term says no. That
France is poorer? In consumption terms, yes, a little. That the French are worse off? That depends
entirely on whether those missing hours are unemployment or holiday.

And that question — is a short working year a loss or a purchase — is exactly what the fifth session
models, and exactly what the last act of this video is going to put a price on. Get the denominator
wrong and you will turn a statement about leisure into a false statement about productivity.

## BeatHDI - 170 s

Fifth act. Everything we have built measures production. Nobody actually wants production. So how
far off is it as a measure of what we do want?

The famous first answer is the Human Development Index, and its method is: pick the things that seem
to matter, and average them.

Three components. Life expectancy, rescaled onto zero-to-one between twenty years and eighty-five.
Education, which is itself the average of two schooling measures, each divided by its own cap.
And income — national income per head, in logs, rescaled between a hundred dollars and seventy-five
thousand.

Then take the geometric mean of the three: multiply them and take the cube root.

[pause]

Three design choices there, and it is worth being precise about which of them has an argument behind
it.

The logarithm on income, and only on income, is the one with real content. It imposes diminishing
returns: a thousand dollars more matters much more at two thousand than at sixty thousand. The other
two components are linear — which asserts that a year of life expectancy is worth the same at
forty-five as at eighty. That is not obviously right. It is just simpler.

[pause]

The geometric mean, though, is doing genuine work. Take logs of the index: the log of the index is
the average of the logs of the three components. Which makes them **complements**. The marginal value
of more income is higher when health is already high. And if any single component goes to zero, the
whole index goes to zero — a country cannot buy its way to a good score while its people die young.
An arithmetic mean would make the three perfect substitutes and allow exactly that. The United
Nations switched from arithmetic to geometric in twenty ten for this reason.

And the caps are simply conventions. Some countries' national income exceeds the seventy-five
thousand ceiling, so their income sub-index comes out above one. Kurlat records that in a footnote
without apology, because it is not a mistake — it is what a convention looks like when reality walks
past it.

[pause]

And now the punchline, which is on page thirty-two. The correlation between this index and plain
output per head, across countries, is nought point nine four.

The index built to correct output mostly reproduces output. That is not a failure — it tells us
something real, that income, health and schooling move together across countries. But it does mean
that a committee's list adds almost no information. Which invites the obvious question: could we
build a welfare measure out of *theory* instead of a list?

## BeatRawls - 165 s

Jones and Klenow did exactly that, and Kurlat reproduces it in section two point two. It is the most
graduate-level thing in this lecture, and it is worth every minute.

Here is the thought experiment. Take a person — call him Rawls — and tell him he will live one year
in a country, but he does not get to know *who* in that country he will be. Rich or poor, employed or
not, long-lived or not. He chooses from behind a **veil of ignorance**.

Now offer him two options. Option one: a year in the country we want to measure. Option two: a year
in the United States, with everybody's consumption multiplied by a factor lambda.

**Lambda is the number that makes him indifferent.** That is the welfare measure. Not an index. An
equivalent variation — the compensation that makes one country as good as another.

[pause]

To compute it we need his preferences, and Kurlat's equation two point two point one gives them.
Utility is the expected value of three things added together: a constant u-bar, the flow value of
simply being alive; consumption to the power one minus sigma, over one minus sigma, which is the
standard constant-relative-risk-aversion form; minus theta times the square of time spent working.
And the whole bracket is multiplied by an indicator that is one if he is alive and zero if he is not.

Four modelling commitments, and each one is doing work.

[pause]

First, it is **consumption**, not output. Investment and exports do not enter Rawls's year — he
cannot eat a machine tool. So the object is consumption, public and private, and a country that
invests heavily scores below its output rank in this one-year experiment.

Second, that alive-indicator multiplies **everything**. The dead get zero, not a low number. So life
expectancy enters as a probability weight on the entire bracket — which means u-bar, the value of
being alive at zero consumption, has to be pinned down, because it decides how much mortality
matters.

Third, the squared term prices work as a convex disutility. Which means non-market time is valued —
this is the formal answer to the two-neighbours-mowing-lawns problem from the first act.

And fourth, sigma does double duty. It is risk aversion behind the veil. But because a spread-out
consumption distribution simply *is* risk when you do not know who you will be, the same parameter
becomes aversion to **inequality**. That equivalence is the cleverest move in the paper.

[pause]

## BeatJensen - 55 s

The machinery underneath is Jensen's inequality. Draw a concave utility function, mark two
consumption levels, and connect them with a chord. The chord lies below the curve. The height of the
curve at the average is utility of the mean; the midpoint of the chord is the mean of the utility.
The first is higher. So a certain average is preferred to the gamble, which is what risk aversion
means — and the gap between them is exactly what we are about to compute.

One warning from Kurlat, worth quoting exactly, on page thirty-five: *"It's wrong to say people
dislike risk because their utility function is concave. Instead, one should say: in economic models,
we describe people's preferences with concave utility functions to capture the fact that people
dislike risk. Do not put the mathematical cart before the conceptual horse."*

## BeatLambdaDerive - 140 s

Now solve it. Take sigma equal to one, which is Jones and Klenow's own choice — and notice that it
is the *conservative* one, because it is at the low end of estimated risk aversion and therefore
gives us the **smallest** inequality penalty we could honestly claim.

With sigma equal to one, the consumption term becomes the logarithm of consumption. And let e be the
fraction of a full life that a person in this country actually lives — life expectancy over a hundred.

So a country's welfare is e, times the bracket: u-bar, plus expected log consumption, minus the
disutility of work.

Set the American version, with consumption scaled by lambda, equal to the other country's. Scaling
consumption by lambda inside a logarithm just adds log lambda. So log lambda is sitting alone on one
side, and everything else is data.

[pause]

One more ingredient. Within any country, consumption is spread out, and it is well described as
lognormal — so let s squared be the variance of log consumption. Then there is a standard identity:
the expected value of the log is the log of the mean, **minus one half s squared**.

That is Jensen's inequality made quantitative. And it is the same convexity that made the arithmetic
mean of growth rates lie to us in the third act — appearing here for the second time, in a completely
different costume.

[pause]

Substitute, and rearrange, and log lambda falls apart into four additive terms.

Term one: the log of the ratio of average consumption. This is the only term that ordinary output per
head comes close to capturing — and even here it is consumption, not output.

Term two: minus one half the difference in the variances of log consumption. **Inequality, priced at
exactly half the log variance.** With a general sigma it would be sigma over two, so it scales
linearly in risk aversion.

Term three: the leisure difference, convex in hours worked.

Term four: the mortality difference, weighted by the flow value of a year of life — which is u-bar
plus the year's flow utility.

[pause]

## BeatLambdaBrazil - 130 s

Now put Brazilian and American numbers into it. Household consumption per head, at constant
purchasing power: about eleven and a half thousand against fifty and a half thousand. That first term
is minus one point four eight — a consumption ratio of nought point two three.

Inequality: Brazil's Gini is fifty-one point five, the United States' is forty-one point eight. Under
lognormality those map to log standard deviations of nought point nine nine and nought point seven
eight. Half the difference of the squares is minus nought point one nine. So inequality alone costs
Brazil about seventeen per cent of measured consumption.

Life expectancy: seventy-five point eight years against seventy-eight point four. That term depends
on u-bar, which has no market price, so here is the honest way to report it: at u-bar equal to five,
it costs another minus nought point four seven.

Add them up. Lambda is about nought point one two. And the leisure term is missing, because we have
no verified hours series for both countries — we are saying so rather than filling the gap.

[pause]

Look at what happened. Measured at market exchange rates, the United States was eight times richer.
Purchasing power parity cut that to three point nine. And now, pricing the inequality and the
mortality that the average conceals, we are back to roughly eight.

That is the finding of the paper, in general: Western Europe looks considerably *better* than its
output suggests — longer lives, more leisure, less inequality, often twenty to thirty-five per cent
better. And poor countries look *worse* than their purchasing-power output suggests, because shorter
lives and wider inequality compound the consumption gap.

And the correlation of this measure with output per head, across countries? Nought point nine five.

Almost exactly what the Human Development Index gave us. The same lesson, reached from a utility
function instead of a committee's list — which is why we now believe it.

## BeatClose - 115 s

So here is the whole lecture in one picture.

One country. One year. Five numbers.

At the market exchange rate, Brazilian income per head is ten thousand dollars. **Convert.**

At purchasing power parity, twenty-one thousand. **Reweight** — value the basket at a common set of
prices, and half the apparent gap with the United States disappears, because it was a price-level
gap and not an output gap.

Change the denominator, and you get a different question again — output per hour instead of per
person, productivity instead of living standards. **Re-denominate.**

Move from output to consumption, because nobody eats a machine tool. **Re-base.**

And finally, price the spread and the mortality that the average conceals, and lambda comes out near
nought point one two, which puts the gap back where the exchange rate had it — for entirely different
reasons. **Price the risk.**

[pause]

Not one of those five numbers is wrong. Each one is the answer to a different question, and each step
between them is a construction somebody chose.

That is the skill this session is really teaching. Not how to compute output — you will rarely have
to. The skill is to see a macroeconomic number, name the construction behind it, and say which way it
leans. The early base gives you the larger growth rate. A fixed basket overstates inflation. The
market rate understates poor countries. The arithmetic mean overstates growth. And the average
conceals the inequality underneath it, at a cost of exactly half the log variance.

Five signed statements. Every one of them proved on screen.

[pause]

American one at all — and what would it take to close it?
